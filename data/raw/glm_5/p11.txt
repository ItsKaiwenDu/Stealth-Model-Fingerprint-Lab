**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** University Administration and the Academic Planning Committee
**FROM:** Director of Campus Services
**DATE:** October 26, 2023
**SUBJECT:** Assessment of Extended Library Hours During Final Examinations

**Introduction and Context**

This memo addresses the recent proposal from the Student Government Association to implement twenty-four-hour access to the main campus library during the final examination period. As our university continues to emphasize student retention and academic success, the demand for dedicated, safe study spaces has increased. Historically, our library has closed at midnight, a schedule that aligns with standard operational capacities but increasingly conflicts with the nocturnal study habits of our diverse student body. While the request is intuitively aligned with student support, it presents significant logistical and financial challenges for a small university. This analysis evaluates the potential learning benefits, safety requirements, staffing implications, and equity considerations associated with the proposal. It further examines the concept of displaced costs and outlines a framework for evaluating a pilot program. Based on these factors, this memo recommends a conditional approval of a pilot program, subject to strict utilization thresholds and security protocols.

**Learning Benefits and Academic Demand**

The primary argument for extended hours rests on the correlation between study environment quality and academic performance. While students have access to dormitory common rooms or personal apartments, the library offers a unique "third place" distinct from the distractions of home and the social dynamics of the dormitory. For many students, particularly those with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) or other executive function challenges, the structured environment of a library is essential for focus. Furthermore, the modern academic schedule is increasingly asynchronous. We have observed a rise in non-traditional students, those balancing employment with coursework, who may only be available to study during late evening hours. Denying access to academic resources during these critical peak periods risks institutionalizing a disadvantage for students whose biological clocks or work schedules favor late-night productivity. Therefore, the potential learning benefit is not merely incremental; it represents a necessary accommodation for diverse learning styles and schedules.

**Equity and the Digital Divide**

Beyond the general learning benefits, the equity implications of this proposal are profound and arguably the most compelling reason to extend hours. A significant portion of our student body faces housing insecurity or lives in environments ill-suited for concentrated study. For a student living in a crowded apartment or a dormitory with an active social scene, the library is the only sanctuary available for rigorous academic work. Closing the facility at midnight effectively tells these students that their academic success is conditional on their ability to afford a quiet, private study space. Additionally, we must consider the digital divide. While the university provides laptop loans, reliable high-speed internet and access to specialized printing or software remains tethered to campus facilities. By restricting access to the hours of midnight to 6:00 AM, we inadvertently privilege students who have the means to replicate these resources at home. Keeping the library open all night acts as a mechanism for leveling the playing field, ensuring that academic success is driven by effort and intellect rather than socioeconomic status.

**Staffing and Operational Constraints**

However, the practicalities of twenty-four-hour operations cannot be ignored. As a small university, we lack the deep reservoir of personnel available to larger state institutions. Our library staff is currently stretched thin, and requiring professional librarians to staff graveyard shifts is unsustainable and would likely result in burnout or high turnover. A twenty-four-hour model would necessitate a shift in staffing paradigm, moving away from professional reference staff during the overnight hours toward a model utilizing security personnel and student workers. This creates a gap in service; a student worker can check out reserves and manage noise, but they cannot provide the research assistance a professional librarian offers. We must accept that overnight hours will be "services-reduced," focusing on space and resource access rather than research support. This requires clear communication to students so they do not arrive expecting full reference services at 3:00 AM.

**Safety and Liability Considerations**

The most significant risk factor in this proposal is campus safety. An open library during the quietest hours of the night presents a target for unauthorized access and increases the potential for medical emergencies or interpersonal conflicts. The safety of student workers is paramount; we cannot ethically place a student employee at a front desk alone at 4:00 AM. Therefore, the cost of this initiative is not merely staffing but security. We would be required to station a security officer in the library for the duration of the extended hours. This presence serves a dual purpose: it deters potential bad actors and provides an immediate response mechanism for students experiencing distress, a not-uncommon occurrence during the high-stress exam period. The liability of having students in a building without adequate security coverage is a risk the university cannot absorb. Consequently, the safety protocol costs become the primary financial driver of this initiative.

**Displaced Costs and Opportunity Costs**

In analyzing the budget, we must consider the concept of displaced costs. Often, the argument against extended hours focuses on the direct line-item increase in electricity and wages. However, a sophisticated analysis recognizes that costs are often merely displaced rather than created. If a student cannot study in the library at 2:00 AM, they may study in a dormitory lounge or a twenty-four-hour dining hall. This shifts the utility costs, cleaning requirements, and wear-and-tear to the Housing and Residential Life budget or the Food Services budget. Currently, these departments absorb the cost of overnight usage in facilities not designed for intensive study. By consolidating late-night study in the library, we centralize security and utility costs, potentially realizing efficiencies in cleaning and maintenance across other campus buildings. However, we must also consider the opportunity cost. Funds spent on overnight security and HVAC are funds not available for library collections, database subscriptions, or daytime staffing. We must weigh the marginal benefit of the 12:00 AM to 6:00 AM access against these foregone academic investments.

**Evaluation of a Pilot Program**

Given these variables, a permanent implementation is ill-advised without empirical data. We should instead authorize a pilot program for the upcoming fall semester final exams. The success of this pilot should not be measured solely by headcount, as a low headcount does not necessarily indicate failure if the usage is critical for those specific students. Instead, we should utilize a mixed-methods evaluation. We will install traffic counters at the entrance to quantify volume. More importantly, we will deploy a brief digital survey to students exiting the building during the 6:00 AM shift change, asking them to rate the importance of the access to their exam performance on a Likert scale. We must also track incident reports meticulously. If security incidents spike, or if the building is consistently hosting fewer than fifteen students, the model is unsustainable. The threshold for continuation should be an average utilization of twenty-five students per hour, or a survey result indicating that 80% of users felt the access was "essential" for their exam preparation.

**Recommendation and Conditions**

Based on the equity benefits and the necessity of providing safe study spaces, I recommend approving a twenty-four-hour access pilot for the main library during the designated examination period, provided three explicit conditions are met. First, the overnight shift (12:00 AM to 6:00 AM) must be staffed by a contracted security guard rather than student workers to ensure safety and liability protection. Second, the library should open only specific zones—such as the main reading room and computer lab—to reduce HVAC and maintenance costs while allowing the rest of the building to "sleep." Third, the pilot must be strictly evaluated against the utilization thresholds described above. If the library fails to meet the minimum usage or safety standards during the trial, the initiative should be discontinued. This approach allows the university to demonstrate responsiveness to student needs while protecting institutional resources and ensuring the safety of our community.
