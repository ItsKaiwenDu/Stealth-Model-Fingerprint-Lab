# The Last Instruction

The clock above the counter read 11:47 PM. The station restaurant smelled of burnt coffee and disinfectant, and a fluorescent light buzzed and flickered in the corner like something dying.

Margot sat across from her brother, a laminated menu untouched between them. Three other people occupied the space: a woman in a red coat hunched over a bowl of soup, a teenager with headphones staring at his phone, and an old man who appeared to have fallen asleep in his booth, his head tilted back against the vinyl.

"He said never open it," Margot said. She kept her voice low, though there was no one to hear.

Julian turned his coffee cup in a slow circle. "He's been dead three weeks."

"I know how long he's been dead."

"Do you?" He looked up. His eyes were bloodshot, the skin beneath them dark and bruised. "Because you didn't come to the hospital. Not once."

Margot's jaw tightened. She looked out the window at the empty platform, the rain streaking the glass. "That's not why we're here."

"No. We're here because you called me. Said it was urgent." He leaned back. "So here I am. Urgent."

The waitress appeared—a woman in her sixties with iron-gray hair and a name tag that read DOLORES. She stood with her pen poised, her face patient in the way of people who had worked the night shift long enough to stop expecting anything interesting.

"Just coffee for me," Julian said.

Margot shook her head. "Nothing."

"Suit yourself, honey." Dolores left the pot on the table and retreated behind the counter.

Julian poured. The sound was loud in the quiet room. "So. The letter."

"Yes."

"Where is it?"

"In my bag."

"Are you going to show me, or is this an interrogation?"

Margot reached down and retrieved her purse. She pulled out a thick envelope, cream-colored, the paper stock expensive. Their father's handwriting slanted across the front in black ink: *To my children. To be destroyed unopened.*

She set it on the table between them.

Julian stared at it. "I assumed it would be thinner."

"He used both our names. Not just 'children.' He wrote out our full names. First and middle."

"I remember what he named us."

The woman in the red coat glanced over, then returned to her soup.

"Are you going to just look at it?" Margot asked.

"What do you want me to do, Margot? Tear it open right here?" He picked up his coffee, blew across the surface. "You called this meeting. You clearly have an opinion. Let's hear it."

She folded her hands. "We burn it."

"That's what he wanted."

"Yes."

"Then what's the problem?"

"The problem is you." She met his eyes. "You're going to open it. I know you are. You'll wait until I leave, and you'll tell yourself you have every right, and you'll open it. So I want you to do it now. While I'm here. So at least I know what he said."

Julian's expression didn't change, but something shifted behind his eyes. "You think I'm going to defy his last wish?"

"I think you do what you want. You always have."

"That's a hell of a thing to say."

"Is it inaccurate?"

He took a slow sip of coffee. Set the cup down with exaggerated care. "You know, I spent two months sitting with him. Every day. Watching him get smaller. Watching him forget where he was, who he was." He paused. "He asked about you. Every few days. 'Where's Margot? Is Margot coming?' And every time, I had to tell him something. Make up an excuse. 'She's busy, Dad. She'll come soon.' And he'd nod, and he'd believe me, because he had no choice."

Margot's throat tightened. "I couldn't—"

"Couldn't what? Couldn't sit in a room with a man who spent your whole life telling you what to do? Who never let you make a single decision without his approval?" Julian's voice was quiet, but it cut. "I get it. He was hard on you. He was impossible. But he was also dying, Margot. And you weren't there."

"You think I don't know that?" The words came out sharper than she intended. The teenager glanced up, then back at his phone. "You think I don't think about it every single day? Every night when I can't sleep?"

"I think you ran. Like you always do."

"I learned from the best."

Julian laughed, a short, bitter sound. "I didn't run. I just stopped caring what he thought. There's a difference."

"Is there? Because from where I stood, you vanished for fifteen years. You visited twice. Twice. You sent cards at Christmas, sometimes. And now you're sitting here acting like you were his caretaker."

"I was there at the end. That's what matters."

"Was it? Or was it just easier to play the prodigal son after he was too sick to yell at you?"

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the fluorescent buzzing seemed to pause. The old man in the corner let out a soft snore.

Julian picked up the letter. He turned it over, examining the seal. Red wax, their father's initials pressed into it.

"He never told us what this was about," Julian said. "Did he?"

"No. Just that we were to destroy it unopened. That it was his final instruction, and if we loved him, we'd respect it."

"If we loved him." He snorted. "Emotional blackmail. Even from the grave."

"That was Dad."

"Yeah. That was Dad."

Margot watched her brother's fingers trace the edge of the envelope. "What do you think is in it?"

"Does it matter?"

"It might."

He set the letter down. "I think it's the truth. About Mom. About why she left." He looked at her. "Don't you?"

She had spent twenty-three years trying not to think about it. Their mother had walked out when Margot was twelve and Julian was ten. No explanation. No note. Just an empty closet and a missing suitcase. Their father had said she was "sick" and "confused" and that they shouldn't go looking for her. They never did. Margot had wanted to, once, in her early twenties, but by then there was no trail to follow.

"He told us never to contact her," she said.

"He told us a lot of things."

"Do you think she's alive?"

"I don't know." Julian ran a hand through his hair. "But if there was something to say about her, something he couldn't tell us while he was alive—this would be it. Wouldn't it?"

The waitress returned to top off Julian's coffee. She looked at the untouched letter, the two siblings sitting in frozen silence, and seemed to decide against asking if they wanted dessert.

"We could open it," Julian said. "Just open it and be done."

"And if it destroys everything we thought we knew?"

"What if it doesn't? What if it explains everything?"

Margot looked at her brother—really looked at him, for the first time in years. The lines around his eyes. The gray in his hair. The way he held himself, tensed, defensive.

"Would it change anything?" she asked. "If we found out he lied? If we found out he drove her away? Would any of that bring her back? Would it bring him back? Would it fix us?"

"Fix us," Julian repeated. "Is that what we're supposed to be?"

"Aren't we? We're brother and sister. We're all that's left."

"You say that like it means something. Like we chose it."

"We didn't have to choose. It just is." She touched the edge of the envelope. "He gave us one last instruction. One. And he made us promise."

"He made a lot of demands. We ignored most of them."

"You did. I didn't."

"Exactly." Julian's voice softened. "You did everything he asked. You stayed close. You took over the business. You visited every Sunday. And what did it get you, Margot? Where were you when he actually needed you?"

She couldn't answer. The truth was too heavy, too knotted.

"Maybe," Julian said, "he didn't want us to read it because he was protecting himself. Not us. Maybe he didn't want us to know who he really was."

"Or maybe," she whispered, "he was protecting us."

They sat with that. The woman in the red coat stood, left money on the table, and walked out into the station. The teenager packed his bag and followed. The old man slept on.

Julian picked up the letter again. He held it over the candle in the center of the table—a small flame in a glass holder, decorative, functional.

"If we do this," he said, "there's no going back."

"I know."

"If we read it, we can't unread it."

"I know."

"But if we burn it, we'll always wonder."

"Maybe that's the point. Maybe the instruction was the gift."

Julian's hand trembled slightly. The flame wavered.

Margot reached out and placed her hand on his wrist. "I wasn't there," she said. "At the end. I know I should have been. I know I failed him. But I spent my whole life trying to be what he wanted, and it was never enough. It was never going to be enough. And the one time I tried to be something for myself—just once—it fell apart, and I ran, and I kept running."

Julian looked at her. "What were you trying to be?"

"Happy. Just... happy." She let out a shaking breath. "I wanted him to see me. Not the daughter he designed. Just me."

"He saw you, Margot. He just didn't know how to say it."

"Neither did you."

"No." He turned his hand over, squeezed hers. "I guess I didn't."

The letter sat between them, heavy with everything they weren't saying. The weight of a man who could never bend, and the children who broke trying.

Julian moved the envelope away from the flame. He set it on the table, smoothed his palm across it once, and then slid it toward Margot.

"Your call," he said. "You're the one who stayed. You're the one he trusted to keep it."

Margot looked at the letter. Her father's handwriting. His final command. *To be destroyed unopened.*

She picked it up.

The candle flickered.

Outside, a train horn sounded in the distance—low, mournful, moving on to someplace else.
