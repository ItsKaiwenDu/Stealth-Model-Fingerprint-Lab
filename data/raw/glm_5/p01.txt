The Keeper of the Tide Clock

The salt wind was the first thing to rust the gears, and it was the last thing Silas Vane thought about at night. For thirty years, he had tended the Admiralty Clock in the tower overlooking the harbor of Oakhaven. It was a brute of a machine, a brass and iron heart that drove the town’s schedule, dictating when the ferries departed and when the fishermen returned.

Silas was a man of intervals. He walked with a gait that matched the pendulum’s sway—left, right, pause, left. In his waistcoat pocket, he kept a heavy silver chronometer, a family heirloom that ran precisely three minutes and eleven seconds fast. He never adjusted it. He liked the feeling of being slightly ahead of the world, living in a future that hadn’t quite settled into the present.

The trouble began on a Tuesday in November, when the sky was the color of wet slate.

Silas was atop the ladder, oiling the escapement, when he felt the rhythm break. The clock didn’t stop; it slipped. The second hand, usually a staccato march, seemed to shudder. To the untrained eye, it was nothing—a blink. But Silas heard it. The mechanism coughed, skipping a tooth.

He descended and checked the pendulum. The beat was even. He checked the weights. True. He pulled out his silver watch. He counted the seconds against the tower’s face.

The loss was exactly eleven seconds.

He reset the hands, assuming a fluke of the wind or a momentary seizure of the gear train. He made a note in the logbook: *Minor irregularity. Adjusted.*

But the next day, it happened again.

At precisely 9:14 in the morning, the clock stuttered. Silas was watching from the square below, a bag of roasted chestnuts warming his gloved hands. He saw the second hand hesitate, then jump. Eleven seconds vanished.

He looked around the cobblestones, searching for the cause. Usually, such a mechanical slip was preceded by a jolt—a heavy cart striking the foundation, or a storm rattling the panes. The air was still. The gulls were silent.

Then, seven seconds later, a woman standing by the fishmonger’s stall dropped her basket.

It wasn’t a fumble. It was a decision. Silas saw it in her posture. She had been holding the handle with a white-knuckled grip, debating something internal. Then, her shoulders dropped, her hands opened, and the basket fell. Fish scattered across the wet stones. She didn’t scramble to pick them up; she just stared at them, her face crumpling into a look of profound, quiet exhaustion.

Silas approached her later, helping gather the scattered mackerel. "A heavy loss," he said gently.

She rubbed her forehead, leaving a smear of fish scales on her brow. "I shouldn't have come to the market today," she murmured. "I knew the ice was thin. But I thought I had to. Now look."

Silas froze. *I knew the ice was thin.* She had regretted the decision the moment she enacted it.

He returned to the tower, his mind racing. The clock had lost time *before* she dropped the basket. He checked his silver watch. The gap between his time and the tower’s time had widened.

Over the following weeks, Silas became a statue in the town square. He abandoned his oiling can to stand in the shadow of the apothecary, his eyes glued to the clock face, his notebook in hand. He began to see the pattern, a terrifying, invisible geometry.

The clock did not lose time because of the regret. It lost time before it.

On a Thursday, the clock stuttered at 2:00 PM. Silas scanned the crowd. He saw a young man, a dockworker, leaning against a lamppost, lighting a cigarette. He hadn’t moved toward the ferry terminal yet. He stood there, smoke curling around his face, staring at the ticket booth.

The clock skipped eleven seconds.

Five seconds later, the young man pushed off the lamppoint and jogged toward the booth, buying a one-way ticket to the mainland.

Silas intercepted him at the gangplank. "Leaving?"

"For the city," the boy said, his eyes red-rimmed. "I can’t stand the sound of the waves anymore."

"You’ll regret it," Silas said, the words tumbling out before he could stop them.

The boy laughed bitterly. "Maybe. But staying is worse."

Two weeks later, Silas heard the boy had been robbed on his first night in the city and was now sleeping in a shelter. The decision to buy that ticket was a wound that hadn't stopped bleeding.

Silas’s logbook filled with frantic notations. He drew lines connecting the skips to the people. He realized the clock was not malfunctioning; it was responding to a frequency he couldn’t hear. It was measuring the weight of a soul before the soul itself knew the burden it was about to carry.

The phenomenon grew worse. The skips came faster. Sometimes three or four in an hour. The town began to drift. The 5:15 ferry departed at 5:14 by the tower’s face, arriving "early" to the confusion of the mainlanders. The church bells rang out of sync, creating a dissonance that made the town’s dogs howl.

Silas stopped sleeping. He sat in the clock tower, watching the gears turn. He realized the terrifying implication: the clock wasn’t just predicting regret. It was offering a reprieve. It was eating the time. If the clock skipped eleven seconds, did the decision actually happen in that void? Did the universe blink, allowing the tragedy to occur in a space that didn't technically exist?

He had to stop it. He had to catch up.

One evening, a heavy fog rolled in, dampening the sound of the surf. Silas was calibrating the minute hand when he felt a sudden, violent lurch in the mechanism. It wasn’t a skip. It was a seizure.

The clock lost eleven seconds. Then, trembling, it lost eleven more.

Silas ran to the window. The square below was empty. The fog was thick, obscuring the shops, the fountain, the world. There was no one down there. No one to make a decision.

He checked his silver watch. The gap was massive now. The tower clock was reading 6:42 PM. His pocket watch read 6:55 PM.

The clock shuddered again. Another eleven seconds erased.

Silas backed away from the window. He was the only person in the tower. He was the only person in the vicinity. If the clock was losing time, and there was no one else around, the decision had to be his.

He looked at his tools spread out on the workbench. He looked at the heavy brass gears turning in their relentless cycle. He thought about the young man on the ferry, the woman with the fish. He thought about his own thirty years of solitude in this stone spire.

*What decision am I about to make?*

He stood still, terrified to move, terrified to choose. If he moved left, would he knock over the oil lamp? If he moved right, would he slip on the damp floor? He closed his eyes, trying to divine the future, trying to sense the shape of his own coming regret.

The clock skipped again. The second hand jumped.

He realized with a cold dread that the regret wasn’t about a fall or a fire. It was about the clock itself. The council had sent a letter last week—a letter he had ignored. They wanted to electrify the mechanism. They wanted to remove the pendulum and install a quartz regulator. They said the drift was unprofessional.

Silas had burned the letter.

The clock shuddered. Eleven seconds gone.

He looked at the pendulum, swinging in its wide, ancient arc. He had to stop the drift. He had to reset the hands to match his silver watch, to anchor the town back to the "correct" time.

He reached out, his hand shaking, to grab the minute hand and force it forward—to catch up to the reality his pocket watch claimed was true.

The clock skipped.

He grabbed the cold metal hand. He pulled.

The gear train screamed. It wasn’t designed to be forced backward or forward by hand; it was designed to be wound, to be trusted. The resistance was immense. A brass cog inside the housing cracked with a sound like a gunshot.

Silas recoiled. The machine groaned. The pendulum swung wildly, then stopped.

The silence that followed was absolute. The Admiralty Clock, the heart of Oakhaven, was dead.

Silas stood in the quiet tower, the sound of his own ragged breathing replacing the ticking. He looked at the face. The hands were frozen at 6:44 PM.

He pulled out his silver watch. It ticked on, cheerful and oblivious, reading 6:58 PM.

He had stopped the clock. He had broken the mechanism. And in doing so, he had saved the town from the drift.

Or so he thought.

He descended the stairs, his legs heavy. He walked out into the square. The fog was lifting. People were emerging from the pubs and shops, looking up at the silent tower, confusion on their faces. They were checking their phones, their wrists.

Silas felt a swell of pride. He had severed the link. He had stopped the engine of regret.

He walked toward the harbor, needing the air. He saw the ferry captain standing at the rail of the incoming boat. The captain waved, shouting something over the engine noise.

Silas waved back. He felt free. No more skipping. No more watching. He had made a decision to break the clock, and he didn't regret it. The machine had been a tyrant.

He reached the dock just as the ferry docked. The captain came down the gangplank, his face grave.

"Silas," the captain said. "You need to see this."

"See what?" Silas asked. "I fixed it. The drift is over."

The captain looked at his own watch, then at the frozen tower, then back at Silas. "The drift? We’ve been running on your tower’s time for years, Silas. It’s why the ferries never crashed."

Silas frowned. "My time? My clock was always behind. I kept my watch three minutes fast."

The captain pulled a pocket watch from his coat. It was a standard railway issue, synchronized to the mainland.

"This is mainland time," the captain said, clicking it open. "It matches your tower exactly. Or it did, before you stopped it."

Silas stared. "That's impossible. My silver watch... I keep it fast."

"Let me see," the captain said.

Silas handed over the heavy silver chronometer. The captain looked at it, then at his own.

"Silas," the captain whispered. "Your silver watch is the one that's wrong."

Silas snatched it back. He looked at the face. It was ticking steadily. It read 7:00 PM. The captain’s watch read 6:56 PM. The frozen tower read 6:44 PM.

"No," Silas stammered. "I keep it three minutes ahead. I always have. To stay ahead of the sorrow."

The captain shook his head slowly. "You’ve been living in a time pocket. That silver watch of yours... it hasn't been running fast. It’s been running perfectly true. For the last thirty years, *you* have been the one lagging behind. The clock tower... it wasn't losing time, Silas. It was waiting."

Silas looked back at the tower. The hands were frozen.

He realized, with a sudden, sickening vertigo, that the clock hadn't been skipping into the past. It had been dragging the town backward to match him. He was the anchor. Every time he had checked his silver watch and decided the tower was slow, he had forced the town to linger in a moment that should have passed.

The decision he had just made—the decision to break the clock—was not the regret.

The regret was the thirty years he had spent winding the clock, ensuring that the town never moved forward, trapping everyone in his own private, slow-moving dusk.

The captain looked at his watch. "We're late for the mainland departure," he said quietly. "We're always late."

Silas stood alone on the dock as the passengers shuffled past, their faces grey and tired, looking at the dead clock with something like relief. They didn't know why they felt lighter, but Silas did.

He looked at his silver watch. It ticked on, loud and arrogant, counting seconds that no longer mattered. He realized now that the "buffer" he had always cherished was not a lead on the world, but a delay. He had not been living in the future. He had been holding everyone back in the past.

He had made the decision to break the machine, thinking it would save them from the drift. But the machine had been the only thing keeping him company in his lag. Now, he was truly, finally alone in the wrong time.

He opened the glass case of his silver watch. With a trembling thumb, he pressed the reset button, holding it until the hands stopped.

He let out a breath, watching the second hand freeze.

He regretted that, too.
