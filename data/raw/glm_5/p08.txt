**The Bench**

The bench was made of a wood so dark it looked like it was still wet, though Leo knew it wasn’t. He pressed his thumb into the grain, trying to leave a mark, but the varnish was hard and unyielding. It smelled of lemon polish and old dust, a sharp, chemical scent that lingered in the back of his throat.

He shifted his weight. His legs were too short to reach the floor, so they dangled, the rubber soles of his sneakers knocking a rhythm against the bench’s metal frame. *Thump, thump, pause. Thump, thump, pause.*

"Stop that," his mother whispered. She didn't look at him. She was staring at the heavy double doors at the end of the corridor, her hands clutching a leather handbag so tightly her knuckles had turned the color of skim milk. She was wearing her blue dress, the one that made a swishing sound when she walked, and her heels were clicking against the terrazzo floor even though she was sitting still. It sounded like ticking.

Leo stopped kicking. He looked down the long hallway. It was a place of echoes. Every shoe squeak, every distant cough, bounced off the high ceiling and came back doubled. The walls were paneled in the same dark wood, framed by marble that was streaked with grey and white, like a frozen television screen.

To his left, his father was standing by a water fountain. He wasn't drinking. He was leaning against the wall, one ankle crossed over the other, playing with the change in his pocket. Leo could hear the metallic clink of the coins. It was a nervous sound, unlike the solid thwack of the coins hitting the ceramic piggy bank at home. His father was wearing a suit that was slightly too large in the shoulders. He looked like he was trying to take up more space than he actually needed.

"Leo," a voice said.

Leo turned. A woman in a grey skirt suit was crouching in front of him. She had a clipboard and a pen that clicked loudly. She smelled like coffee and peppermint. She wasn't looking at him with the tight, stretched look his mother had. She was smiling, but it didn't reach her eyes. It stopped at her cheeks.

"Do you remember me?" she asked. "We talked last week. I have the coloring book."

Leo nodded. He remembered. He didn't want the coloring book. He wanted to go to the food court at the mall where the ceiling fans spun slowly and the air smelled like frying dough.

"I just need to ask you a few questions while we wait," she said, her voice low and smooth, like butter melting. "Just some easy things. Favorite subjects. What you had for breakfast."

She clicked the pen. *Click, click.*

Leo answered. He talked about cereal. He talked about math. He didn't mention that the cereal had been eaten in a hurry because the car wouldn't start, or that he had forgotten his math homework because the backpack was still packed in a box in the hall. The woman wrote everything down. Her handwriting was small and jagged, covering the white lines of the paper. She wrote so much for such small answers.

While he talked, he watched his parents. They weren't talking to each other. They were two islands separated by a sea of polished floor. But they were both watching the woman with the clipboard. His mother’s lips were moving slightly, rehearsing something. His father had stopped jingling his coins; his hands were shoved deep into his pockets, making the fabric of his trousers bunch up.

The woman stood up. "You're doing great, Leo. Just stay here. Don't wander off."

She walked away, her heels making sharp *tock-tock-tock* sounds against the floor. She went toward a group of people standing near the double doors. There were two men in dark suits and another woman in a black robe, though the robe wasn't on, just draped over her arm. They formed a circle, a huddle of grey and black.

Leo watched them. He couldn't hear the words, but he could hear the tone. It was a negotiation tone. It was the same tone his mother used when the mechanic told her the price of a new alternator. It was the same tone his father used on the phone with the insurance company. It was a tight, bargaining sound. It sounded like *If you give me this, I will give you that.*

But there was no alternator here. There was no car.

Leo looked at his hands. He looked at the water spot on his sneaker. He looked at the pattern of the terrazzo floor—chips of red and blue embedded in the beige. He felt a strange sensation in his stomach, like the time the elevator had lurched at the mall. It was a dropping feeling.

He realized he was the only one sitting.

Everyone else was standing. The adults were vertical, looming. They were arranging themselves in specific configurations. His mother stood near the water fountain, her arms crossed over her chest. His father stood near a pillar. The woman with the clipboard moved between them. She would talk to his mother, then walk to his father. Then she would talk to the men in suits.

They were passing something back and forth. Not a physical object, but glances. Nods. Small shrugs.

His mother looked at his father and said something. It wasn't a shout. It was quiet, forceful. His father rubbed the back of his neck. He nodded, once. It was a heavy nod, like he was agreeing to carry something very heavy up a flight of stairs.

Leo understood, suddenly, that they were building something. They were constructing a schedule. He didn't see the paper, but he could feel the lines being drawn. He imagined a calendar, the kind with the red numbers for holidays. He saw the days being cut apart.

*Tuesdays.* That was a word floating in the air. He saw his father’s mouth shape the word. *Tuesday. Thursday. Alternate weekends.*

The words were heavy. They fell out of the adults' mouths and landed on the floor, turning into bricks. They were building a wall, or maybe a bridge. A bridge that he would have to walk across, back and forth, carrying his backpack.

The woman with the clipboard walked back over. She didn't crouch this time. She stood over him, blocking the light from the ceiling fixture. Her shadow fell across his lap.

"Leo," she said. "We’re going to go into the room now. It’s going to be very quick. You just have to sit in the big chair and listen. Can you do that?"

"Is it like school?" Leo asked. His voice sounded thin in the big hallway.

"It's a little like school," she said. "But quieter. And there are no tests."

"Okay," he said.

"Good boy." She touched his shoulder. Her hand was light, barely there. It felt like a ghost passing through him.

She turned to his parents. "We have the agreement drawn up. It’s standard. Once the magistrate signs off on the minutes, the order is temporary. We’ll review the specifics in thirty days."

*Agreement. Magistrate. Order.*

The words washed over Leo. They tasted like metal. He looked at his father. His father was looking at his watch again. He wasn't looking at Leo. He was looking at the time. He was calculating how much time he had, how much time he was losing, how much time he was gaining.

His mother walked over to the bench. She reached out and smoothed Leo's collar. Her fingers brushed his neck. They were cold.

"Listen to the judge," she whispered. Her breath was warm, but her voice was shaky. "And when… when you go with your father, remember to pack your inhaler. Don’t let him forget the inhaler."

"I won't," Leo said.

"And be good." She pulled back. Her eyes were wet, but she blinked it away, fast. She was an adult, and adults in this building did not break. They just bent.

His father walked over. He stood at a distance, respecting the invisible line that seemed to have been drawn on the floor between him and Leo’s mother. "I’ll have him back on Sunday by six," he said. He wasn't talking to Leo. He was talking to the air, to the woman with the clipboard, to the legal contract that was forming in the ether.

"Sunday," his mother repeated. She didn't say "Have him back." She just said the word, testing its weight.

Leo sat very still. He felt like a package. A box marked *Fragile* or *This Side Up*. He looked down at his sneakers. They were scuffed. He had gotten the scuff from climbing the fence in the backyard to get the soccer ball. That was three weeks ago. Before the hallway. Before the lawyers. Before the word *agreement* became the only word anyone cared about.

He realized that the backyard was no longer his backyard. It was a location. A coordinate. It was part of the *first weekend* or the *third weekend*. The adults were dividing the world. They were taking the map of his life and drawing a thick black line down the middle.

The double doors opened. A man in a uniform poked his head out. "Case number four-twenty-two," he called. The numbers hung in the air, sterile and final.

"That's us," the woman in the grey suit said. "Let's go."

Leo slid off the bench. His feet hit the floor with a slap. He stood up. He was short in the forest of legs and briefcases.

"Walk between us," his mother said.

Leo took a step. He looked at his father. His father looked at the ceiling. He looked at his mother. She was looking straight ahead, her chin tilted up, leading the way toward the doors.

Leo walked. He felt the distance between his parents widen, a canyon opening up. He was the bridge. He was the thin strip of land connecting two separate countries that used to be one. He thought about his room, his dinosaur sheets, the nightlight that cast stars on the ceiling. He wondered if the stars would stay in the same place, or if he would have to learn new constellations now.

The doors were thick and heavy. The man in the uniform held them open. As Leo passed through, the air changed. It got colder, filtered through a machine. It smelled like paper and ink and silence.

He saw the room ahead. It was small, with a high bench at the front, like a pulpit. There were chairs lined up in rows. It looked like a church where no one sang.

His parents slowed down, waiting for the lawyers to go first. They were whispering again, the urgent whisper of business being finished.

Leo looked down at his hand. He made a fist, then opened it. He looked at the lines on his palm. They were his own lines, not the ones on the paper the woman held. But as he stepped into the room, he felt the distinct, crushing weight of the paper winning. The paper was stronger. The paper had the stamps and the signatures. The paper decided where he would sleep, and who would sign his report card, and which day was Tuesday.

He sat in the chair indicated by the woman. It was hard wood, like the bench in the hall. He folded his hands in his lap. He watched the adults move to their designated spots, their designated tables. They knew exactly where to stand. They had been given coordinates.

Leo watched the magistrate enter. He watched the room rise. He watched the ritual of the gavel. And as the sound cracked the air, he felt the final piece of the arrangement click into place. He wasn't a person in a family anymore. He was a schedule. He was a visitation. He was a decision that had been made over his head, while he sat kicking his feet against a bench, watching the dust motes dance in the light, waiting for permission to leave.
