# The Bridge at Millhaven

The first light came gray through the river mist, and with it the usual sounds of Millhaven waking—gulls crying over the wharves, the far-off clatter of a cart on cobblestones, the hiss of lanterns being extinguished in the customs house. But by the time the fog began to lift from the Verre River, it was clear that something was wrong. The great iron bridge that connected Millhaven's north and south shores stood empty, its gates chained shut, a single constable standing before the barrier with his hands clasped behind his back.

Tom Sulloway noticed it from the deck of the *Meredith*, a coal barge that had docked in the small hours. He was a wiry man in his forties, a roustabout who had worked the Verre for twenty years, and he had expected to cross the bridge before sunrise to collect his pay packet from the company office on the north side. He stood at the rail, squinting through the remaining mist, watching a small cluster of people gather at the far end. One of them gestured wildly. Another seemed to be arguing with the constable.

"What's happened?" asked Billy Creath, a younger hand who had come up beside him.

Sulloway spat into the water. "Bridge is shut. Someone's chained the gates."

"Chained?"

"You see any carts going across?"

By six o'clock, the word had reached the wharf proper. Dockworkers gathering for the morning shift stood in groups of three or four, speculating. Some had heard that a support strut had cracked in the night. Others whispered about a suicide—someone had jumped, they said, and the police were searching the water. Old Patterson, who had run the chandler's shop on Harbor Street for thirty years, told anyone who would listen that he'd heard a terrible grinding sound around two in the morning, like metal tearing against metal. But Patterson was known to exaggerate, and half the men who listened to him walked away shaking their heads.

The first real disruption came with the travelers. A commercial traveler named Arthur Vane arrived at the south approach at half past six, portmanteau in hand, expecting to cross to the railway station on the north side. He found the gates chained and the constable immovable.

"I've a train at eight," Vane protested. "The line to Pemberton. I cannot miss it."

"Bridge is closed, sir. Orders from above."

"Whose orders? The municipality? The railway company?"

"Cannot say, sir. I'm just to keep it closed."

Vane argued for another ten minutes before retreating to a nearby coffee house, where he spread his grievance to anyone who would listen. By seven, he had convinced himself—and several others—that the closure was a scheme by the railway company to force travelers onto the ferry service, which was now doing brisk business at twice the ordinary rate. A shopkeeper's wife, overhearing this, added her own observation: the ferry operator had been seen in conversation with a municipal official the day before. The speculation grew legs.

At the municipal offices on Court Street, the clerk Harold Fenwick was already besieged. He had arrived at his desk at half past six to find a stack of notes on his blotter, each one marked urgent. Merchants demanding to know when their deliveries could cross. A doctor insisting he needed to reach a patient. A solicitor threatening legal action if he missed his court appearance. Fenwick knew nothing. The bridge was under the jurisdiction of the Public Works Department, and the Public Works Department had sent no explanation. He spent the morning composing careful, noncommittal replies and avoiding the callers who lined the corridor outside his office.

Upstairs, in the mayor's chambers, the situation was no clearer. Mayor Caleb Halloway had received a telegram at six that morning—brief and opaque. *Bridge closed pending inspection. Advise public to use alternate routes.* No further details. When he had sent a runner to the Public Works office, he was told the chief engineer had been summoned to the bridge before dawn and had not returned. The mayor sat at his desk, watching the clock, composing and then discarding statements he could not bring himself to issue. Without facts, he could offer only reassurance, and he had learned long ago that the public preferred hard truths to empty comfort.

On the north side of the river, the railway station became an unlikely clearinghouse for rumor. Travelers stranded by the closure gathered in the waiting room, comparing notes. A woman in a traveling suit claimed she had seen a crew of men in work clothes carrying tools onto the bridge at four in the morning, but she could not say what they had done. A businessman from Pemberton insisted that the bridge had been overdue for repairs for months and that this was simply the city finally addressing the matter, though he could not explain why no notice had been given. A young man with a notebook, clearly a journalist, moved through the crowd asking questions, his pen scratching across the pages. He would not say which paper he represented.

By eight o'clock, the morning had taken on a strange texture. The ordinary rhythms of commerce continued—carts still arrived at the wharves, laborers still hauled crates, the steam whistle at the sawmill still blew—but underneath it all ran a current of unease. At the grocery on Mill Street, Mrs. Althea Crane listened as her customers debated the closure. One insisted that a ship had collided with the bridge's central pier in the night; another claimed to have heard that a section of the roadway had simply given way. A third, an older man with a grim expression, suggested darker possibilities—sabotage, perhaps, or a threat against the city that officials were keeping quiet. Mrs. Crane said nothing, but she made a note to send her son to the north side by ferry, in case the situation grew worse.

The ferry, indeed, was the only reliable fact of the morning. It ran back and forth across the Verre with unusual frequency, its decks crowded with passengers who had expected to cross on foot or by carriage. The ferryman, a taciturn old fellow named Willis, made no comment on the bridge. He simply collected his fares and guided the vessel across the brown water. Some passengers found his silence suspicious. Others were simply grateful for the passage.

By midmorning, a small crowd had gathered at the south approach, watching the bridge as if expecting it to reveal its secret. The constable had been joined by two others, and a wooden barrier had been erected in front of the gates. A boy with a newspaper under his arm worked the edges of the crowd, calling out the headlines. The paper had nothing definitive—only a brief paragraph acknowledging the closure and promising further details as they became available. A few people bought copies; most simply shook their heads and turned away.

Sulloway, having finally reached the north side by ferry, stood at the company office window waiting for his pay. The clerk counted out the coins slowly, asking about the mood on the wharves. Sulloway shrugged.

"Same as always," he said. "Men talking. Nobody knows anything."

"Worst kind of morning," the clerk said.

Sulloway pocketed his wages and stepped back into the sunlit street. Above him, the bridge stood silent, its ironwork gleaming in the late morning light, its gates still chained, its secret still its own.
