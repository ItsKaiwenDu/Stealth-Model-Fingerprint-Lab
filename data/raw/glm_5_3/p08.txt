# The Long Bench

The bench in the hallway has a crack running through its third slat, and I have been keeping my thumb in it for most of the morning. It's the kind of crack that got there slowly. Somebody pressed on it for years, waiting, the way I am now, and the wood gave up a little at a time.

My aunt Dana sits on one side of me and Mr. Pell sits on the other. Mr. Pell is not really ours. He belongs to the office with the frosted windows on Third Street, the one where the magazines are all about houses and boats, and he wears a tie with tiny gray squares on it. When he crosses his legs, his sock shows, and the sock has a hole he doesn't know about, a little moon of skin above his heel. I looked at it for a long time during the first part of the morning and it made me like him more.

Down the hall there is a water fountain, and every few minutes someone goes to it and drinks. It makes a sound like a small animal, a gurgle and then a clunk, and then the water goes off. The floor is beige tile, shiny, and my sneakers make a squeak when I slide them even a tiny bit, so I have stopped sliding them. Aunt Dana said *we're just going to sit quietly for a while, okay, buddy*, and that was forty minutes ago, or an hour, or longer. Time works differently in this building. The clock at the end of the hall has a second hand that jumps instead of sweeping, and I've watched it jump maybe six hundred times.

Room 4B is behind a brown door with a brass number that someone has polished so hard it's going thin. My mother is behind that door. So is my father, and a woman named Ms. Okonkwo who has a folder with my name on the tab—I saw it when she carried it past us, *M. CASTELLANOS*, in black marker, and the M is my name but I'm not going to think about the rest. Ms. Okonkwo smells like cough drops and she let me pick a candy from her desk once, a butterscotch, and she said *you're being very patient* in a voice like she meant it and also like she'd said it to a hundred kids before me.

Nobody has explained why I can't go in the room. Aunt Dana said it would be *boring*, said it fast, and Mr. Pell looked at his folder and turned a page that wasn't ready to be turned. I know boring. Boring is the DMV with Aunt Dana in March. This is not boring for them, whatever it is. I can tell because of the shoes.

That's the thing I've figured out about this building. You can read everything by shoes. The lawyers' shoes are the shiniest, hard and pointy like beetle shells, and they walk fast from one end of the hall to the other with paper cups of coffee that steam. The clerks wear flat shoes, comfortable ones, the kind that give up easily. My father's shoes this morning were the brown ones, but he shined them, and he never shines them, and I saw a drip of shoe polish on his sock when he crossed his legs in the elevator. My mother wore her boots with the heel, and she stood in the elevator instead of leaning, and I wanted to tell her about my thumb and the crack in the bench and about how Mr. Pell's sock has a hole, all the small things I tell her, but the elevator was full of beetle-shell shoes and everyone was quiet the way people are quiet in hospitals.

A man in a windbreaker just came out of 4A, the room next to ours, with a woman who was crying quietly, not the loud kind, the kind that's mostly breathing. He put his hand on her back and steered her toward the elevators. Nobody on our bench looked up except me. I looked at their shoes instead: his sneakers, her flats, one of the straps unbuckled. When the elevator doors closed, Aunt Dana let out a breath through her nose and Mr. Pell said, "Could be a while," and Aunt Dana said, "I know it."

Then a strange thing happened. A woman came out of 4B—the door opened just wide enough for her—and she leaned down to Mr. Pell and whispered. I heard three words: *almost*, *number*, and *summer*. Mr. Pell nodded twice. The woman looked at me and her face did something complicated, a smile starting and then changing its mind, and she went back in and the door clicked.

Summer. I held on to that word. Summer is the pool on Delancey Street and the fan in Aunt Dana's kitchen and the year my father taught me to whistle using a blade of grass, the year before. Summer is a word that belongs to a schedule, and schedules belong to somebody, and I understood—slowly, the way you understand it's gotten dark while you were reading—that inside that room there is a piece of paper, or there will be, with a summer on it, and my name near the summer, and people who are not me are deciding which door the summer comes out of.

I don't know how I knew. Nobody said it. It was in the whisper, and in the polished shoes, and in the folder with my name that Ms. Okonkwo carries like something breakable. It was in the way Aunt Dana keeps touching the collar of her blouse, fixing it though nothing's wrong with it, and the way she asked me twice this morning if I was warm enough. It was in the fact of the bench. You don't make a kid wait on a bench for something boring.

"Can I get water?" I said.

Aunt Dana stood up like she'd been waiting for the question. We walked to the fountain together and she drank after me and wiped her mouth with her thumb. On the way back she stopped, halfway, and squeezed my shoulder, and I saw that her eyes were wet in the corners, just the corners, and she blinked it away and said, "You want a chip from the machine? They got those pretend-healthy ones."

The vending machine is lit from inside like an aquarium. I chose the pretzels because they take the longest to eat. While I worked on them, another lawyer came down the hall toward 4B, a woman I hadn't seen before, with a rolling bag, and Mr. Pell stood up to meet her and they talked with their heads close together, and I heard *the girl* and *his sister* and *final*, and then the woman looked over at me, one quick look, and went in.

The girl. That's me, in there. I am a piece of paper with a summer on it and a girl on it, and the door has my whole rest-of-it behind it, being talked about in low voices so the pieces of it won't spill out into the hall.

Mr. Pell sat back down. His sock-hole showed again. Aunt Dana took my pretzel bag and held it while I got the last crumbs, then folded it into a small square and put it in her purse, which she does with all the wrappers, all the receipts, everything.

The clock jumped. The fountain gurgled down the hall.

Then the brown door opened all the way, and Ms. Okonkwo was standing in it, and she smiled at me the way adults smile when they have finished deciding something, and she said, "Mia? Come on in, sweetheart. We're ready for you now."

Aunt Dana's hand found my shoulder again. Mr. Pell picked up the folder. I took my thumb out of the crack in the bench, and I stood up, and my sneakers squeaked on the shiny floor, once, loud in the quiet hall—and I walked toward the door.
