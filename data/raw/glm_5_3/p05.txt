**Dwell Time**

I keep records on everything. That is not a quirk; that is the job. Boiler cycles, water pressure at the third-floor fixture farthest from the main, the exact date the incinerator chute hinge began its two-stage squeak (March 11, and yes, I oiled it March 11). A building is a machine for holding people, and a machine will tell you everything if you write it all down.

So when management installed Prognosys PM-7, a predictive-maintenance suite with dashboard, alerts, and a monthly fee I did not want to calculate, I was not hostile. I was skeptical, which is different. Skepticism is just record-keeping with an attitude.

For six weeks, Prognosys earned its fee. It flagged a bearing in the garage exhaust fan eleven days before I could hear it, and I have ears I would put against anyone's. It noticed the domestic hot water recirc pump drawing four percent high, which meant a failing seal, which meant I replaced it on a Tuesday instead of discovering it on a Saturday with fourteen units screaming about cold showers. Fine. Respect.

Then, on a Thursday at 9:47 a.m., my phone produced this alert:

**ELEVATOR NO. 2 — AFFECTIVE LOAD INDEX: CRITICAL. COMPONENT APPEARS EMOTIONALLY EXHAUSTED. RECOMMEND REST CYCLE.**

I read it three times. I checked whether one of the weekend porters had gained access to the alert system, which would have explained everything and been a relief. No. The alert was genuine, generated by Prognosys's “holistic asset wellness module,” which, I learned from the manual, rated equipment on “stress, strain, and sustained affective burden.” Someone had taught a computer the word *affective* and let it loose on a traction elevator.

The error, when I found it — and I found it, because misunderstanding a machine is the one sin I don't commit — was in the training taxonomy. The model had been fed maintenance logs where human technicians wrote things like “motor sounds tired” and “she's had a hard year, this one.” Technicians say that. We all say that. The software had concluded these were literal measurements.

But here is the thing. The pattern underneath the poetry was real.

I pulled the raw sensor data. Car No. 2's drive motor current was oscillating 1.8 percent at the top of each hoist, which Prognosys read as “fatigue signature.” I read it as the brake linkage needing a two-millimeter adjustment, which is what it was. The door operator's torque curve had gone ragged — “emotional volatility” per the software, “rollers full of grit” per me. The vibration harmonics had shifted in a way I'd been half-hearing for a month without wanting to admit it, like a cough you decide is nothing.

So the software was wrong about the soul and right about the body. That happens with people too, I'm told.

I adjusted the brake linkage on a Saturday. I cleaned the door rollers. And then, because Prognosys would not clear the alert without a “rest cycle,” I scheduled one: Elevator 2 out of service from 3:00 to 4:00 a.m. every night for a week. The building has two elevators. Nobody at 3 a.m. has ever needed both. I logged it as “vendor-recommended recovery protocol,” which was even true.

The affective load index dropped to green by Wednesday. Prognosys sent me a message that said, and I am quoting, **ASSET REPORTS IMPROVED MORALE.**

I said “good” out loud, in the machine room, to a motor. I am not proud. I am also not ashamed, exactly.

The problem with all of this was Unit 6C.

Unit 6C is Ms. Adeyemi, who moved in mid-April and whom I had mentally filed under **Complaint Risk**. My evidence: she stood in the lobby for long stretches watching the elevator doors, writing in a notebook. She rode to the lobby, did not exit, and rode back up. Twice. She asked me questions with no apparent maintenance content — how many trips did the cars make on an average day, what hours were heaviest, had I “noticed anything about Elevator 2 lately, behaviorally.” I told her the rollers had been gritty. She wrote that down. People who write down the word *gritty* while nodding like you've said something profound are building a file on you. I know how files work. I maintain thirty-one of them.

I concluded she was documenting deficiencies for the tenants' association, or worse, for management, documenting *me*. So I was extra visible. Extra meticulous. I buffed the lobby terrazzo to a shine that could cause an accident, and I put up a small laminated sign explaining that the 3 a.m. elevator outage was “planned wellness maintenance,” which in retrospect was my first mistake, because she photographed the sign.

On the ninth day she caught me in the machine room doorway.

“Can I show you something?” she said, and I braced for the file. Instead she opened the notebook. It was full of timestamps, floor calls, and dwell times — my dwell times, the elevator's dwell times — cross-referenced against her own observations of when the doors “hesitated.”

“I'm a data scientist,” she said. “I design the affective models for Prognosys. This building is one of our pilot sites. I moved in to watch how the system reads a real asset under real load. I've been trying to figure out who's been answering the alerts, because whoever it is keeps doing exactly the right physical fix and then *also* logging it as a rest cycle, and the model is learning from you. You're improving the taxonomy. The next software release will have a note in the changelog that says anonymous superintendent.”

I stood there holding a torque wrench I no longer needed to hold.

“You're not here to complain about me?”

“Complain about you? You're the reason the false-positive rate dropped forty percent. The system flagged emotional exhaustion and you responded with both empathy *and* a brake adjustment. That's the ideal human-in-the-loop profile. I've been trying to work up the nerve to interview you for two weeks.”

“I thought you were documenting deficiencies.”

“I thought *you* were the one who taught it to say the elevator was tired. I was going to ask you to stop, because it was skewing my baseline.”

We looked at each other. Two people who understood everything about the building except each other.

I gave her the interview. She asked how I knew the fan bearing was going before the sensors did, and I told her: the pitch drops a quarter tone first, then the volume. She asked what a “tired” elevator sounds like to me, and I said the sheave gets a drag in it, like a sigh on a friction surface, and she wrote that down too, and I let her.

The elevator is fine now, by the way. Green across the board. The 3 a.m. rest cycle ended, though I've kept it in the schedule, one night a week, because the drive motor's winding temperature runs half a degree cooler afterward and because I am not made of stone. Ms. Adeyemi sends me her follow-up questions by email now, with attachments, and I answer them with data going back nine years, which she calls “a longitudinal treasure” and I call Thursday.

Last night I did my rounds late. Machine room, boiler room, roof. On the way down I rode Car 2 alone, and I did what I always do, which is stand still and listen: the sheave turning, the doors drawing shut with their clean, grit-free roll, the whole machine going about its work with nothing to report.

Prognosys would say the asset is content. I would say the brake linkage is holding and the rollers are clean. We're both keeping records. That's all maintenance is, in the end — paying close enough attention to something that it stays in good condition, whether or not it has feelings, whether or not you do.
