# The Word for Both

People ask what a translator does when a language runs out. Languages never run out. People do. In thirty-one years I have translated for courts, for cardiologists, for a widowed archbishop and for a manufacturer of ski bindings, and I have done most of it from Sordano, the speech of Val Sorda, a valley of eleven villages where the words are older than the roads. The valley tongue has four terms for the sound water makes under ice and no word at all for coincidence, because in Sordano nothing merely happens; everything was assembled beforehand, by someone, for a reason. I was raised on that grammar. It has made me patient with sentences and merciless with liars.

In September a lawyer in Milan engaged me on behalf of the Menegus family. The job: a certified translation of an apology dictated in 1974 by Lucia Ossoler, matriarch of the mill family, to the parish priest of San Boldo, who took it down and kept it in the parish archive until his successor found it while boxing up the ledgers. The Menegus heirs needed it in English because the deed ceremony required it—the water rights to the millstream had to be settled, and the American branch of the family had to be present for the signature.

Three weeks later a notary in Denver engaged me on behalf of the De Zolt family. He set a photocopy on the table and I recognized it before he finished his sentence: the same foxed upper corner, the same bloom of damp across the third page, the same three corrections in the priest's tidy hand. Word for word, line for line, the letters were identical. Except for the last line, which the priest had noted was written by Lucia herself, her hand already failing.

One copy read: *E ve lasso la rògia.*
The other read: *E ve lasso la ràbia.*

The notary did not know there was a rival family. The lawyer in Milan had described the De Zolts as "persons of bad faith who may contest the title." The De Zolts described the Menegus heirs as "squatters on our grandmother's grief." The paper needed no family tree. It told me everything: two brothers, Pietro and Aldo, who split the millstream in 1958 after their father died—alternating days for the water, then a missed day, then an accusation, then sixty years of courts. The brothers died unreconciled. Lucia, who had kept her silence like a second kitchen, dictated her apology at seventy and wrote the last line herself. The priest made copies. One line survived intact. One line was corrupted, somewhere, by someone transcribing Lucia's spidery hand—a *g* misread as a *b*, a single letter, and behind that letter two families built sixty years of argument.

Each family wanted the final line translated its way. The Menegus lawyer was direct: *rògia* meant the millstream itself, the channel cut in 1612, and if Lucia was leaving the water to both sons, then the water had never been Pietro's to take. The De Zolt notary was equally direct: *ràbia* meant rage, and a grandmother leaving her rage behind her was releasing them from everything, claim and counterclaim alike. Each side offered to double my fee. Each side wanted the word that ended the argument in their favor.

Here is what I knew, and knowing settled the matter before I closed the folder.

Lucia's letter contains no Italian. None. The priest noted that she refused Italian even for numbers—she said the days of the week in Sordano or she did not say them. *Ràbia* is borrowed Italian. *Rògia* is the valley's own word, and it is the word that cannot be carried across, because in Sordano it means two things at once. It is the channel that turns the mill wheel. It is also the quarrel. The elders said *far rògia* of any long dispute, and they lowered their voices when they said it, the same way they lowered their voices at the stream itself. The word for the water and the word for the war over the water are one word, because in that valley nobody has ever pretended they were separate things.

So the choice was never between two readings of the last line. The choice was what to do with a word that means both. I could write *millstream* and hand the Menegus family a weapon dressed as an apology. I could write *quarrel* and hand the De Zolts an accusation dressed as absolution. Or I could do what I was trained to do, which is neither.

I do not agonize. Agonizing is for people who have not done the reading. I delivered one certified translation, one, to the notary in the valley, with a translator's note appended in the required place. The final line reads:

*And I leave you the rògia: the water that runs between you, and everything it has carried.*

The note explains, in the flat prose the law prefers, that the term denotes simultaneously an irrigation channel and a protracted dispute, that the two meanings are not adjacent but fused, and that the author wrote this line in her own hand while the remainder was taken down by another.

The reading was held in November, in the empty mill, because the deed ceremony required both families and neither would enter the other's house. I read the Sordano first, as the original. Then I read the English. When I reached the last line I said the valley word aloud—*la rògia*—before its translation, because a certified translation is a bridge, and a bridge has two ends.

Two old men in the second row stood up. Cousins, eighty-three and eighty-six, sons of Pietro and Aldo. They had not spoken since 1961. The younger one said the word after me, quietly, the way you say a word you heard your grandmother say while she stood at the door watching the water. Then the older one said it. Then, before I could be asked, the Menegus heir answered the De Zolt heir's question about what the word meant, and he answered it correctly, and they discovered they had each been told the same thing by different grandmothers.

The lawyers had drawn up two competing deeds. Neither was signed. The cousins asked the notary for a third, and what they signed instead restored the arrangement of 1612: the water held in common, alternating days, both family names on the mill, no line drawn between them. The hydro company, which needed a single owner, withdrew its offer. Nobody mourned it.

The lawyers asked me afterward how I had chosen between the families. I told them the truth: I did not choose between the families. I chose the word. An untranslatable term is not a wall; it is a door somebody bricked up on purpose, or by accident, and then argued about for sixty years. My work is not to knock the wall down. My work is to hand back the key and stand aside.

I kept both fees. I had translated one letter twice—once into English, and once out of the place where the two families had buried it.
