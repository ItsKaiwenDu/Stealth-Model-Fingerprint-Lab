**The Chain Across the Spans**

The first to know were the men who could not cross. At half past four in the morning, in a fog thick enough to scoop, Walker Byrd — a drover who brought cattle over the river twice a week — rode his pony up the eastern approach and found a chain strung between iron posts, and beyond it a lantern burning on the bridge decking, and beyond the lantern nothing but fog and the sound of the river working below. He called out twice. Nobody answered. The lantern swayed a little in the wind, which meant somebody had hung it recently and carelessly, and that was the whole of what Walker Byrd knew.

By six, the chain was the best-known fact in the port, and everything else about it was unknown.

The knowledge traveled the way goods travel: by hand, unevenly, with breakage. At the fish market under the north wharf, a woman who sold kippers from a barrow said the bridge had been closed by order of the borough surveyor, because a crack had been found in the third pier. She knew this because her cousin's husband laid bricks for the waterworks and had heard it from a man at the pub. Her neighbor, who sold mussels and did not like her, said it was not a crack but a barge, sunk against the pier sometime in the night, and the chain was up until the wreck could be raised. The mussel-seller had it from a lighterman directly, or from someone who stood near a lighterman, and in either case it had the advantage of being about boats, which felt more likely on a river.

The lightermen themselves knew less than they were credited with. Two of them had rowed under the spans at dawn to check their moorings, and both reported the same thing: they had seen men on the bridge — four men, or possibly five — in dark coats, walking slowly and stopping at intervals, one of them carrying what looked like a long rod or a measuring stick. One of the lightermen swore the men had chalk marks on the stonework. The other swore they had not stopped at the piers at all but at the middle of the center span, which meant it was not the masonry that was wrong but the suspension chains, and everyone knew the chains had been a worry since the great frost, or since before that, or since they were hung.

At the harbor master's office, a clerk named Pell unlocked the door at seven and found three people already waiting, which had never happened in his eleven years. He had received no notice of any closure. There was nothing on his desk, nothing in the letter tray, and when he sent a boy to the telegraph office, the boy came back to say the instruments had carried nothing since the previous evening, which the telegraph clerk found strange but not unprecedented, since the line to the county seat had been unreliable all month. Pell told the waiting three that the matter was being looked into. He said it with the flat confidence of a man who has learned that the phrase buys two hours, and it bought him one and a half.

The two hours were spent badly. At Hendry's coffee house, where the carters gathered before the hauling began, the closure was already three different stories. The carters were the ones who suffered first and knew it: the ferry took wagons, but slowly, and only at certain hours, and the toll was doubled for freight. A carter named Moss, who had forty hundredweight of tile to move before noon, worked through the arithmetic on the back of a billhead and announced, to general agreement, that whatever the reason, it would have to be very good to cost him what it was costing him. A retired river policeman drinking coffee at the end of the bench said, quietly, that in his day a bridge went up overnight like that for one of two reasons: somebody had died on it, or somebody was expected to. Nobody asked him to explain the second reason, and he did not.

The ferryman, Oakes, had the best morning of his life and understood the least. He had been told nothing either, but the chain funneled every foot-passenger in the port onto his deck, and he took their pennies and their theories with equal good humor. By eight o'clock he had heard that the bridge was condemned, that it was being strengthened for a military crossing, that a man had hanged himself from the center span and the rope was still there, that the closure was a labor dispute, that the county had failed to pay the maintenance contractor, and that there was a body under the third pier — the same pier, the kipper-woman would have noted with satisfaction, that she had named at dawn. Oakes repeated none of these and believed none of them and was fairly sure, from the look of the chain, that whoever had strung it had done so in a hurry, with the posts already in place, which suggested to him that the closing had been planned before it was needed. But he kept this to himself, because a ferryman who speculates freely soon has no peace.

At the railway station, up the hill and away from the water, the news arrived late and distorted. A porter heard it from a newsboy, who had heard it from a fish porter, and by the time it reached the stationmaster's office the bridge was reported closed for a month. A commercial traveler bound for the far side — a tidy man with a leather sample case full of hardware — came to the booking window to ask whether his connection was affected, and was told, after some consultation, that the station had no official information but that he might try the ferry, or the low bridge four miles upstream, which everyone agreed was passable except the people who had actually driven it lately.

The officials, such as they were, spent the morning locating one another. Pell from the harbor office sent a note to the borough surveyor's office and got back a reply saying the surveyor was out of town until Friday. The police sergeant on the beat, drafted into questions at every corner, said only that he had no instructions, and this was true, and he repeated it so often that by ten o'clock half the port believed he had secret instructions. A man from the tollhouse came down to the wharf at half past nine to see the chain for himself, out of professional grievance, and stood before it in his gray coat with his hands behind his back like a mourner, while the crowd that had gathered watched him for a sign and got none.

By late morning the fog had lifted, and the bridge stood plainly visible — whole, level, ordinary, its chains bright with damp, its lantern still burning though daylight made it pointless. From the wharf you could see the four men, or possibly five, still walking the spans with their rod, stopping, measuring, writing in a book. Someone produced a spyglass, and a hundred people waited in the cold to be told what the spyglass would reveal, and it revealed men writing in a book, which was somehow more disturbing than a crack would have been.

The mussels were sold by then. The tile went over on the two o'clock ferry. The bridge stayed shut, and by evening the port knew everything about it except the reason.
