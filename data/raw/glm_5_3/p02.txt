# The Last Train to Somewhere Else

The station restaurant at midnight kept only two lights on: one over the counter and one over the table by the window, as if the place had already begun to forget itself. Nadia took the seat facing the platform so she could watch for her connection. Her brother Marco sat opposite, coat still buttoned, like a man who wanted to be able to leave at any moment.

The waiter brought two coffees neither of them had ordered fast enough to refuse. He set down a plate of biscotti with the weary ceremony of someone who had stopped pretending, decades ago, that anything here was fresh.

"So," Marco said.

"So."

The letter sat on the table between them. Sealed, yellowed at the folds, their father's handwriting on the front—both their names, joined by an ampersand, and beneath it, in the smaller script he'd used for anything serious: *Do not open. Burn unopened. Promise me.*

"You didn't burn it," Marco said.

"You didn't either."

"I was going to."

"When?"

He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn, then back. "I kept thinking there'd be a right moment. There's never a right moment for anything with him. Even now."

"He's been dead eight months, Marco."

"I know how long he's been dead."

The loudspeaker crackled something unintelligible about a delayed train. Out on the platform, a single traveler paced, dragging a suitcase on bad wheels.

"It was in the strongbox," Nadia said. "Under the deed to the house and the photograph of Mum. Taped to the back of the photograph, actually. Which means he wanted it found, or he wanted it hidden where only someone who went looking would find it. With him you could never tell which was which."

"Maybe both." Marco broke a biscotto in half and didn't eat either piece. "You went through the strongbox without me."

"I was clearing the house. You were in Berlin. You were always somewhere."

"There it is," he said softly.

"There what is?"

"Nothing. Forget it." He brushed crumbs off the table with the side of his hand. "The point is he told us not to open it. Explicitly. In writing. When has he ever asked us for anything in writing?"

"He never asked us for anything at all. That was the problem."

Nadia looked at the letter. Their father's hands had sealed that envelope. His thumb had pressed the flap down, probably licked it, probably gotten a paper cut, probably sworn in the particular way he swore, quietly, so no one would hear him lose at anything. Eight months dead and she could still hear him losing quietly all over the house.

"Open it," she said.

Marco went still. "You just said—"

"I know what I just said. I've been saying it to myself since March. Don't open it. He asked. It's the only thing he ever asked. And then I think: he asked because he knew we'd want to. He was managing us from beyond the—no. Not beyond anywhere. He was managing us from his desk, which is worse, because at least the dead have an excuse."

"He wasn't managing us."

"Marco. He wrote *promise me*. To both of us. Together. He never once asked us to do anything together while he was alive, and his last act was to make us co-conspirators in our own ignorance."

Marco smiled despite himself, a small crooked thing. "You always said his love came with conditions."

"No. I said his silence came with conditions. The love was—available. In the other room. You could visit it on Sundays."

The waiter drifted over, refilled their coffees from a pot that had been sitting too long, and drifted away. Neither of them touched the cups.

"You could have visited more," Marco said. He said it to the window.

"Careful."

"I'm just—"

"I know what you're just. You're just doing the thing. The thing where you leave for eleven years and then arrive at the funeral and then arrive tonight, at midnight, in a train station, and start auditing my Sundays."

"I called every month."

"You called every month," she repeated. "And I sat with him every month. Every week. Every Tuesday when he'd say 'you didn't have to come' in the exact tone that meant the opposite, or maybe meant nothing, or maybe meant come every day, who knows, because he would rather be carved open by wolves than say *I'm lonely, Nadia, the house is too big since your mother, please stay*."

She hadn't meant to say all of that. She watched Marco absorb it—watched him decide, visibly, not to use the opening. That was almost worse.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That it was heavy."

"It wasn't heavy. It was just... only mine. The way things got to be only mine."

"Because you made them only yours."

"Because you *left*."

The word sat there between the coffee cups like a third sibling. Marco picked up one half of his biscotto and finally ate it, slowly, buying himself the time. Out on the platform, the traveler with the suitcase gave up and sat down on a bench facing the wrong direction for the train.

"When I was nineteen," Marco said, "he found out I'd applied to the conservatory. Do you know what he did? He didn't yell. He took the acceptance letter—I'd left it on the kitchen table like an idiot—and he put it in the bin. Not even torn up. Just placed it under the peelings so it would go out with the compost quietly. That was his verdict. Compost." He laughed without any air in it. "So yes. I left. And no, I never once wondered why you stayed, because I knew. You stayed because you're stronger than me, or softer, or you loved him more, or he needed you more—I don't know which, Nadia, I honestly never worked out which."

"You could have asked."

"You could have asked me to stay."

The loudspeaker crackled again. Her connection, the voice seemed to say, was now the last train, now boarding in twenty minutes. Neither of them moved.

Nadia turned the letter over. On the back, nothing. Just the paper, thinning slightly at one corner where it had been handled—more than once, more than twice, over years. He had held this. He had taken it out and looked at it and put it back, the way she used to find him doing with the photograph of their mother, late, when he thought the house was asleep.

"He read it," she said quietly. "Or at least he held it. Look at the corner."

Marco leaned in, and for a moment they were children again, heads bent over the same small mystery, shoulder to shoulder over a beetle or a broken watch.

"Or he wrote it and reread it before he sealed it," Marco said.

"Nobody rereads a letter they're ordering burned."

"People reread letters they're *afraid* of."

That stopped her. She sat back.

"Afraid of what?" she said. "Afraid we'd open it, or afraid we wouldn't?"

Marco didn't answer. He was looking at her the way he used to look at a difficult passage of music, tilting his head, finding the fingering.

"Whatever's in it," he said, "it's either an apology or an explanation. Those are the only two letters men like him write and don't send. And if it's an apology, we don't need it anymore—we did the apologizing for him, both of us, to ourselves, for years. And if it's an explanation—" He stopped.

"If it's an explanation," Nadia said, "then something finally gets explained. And then it's over. All of it. Whatever's left of him that's still a question."

"So we keep it," Marco said. "Sealed."

"Forever?"

"For as long as we want him alive."

She understood then that they were not talking about the letter, and they had not been talking about the letter for some time, and possibly they had never once, in their entire lives, been talking about the letter.

The loudspeaker announced the last train. Nadia picked up the envelope, and instead of opening it, instead of pocketing it, she held it out across the table.

"You keep it," she said.

"Me?"

"You're the one who left. So it lives with you. And that means—" her voice caught, and she let it, because she was done with the quiet losing— "that means you have to come back to give it back. To me. Or to burn it with me. Whenever we decide. Together. That's the condition, Marco. His love came with conditions; mine comes with a train schedule."

Marco took the letter. For a second his hand closed over hers on top of it and stayed there, briefly, awkwardly, the way their father had never once held either of them, and the way they had both, all these years, gone on quietly losing for want of.

"The eleven-forty," he said. "There's another one at six."

"Then walk me to the platform," she said. "You can tell me about the conservatory. All of it. Start from the bin."

He did.
