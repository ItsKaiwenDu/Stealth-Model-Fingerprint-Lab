# The Honest Pane

Dr. Iris Calloway arrived at the Halloway Conservatory at eleven-forty in the morning, which she considered the professionally correct time: late enough to seem unhurried, early enough to see the thing happen.

Ellis Barrow, the curator, met her at the staff door with the handshake of a man who hadn't slept properly in a month. "You're the fourth person I've called," he said. "The agronomist took soil samples. The engineer checked for gas leaks. The retired fellow from the university wanted to talk about ley lines."

"And you want a botanist."

"I want a botanist who won't laugh at me."

Inside, the glasshouse was a long cathedral of light, humid and green, benches of geraniums and citrus and orchids marching toward the far end. Calloway didn't need to be shown the phenomenon. She could see it from the door. Every plant in the building leaned — tomatoes, fuchsias, the ferns hanging in baskets, even the little forest of seedlings on the propagation bench — all tilted, gently but unanimously, toward the northeast corner.

The northeast corner held a drain, an empty bench, and nothing else.

"They started in April," Barrow said. "A few degrees off vertical. Now some of them are practically lying down. And it's worse at noon. Watch."

She watched. As the sun climbed toward its highest point, the lean didn't swing with it. Plants track the sun — every botanist knows heliotropic sunflowers turning east to west — but these plants ignored the noon sun, which stood squarely in the southern sky, and bent harder toward the empty north corner. At 12:15 it was at its most pronounced. By one o'clock, it had relaxed, though never fully upright.

"The university man," Barrow said, "says plants don't have memory. But this corner — Sam used to start seedlings there. With grow lamps. Twenty years of seedlings under lamps. Maybe they learned it. Maybe they're waiting for the lamps."

That was the story everyone at the conservatory told, and Calloway could see why. It had the shape of an explanation. It was also, she was fairly sure, wrong — because she stopped at the propagation bench and looked at the flat of basil sown nine days ago. Nine-day-old basil had never seen Sam's lamps, never met the corner, held no grudge and no hope. Every seedling leaned northeast like the rest.

"Your new seedlings lean too," she said.

Barrow stared at them as though they'd personally betrayed him.

She spent the afternoon doing unglamorous things. She checked the irrigation lines for uneven watering — leaning can be a water-seeking response — and found them even. She measured the temperature at a dozen points; the corner was slightly cooler than the south wall, which ruled out a hidden heat source. She looked at the corner itself: drain, empty bench, gravel floor, and above it, high up in the ridge of the roof, a run of glass panes like all the other glass panes.

Her second clue came from a ladder.

The conservatory owned one ladder, an aluminum extension model, and Calloway noticed it wasn't tall enough to reach the ridge panes — not by a good eight feet. In the supply closet she found half a dozen gallons of shading compound, the milky spray-on stuff glasshouses use in summer to cut heat and light, and a sprayer whose wand was bent from years of reaching upward. On the wall hung a work calendar in the head grower's handwriting. Every May for the past decade it said the same thing: *spray shade, whole house.* Except this May, where it said *spray shade, benches + south + west.*

"Ferro," she called, and the head grower emerged from behind the citrus. He was sixty-ish, sun-cured, with the wary posture of a man expecting an accusation.

"Sam Ferro," he said. "You're the botanist."

"Why does the calendar say you only sprayed part of the house this year?"

"Because the scaffolding company took back their tower. Budget. It was here in March for the repointing — we'd rent it every few years, spray the high panes from the platform. This year the board cut the line item. You try spraying a ridge pane twenty-two feet up with a bent wand."

She looked up at the roof. The glass overhead was faintly, uniformly grayish — old, weathered, coated with a fine etching of a century of grime and last year's washed-out shade compound. All of it looked the same to the naked eye. That was the trouble with glass. It lies by omission.

Her third clue she nearly stepped on. Crossing the gravel near the empty bench, her boot crunched on something that wasn't gravel. She crouched. Wedged between the stones, half-buried, was a triangular shard of glass — but not clear glass. This shard was *figured* glass, patterned with a fern-and-diamond texture, the kind used in the grand glasshouses of the 1900s to diffuse light. She held it up to the sun and the light came through scattered, soft, broken into a hundred small glows.

Calloway looked up at the ridge again. Every pane up there, as far as she could tell from below, was flat modern glass — except none of them were, because this shard had to come from somewhere.

At eleven-forty the next day she came back with a garden hose.

Barrow and Ferro watched her drag it down the center aisle and turn the nozzle to a fine mist. She started at the south wall and worked her way along, spraying the glass overhead. Where the water hit, the panes ran white — milky rivulets, the old shading compound reactivating and dripping, the glass underneath diffused and figured and dim even when wet. Pane after pane, wall after wall, the whole house wept white.

Then she aimed the hose at the northeast ridge, above the empty corner.

The water ran off those panes *clear*. Perfectly, conspicuously clear — sheeting down like water off a new windshield, no milk, no residue, nothing to soften what came through. And through those four panes, at just past noon, the sun fell in a hard, clean shaft that reached deeper into the house than any other light in the building — a bright stripe that landed, of course, on the plants nearest the corner, and scattered onward through the leaves.

There it was. The whole mystery in one stripe of honest light.

"Oh," Barrow said quietly.

Ferro had gone very still.

Calloway kept the hose on the pane. "Every original pane in this house is figured glass," she said, "and every one of them has been sprayed with shading compound every summer since before I was born. The light in here is diffused on purpose — soft, scattered, even. Those are growing conditions. But figured glass diffuses, and shade compound cuts, and a plain pane of modern float glass does neither." She let the water fall. "How long ago was it replaced?"

Ferro exhaled like a man setting down something heavy. He didn't answer with words. He walked to the back shed, and they followed, and he pulled a burlap bundle off a shelf and unwrapped it on the potting bench: three large pieces of figured glass, fern-and-diamond pattern, their edges clean breaks.

"February," he said finally. "Ice storm. I was up on the tower re-running the vent cable before the scaffolding went back, and I kicked a rung and the pane went. I had stock glass in the shed from the cold-frame repair, so I put one in myself before the frost got into the house. Figured glass you can't get anymore without custom orders, and custom orders go through the board, and the board asks how the pane broke." He rubbed his thumb over the old glass. "I was going to shade it in May like always. Then the tower went back to the rental yard."

"So for one summer," Calloway said, "out of a hundred summers, this house has one pane of clear, unshaded glass — at the ridge, angled so that at solar noon the sun comes straight through it. The rest of the roof gives these plants soft, diffuse light. That corner gives them a spotlight. Plants don't bend toward corners, Mr. Barrow. They bend toward the brightest light they can find, and they'll cross diffuse light to reach direct light every time. Your tomatoes aren't remembering Sam's lamps. They're doing arithmetic."

"But the corner's empty," Barrow said.

"The bench is empty. The light isn't. It just doesn't show up on an inventory."

The fix took a week. Ferro ordered the custom figured glass, openly, with a confession attached, and the board — to everyone's relief — was more upset about the scaffolding line item than the pane. They sprayed the new shade compound over everything, corner included, before the replacement even arrived.

By September, the new growth on the geraniums stood straight as soldiers. The old stems kept their lean — stems are history, written in cellulose — and Barrow left them that way, propped and tilted, all pointing at the empty corner like witnesses.

Calloway came back once, in October, at noon. The light through the ridge was soft and gray and evenly scattered everywhere, and the plants, receiving no instructions from anywhere in particular, simply grew upward, the way plants do when nobody's broken anything.
