# Mean Time

The clock on the market tower at Skerrin lost time in only one way. Not the slow bleed of a worn escapement, not the sulking of damp weather—I knew those losses and could forgive them. This was different. A clean loss, always the same. Eleven seconds.

Every noon I stood on the tower steps with the box chronometer in its oilcloth and waited for the wireless signal. When the signal came and the tower clock arrived at twelve eleven seconds late, I climbed back up and made an entry in the ledger. My father kept the ledger before me, and his father before him, back to 1902. Each entry gave the date and the hour of loss, and then, weeks or years later, in smaller hand, the thing it had matched. Eleven seconds on a March afternoon in 1931; the vote to darken the light at Crag Point. Eleven seconds at half past four in 1958; two days afterward, the Cannery Road deed was signed. My father used to say eleven seconds was how long a man holds his breath over a signature. I never learned whether he had timed it or invented it.

The rule I was raised on was simple. The loss followed the decision, the way a wake follows a boat. You heard the hitch in the escapement, you waited, you found out.

My father's last years, his hands went bad, and I came home to do the winding. I had an offer then from the chronometer works at Bleymard and Sons in the city, and I did not answer the letter, and he never mentioned it, and the matter closed itself the way such matters do. After his funeral I found a ferry ticket folded into the back cover of the ledger. Second class, single passage, the ninth of June—the June I came home. I assumed he had bought it for me, to make my leaving easy, and that my staying had made it useless. I never unfolded it all the way.

On his final page, beside the last entry—eleven seconds, the sixth of June, at nine in the evening—there was one word in the margin: *before*. I took it for the start of a sentence he hadn't the strength to finish.

---

That spring the Meridian Company came down from the city. Mr. Vane stood in the hall with his maps and offered a fifty-year lease on the harbor front, and the council put it to a referendum, and the town divided itself politely, the way a family divides over a will.

And the losses changed.

The first I noticed was a hitch at four forty-one on a Tuesday in April. I entered it and waited. On the Friday, Aldo sold the *Antigone*. I went down to the slip where he was coiling rope and asked him when he had decided. He thought about it longer than the question deserved.

"Tuesday," he said. Then, "No. Tuesday I only said it out loud."

"How long had you known?"

He looked at his hands. "Longer than that."

After that I kept the ledger differently. Every hitch went down with its hour, and instead of searching backward through the days for its decision, I waited forward, and forward always paid out. Eleven seconds on a Thursday; the following week, the harbormaster's enrollment papers for his daughter, inland. Eleven seconds on a Sunday, when the town was in church or in bed; four days later the priest withdrew his objection to the roof fund. The clock was not behind the town's decisions. It was ahead of them.

I could not tell you what that means, and I distrust anyone who can. Perhaps people decide long before they let themselves know it, and the clock reads the deciding rather than the telling. Perhaps regret travels backward, arriving like thunder after lightning reversed. Perhaps a brass movement in a salt wind simply does things no one can explain, and I had spent too many years alone with it.

The council noticed the tower running minutes slow and sent for a clockmaker from the city. He had the works apart on my bench for two days, cleaned and oiled everything, and found nothing. "It's a sound machine," he told me, mildly offended that I'd implied otherwise. In his presence it lost nothing at all. He left on the noon ferry, and by Friday there had been three more hitches, and I stopped correcting the hands. The losses were a record. I did not see why a record should be erased to keep the ferry company's timetable tidy.

The tower fell behind. The fishermen grumbled. I received a letter from the council on official paper, and I answered it with one sentence: the movement is sound. In the third week of May I counted forty-one hitches, and I looked up the number of registered voters, which was six hundred and eleven, and I did the arithmetic, and then I shut the book and went down and scrubbed the tower steps, which did not need scrubbing.

I tried asking people, obliquely, the way you ask about money. Whether they had decided anything on such-and-such an afternoon. The harbormaster's wife asked me what business it was of mine, and she was right that it was none, and wrong about everything else, and I stopped asking.

---

On the night of the referendum I went up with the ledger under my arm and sat on the stool my father had kept, because his stool was better than mine and he was past needing it. Below, the hall was lit, and the queue went out the door and along the quay, and I could see Mr. Vane's car at the corner with its city plates.

I took out the ticket. I don't know why that night, except that the ledger was open on my knees and my hands wanted something to do. I unfolded it all the way for the first time, past the old crease he had pressed into it, and there in the clerk's faded ink was the passenger's name.

Edwin Fenn. One passenger. My father's own name.

I sat with that a while. Then I turned to his final page. The sixth of June, at nine in the evening: eleven seconds lost. The ticket was dated the ninth. Three days between the hitch and the sailing, and he had never boarded, and after that entry there were no more entries in his hand. I had always believed the ledger stopped because his hands failed him. The ticket was for the week I came home. He had bought his own leaving, and I had come up the tower stairs with my suitcase, and he had put the ticket in the ledger and closed it, and wound the clock the next morning at seven, and every morning after, for years.

The regret had come first. Three days before any decision there was to regret. And the decision itself never visibly arrived at all—it only failed to happen, the way weather fails to happen, and the clock had counted it anyway, and he had written one word in the margin to say so. Not the start of a sentence. A finding.

I was still sitting there when the hall below went quiet for the count.

The escapement hitched.

I put my palm flat on the case and counted the missing ticks. Eleven. Somewhere below me—the clerk with the tally, Vane at his table, Aldo, the harbormaster's daughter, the whole lit room of them—someone had decided something, or was about to be told they had decided it long ago. It might have been the town's. It might have been mine. I noticed that I had been holding my breath, the length of a signature, and I could not have said over what.

The doors of the hall opened and the crowd came out onto the quay, and their voices came up the tower well to me, blurred and human. I opened the ledger to a fresh line and wrote the date and the hour, and left the third column blank. It would fill itself in, or it would not. In the margin, in a hand steadier than I expected, I copied his word, so that whoever kept the clock after me would have it, and would not have to spend thirty years deciding what it meant: *before*.

I put the ticket back where he had kept it, folded along his crease, and let my breath out.

I did not set the clock right. I have not set it right since. It runs slow behind us all, patient as a wake, and at noon I stand on the steps and listen to the signal and let the seconds go by uncounted, because I have learned what my father learned on the sixth of June, which is that the clock was never losing time at all. It was only refusing to run ahead of us into the part of ourselves we hadn't reached yet.
