When a city's water consumption jumps sharply and no burst main is flooding a street, the temptation is to assume the data are wrong or that the cause will reveal itself. Both assumptions are dangerous. A sudden rise in demand is a signal, and the correct response is to treat the investigation the way a physician treats an unexplained symptom: as a disciplined sequence of hypotheses tested in order of cost and plausibility, each step narrowing the space of explanations until only one survives.

The first task is not explanation but verification. Before hunting causes, the investigator should confirm that the phenomenon is real. This means comparing the period in question with the same weeks in previous years, correcting for weather, and checking whether the billing calendar contains an artifact, such as an extra reading day or a shift in the meter-reading route. It also means confirming that the production meters, the large instruments at the treatment plant or wellfields that measure water entering the system, have been calibrated recently. If the city is producing the same amount of water as always but "consuming" more, the problem may live in accounting rather than hydraulics. Conversely, if production has genuinely risen to meet real draw, the cause is physical, behavioral, or both. This single distinction, between a data problem and a water problem, is the first decision point, and everything downstream depends on it.

Assuming the signal is real, the second step is spatial decomposition. Most modern systems can be divided into district metered areas, zones fed by one or a few meters where inflow can be compared against billed consumption. The most valuable measurement in this entire field is minimum night flow, the rate of water entering a district between two and four in the morning, when almost everyone is asleep. Legitimate demand at that hour is close to zero, so elevated night flow is a strong indicator of leakage or of something continuously drawing water. If night flow is normal across all districts, the city probably does not have a hidden leak, however counterintuitive that feels, and the investigation should pivot away from pipes. If one district shows a spike, the suspect area has been reduced from hundreds of kilometers of main to a few blocks, and the classic tools apply: acoustic loggers that listen for the hiss of escaping water, step testing in which valves are closed section by section while watching the flowmeter fall, and pressure tests on isolated segments.

This is where hypotheses must be ranked honestly. A subsurface leak that drains directly into a storm sewer or a creek can lose enormous volumes without ever surfacing, so "no obvious leak" does not exclude leaks; it merely excludes visible ones. Fire hydrant use is another early candidate, since construction crews, street cleaners, and occasionally unauthorized users draw from hydrants without any record appearing in billing. A new large customer, a brewery, a data center with cooling demands, a bottling plant, should surface in the commercial billing data, which is why the billing ledger and the production telemetry must be reconciled over exactly the same window. Any gap between water produced and water billed is either leakage, unbilled municipal use, theft, or metering error, and that arithmetic gap is itself a measurement that disciplines the whole inquiry.

Controls matter as much as measurements. Comparing the anomalous month to the same month last year controls for season; comparing the affected districts to demographically similar unaffected ones controls for citywide behavior like a heat wave or a price change; and comparing billed volume to metered inflow in each district controls for the possibility that the meter is fine but the customer base genuinely changed. A good investigator also resists confirmation bias by deciding in advance what result would falsify a favored hypothesis. If you believe the answer is a leak, specify what night-flow values would rule it out, and accept that verdict when it arrives.

Here is where a disciplined process earns its keep, because two of the most common explanations initially look foolish. The first is that the city is consuming the water itself. Municipal operations, main flushing, reservoir cleaning, hydrant testing, a parks department valve stuck open on an irrigation line or splash pad, are notoriously poorly recorded, because the water department and its internal customers rarely bill each other. Suggesting that the utility's own maintenance schedule explains the spike sounds like blaming the telescope for the stars, yet in real investigations it happens with embarrassing frequency, usually because one division's routine work never reaches the division that compiles statistics.

The second foolish-sounding hypothesis is ghost flushing. A single toilet with a worn flapper valve can silently pass hundreds of liters a night, refilling a tank that slowly seeps away. Individually invisible and trivial, such leaks aggregate across tens of thousands of households into millions of gallons that look exactly like demand. Saying "the city's consumption rose because toilets are broken" invites ridicule, but it is testable: night-flow analysis in residential districts, combined with targeted customer surveys or subsidised flapper replacements in a pilot neighborhood, can confirm or kill the idea quickly. If a thousand repaired toilets in the pilot area reduce night flow measurably, scale the program.

The decision points, then, fall naturally into a sequence. First, is the anomaly real or an artifact? Second, does mass balance point to leakage, unbilled use, or genuine demand? Third, does night flow localize the problem? Fourth, once a cause is identified, a pilot intervention should be measured against control areas before the city commits money. Throughout, the investigator should publish the reasoning, because a transparent chain of hypotheses and results protects against both premature certainty and the tendency to keep testing until something convenient turns up.

The deeper lesson is methodological. Cities are systems where measurement, behavior, maintenance, and accounting intertwine, and an anomaly in one register may originate in another. The city that succeeds is not the one with the cleverest first guess but the one that lets cheap, decisive measurements eliminate possibilities in order, remains willing to test explanations that seem beneath its dignity, and treats the foolish hypothesis with the same respect as the plausible one until the data speak.
