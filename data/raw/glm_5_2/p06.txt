# The Bridge at Callersford

The fog had not yet burned off the wharves when the first men arrived for work and found the chain strung across the western approach to the Callersford bridge. It was a heavy thing, a ship's cable really, wrapped at intervals with sailcloth where it met the iron stanchions. A boy in a railway porter's coat stood beside it, and when the dock foreman, Aiken, asked him what it meant, the boy said he had been told to stand there and had not been told why.

Aiken walked back to the warehouse shed where his crew was assembling and told them the bridge was shut. He said it plainly, without embellishment, but someone asked whether there had been a collapse, and Aiken said he had not said that, and another man said he had heard the iron trusses had been groaning for a week, which was not true, or at least was not something Aiken had ever heard before this moment. The story moved through the shed like heat from a stove. By the time the men filed out to begin loading the coal barge, three of them were arguing about whether the center pier had cracked or whether the whole thing was precautionary, and none of them had been within fifty yards of it.

The bridge was the only crossing for four miles in either direction. It carried foot traffic, cart traffic, and a single tramline that ferried goods from the railhead on the west bank to the warehouse district on the east. Its closure meant that the cooper's shop on the west side could not receive its shipment of oak staves, that the ferry dock three streets south would be mobbed by nine o'clock, and that Mr. Pellow, who kept the chandlery at the foot of Bridge Street, would have to decide whether to open his shutters at all, since half his custom came from across the river.

Pellow opened his shutters. He could see the chain and the boy from his doorway, and he sent his assistant, a girl named Dorthe, to ask the boy directly what had happened. Dorthe came back and said the boy was new, probably not fifteen, and that he had been given a shilling and a flask of tea and told not to leave until relieved. She said a man in a town clerk's badge had posted a notice on the tollhouse, but it was pasted over an older notice and she could not read the new one without peeling back the edges, which she had not done.

By half past seven the ferry slip was crowded. The ferry could carry forty passengers and two carts per crossing, and the queue stretched past the sailmaker's shop. Among those waiting were two commercial travelers, a woman with a sewing machine crated in pine, a schoolmaster who should have been across the river twenty minutes ago, and a clerk from the assayer's office carrying a leather satchel of mineral samples. The commercial travelers had heard from a cart driver that the bridge had been condemned by a county engineer. The cart driver had heard it from a boy selling bread near the tollhouse, but the bread boy had actually said only that a man with a leather case had been seen walking the bridge deck the previous afternoon, tapping the railings with a small hammer.

The schoolmaster, whose name was Galbraith, attempted to restore order by organizing the queue. He was told, politely but firmly, to mind his own affairs by the woman with the sewing machine, who said she had been waiting since before the shops opened and did not need instruction from a man who could not even get to his own school on time. Galbraith retreated. He found a bench near the ferry office and sat watching the water, and when a dockworker sat beside him and asked if he knew anything about the bridge, Galbraith said he did not, and the dockworker nodded and said he had heard it was a problem with the south pier, which had been shifting since the spring flood.

This was plausible. The spring flood had been severe. The river had risen above the lower wharf stones and lapped at the warehouse floorboards, and several pilings had been displaced downstream. But the bridge piers were stone, cut granite set in hydraulic lime, and there had been no report of movement, no visible crack, no shimmer in the deck. The story of the shifting pier was new, invented or half-remembered in the confusion of the morning, and it spread through the ferry queue with the speed of something people wanted to believe, because if the pier was shifting, then the closure was sensible, and if it was sensible, it was temporary, and if it was temporary, then by afternoon everything would be as it had been.

At the customs house, Deputy Collector Sayers received a note from the harbor master's office stating only that the bridge would remain closed until further notice. There was no explanation, no projected duration. Sayers read the note twice, then called in his clerk and asked whether the tram company had been informed. The clerk did not know. Sayers told him to send word to the tram depot, and the clerk went, and on the way he passed the fish market, where he told the auctioneer, a man named Treweek, that the customs house had received official notice of the bridge closure. Treweek asked what the notice said, and the clerk, who was not a malicious man but who could not resist the weight of being the bearer of official information, said that it said there had been a structural concern raised. He did not say by whom. He did not say what kind. He used the word "structural," which was not in the note, and which carried with it the implication that something had been seen, assessed, and found wanting.

By nine o'clock the fish market had closed its morning auction, and the buyers carried the news back to their shops and stalls. By ten the story had grown a rivet, a cracked casting in the main truss, identified by an engineer from the city who had been brought in quietly the day before. By half past ten the engineer was from London, and the crack ran the full length of the western span, and the bridge might not reopen for a month.

None of this was true, or at least none of it could be verified, and several of the details contradicted each other outright. But the people of Callersford did not require verification. They required a story, and the story required a cause, and the cause required an authority, and so the morning built its own small architecture of explanation, jointed and braced like the bridge itself, and just as dependent on what could not be seen from below.

The boy at the chain drank his tea. The fog lifted. The ferry ran its crossings, and the queue shortened, and the coal barge was loaded, and the cooper sent a man to the ferry with a handcart for the staves. The bridge stood where it had always stood, closed and silent, its deck empty, its ironwork catching the late-morning sun, giving nothing back.
