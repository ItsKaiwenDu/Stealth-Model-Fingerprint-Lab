# The Benches

The bench in the hallway has a groove worn into the wood where people have rested their hands. If you sit at the left end and put your thumb in the groove, your fingers reach the underside, which is cool and unpainted and has a roughness like the inside of a cereal box.

The hallway smells like floor cleaner and the inside of a jacket pocket. There are doors on both sides, all closed, with little silver plates beside them that say things like *Division 7* and *Calendaring*. The plates have a film of something on them, and if you breathe close, they fog up and you can write in them with your finger, but it disappears fast.

Mom is in the room at the end of the hall. The door has a narrow window in it, but it's got that wire stuff inside the glass, and when you stand on tiptoe, you can only see shapes moving, the back of her head, the shoulder of the man in the blue suit who sat next to her in the waiting area and asked her name, and the shoulder of another man who came out of a different room and shook her hand too long. They all went in together and the door clicked shut and she said, "Five minutes, Bean. Stay where I can see you."

You can see her from the bench if you leave the door propped, but it closed on its own, so you can't.

The other woman is across the hall, leaning against the wall. She is wearing a yellow shirt and her hair is pulled back so tight it makes her eyebrows lift. She smiled when you first sat down, a fast smile that came and went. She keeps looking at her phone, then at the door at the end of the hall, then back at her phone. She has a folder with papers in it, and the papers have a pink edge, and she keeps tapping the folder against her thigh like she's keeping time.

It is ten-seventeen. You know this because there is a round clock above the water fountain, and because you have been sitting here since ten-oh-two, which is when they went in. Five minutes was the longest of the five minutes but now it has been longer than five minutes by a lot and you have counted the ceiling tiles, which are square with little holes in them, sixty-one of them in the part of the hall you can see from the bench.

A man comes out of Division 7. He is tall and he takes off his glasses and rubs his eyes with both hands, the way Dad does after the computer, and you think of Dad in his chair, in the apartment, the one with the kitchen that has the tiles that are sticky near the sink. He walks past you and goes outside through the big doors with the metal bar across the middle.

A different man comes out of the room at the end of the hall. Not the blue suit, not the other one. This one has a grey beard and a briefcase and he holds the door for someone and the woman in the yellow shirt straightens up and pushes off the wall and goes in. Before the door closes, you hear Mom say "well I don't think that's—" and then the door closes and the little window shows shapes again.

The clock says ten-twenty-four.

Inside your backpack there is a bag of pretzels and a Capri Sun and a coloring book where you have colored almost everything. The only thing left is a duck on page nine, and you don't have orange, only red and green and blue, so the duck is going to be blue. You already decided this at home. You leave it in the bag because the pretzels would make noise in the quiet hall, and the quiet feels like a thing you shouldn't break.

The bench creaks when you shift. Underneath, someone has stuck a piece of gum between the wood and the metal leg support. It is dry and hard and the color of nothing.

When the door opens again, it's the blue suit man. He is holding a phone to his ear and he says, "She'll need to see the amended terms before—" and then he sees you and turns slightly and says the rest quieter, walking toward the big doors. *Amended* is not a word you know. You think it might be like *a men's*, which made no sense when the doctor said it about Dad's knee.

The woman in the yellow shirt comes out, too, and she stands in the hall and looks at you. Then she does something strange. She sits down on the bench, on the other end, and folds her hands in her lap and looks at the doors of the room like she is waiting for them to say something. She smells like something floral and also like coffee.

"Are you bored?" she says.

You shake your head no.

"My daughter's about your age," she says. "She's at school right now." She says this like you might ask her a question, but you don't. You pull the backpack straps against your chest.

"She likes to draw," the woman says. "What do you like to do?"

You shrug because it is true that you don't know. You liked playing Uno with Dad on the floor of the apartment and you liked Mom making eggs at night when she thought you were asleep, but you don't say these things because they are private and because you don't know this woman and because she is sitting too close even though there is room on the bench.

She nods like you answered and she goes back inside.

Ten-thirty-one. Sixty-one ceiling tiles, and then sixty-one again, in case you missed one.

The drinking fountain has a button that is stiff, and the water comes out warm at first then colder. You drink and the water tastes like the metal spout. There's a drain and you can see down into it and there is a brown leaf or a piece of paper down there, stuck.

Inside the room, there is talking. You can hear it if you press your ear to the door, a low sound like the hum of a refrigerator but with words. The words are not words, from this side. Just the rhythm. Sometimes the rhythm goes fast, like arguing about something, and sometimes it slows down and that's when you know someone is listening.

Mom said bring the backpack because they might go somewhere after. She said it casually, like it was nothing, but she also packed the pretzels and the Capri Sun and the coloring book and a clean shirt folded in a plastic bag. When she packs a clean shirt, it means something is going to happen. She packed a clean shirt the morning they went to the apartment with the boxes, and a clean shirt the morning she went into the room with the woman at the desk who typed while Mom talked.

The hallway is getting fuller now. People standing in pairs, talking to each other in the lowered voices you hear in libraries and hospitals. A woman holds a baby, and the baby is sleeping against her shoulder with its mouth open. A man in a uniform that is not a police uniform comes out of a room and walks fast and a piece of paper falls from his folder, and he doesn't notice. It sits on the floor, face down, until someone else picks it up and takes it inside.

The blue suit man comes back. He goes into the room and doesn't close the door all the way this time. Through the gap you can see the back of a chair and a sliver of table. The table has papers spread on it. Mom's hands are on the table and she is looking at them. The yellow shirt woman is looking at the grey beard man and he is pointing at one of the papers.

"Let's just—" the blue suit man says, and the door closes.

You are eight and you know what the word "arrangement" means because Mom used it twice on the phone with Aunt Cara, and you know what "every other weekend" means because Marcus at school says it about his dad, and you know what "primary residence" means because it was on a paper you saw on the kitchen counter under the magnet shaped like a cat. You know these words but they don't connect yet. They are like puzzle pieces from different boxes that keep showing up in the same pile.

What you know is the bench is hard. What you know is the clock hand moves the same speed whether you watch it or not. What you know is Mom packed a clean shirt.

The door opens and Mom comes out and her face is the face she makes when she is trying not to make a face, the one where her mouth is a straight line and her eyes are dry and wide. She is holding a paper folded in thirds. The blue suit man is behind her, saying something about "filed by Friday," and she says, "Yes. Yes, I understand," and then she sees you and her face opens and the straight line becomes a shape you know, a shape that is trying to be a smile but doesn't have all the parts it needs yet.

She sits down next to you on the bench. The groove is under your thumb. She puts her hand on your knee. The paper is still folded in her lap.

"Still five minutes?" you say.

She does the straight-line mouth again, then the trying-to-smile. "Something like that."

The woman in the yellow shirt comes out of the room, too, and stands across the hall and looks at the two of you, Mom and you, on the bench. The woman's face has something in it, and you can't tell if it's anger or sadness or just the effort of standing still. She looks at you for a long time, and then she looks at Mom, and Mom looks at the floor, and then the woman walks to the big doors and pushes the metal bar and leaves.

Mom picks up your backpack for you. The zipper is stuck on a corner of the coloring book. She yanks it and the zipper gives and the teeth separate and then come back together. She puts it on your shoulder.

"We should get some lunch," she says. "What do you think? Somewhere nice."

"Where?"

"I don't know yet."

"Okay."

The hallway is quieter now. Some of the people have gone into rooms. Some have gone outside. The floor still shines, and the shine is the kind of shine that shows your shoes, but stretched, the shape wrong. The water fountain drips once. The clock says ten-forty-four.

"Bean," Mom says, and you turn and her face is very close, and she puts both hands on your shoulders, the backpack straps between her fingers. "We're going to be fine." She says it like she is telling herself, like the words are the clean shirt in the plastic bag, folded up for later, for when they're needed.

"Okay," you say again.

You walk toward the big doors. The metal bar is cold, and when you push, the outside air comes in and it smells like pavement and exhaust and a hot dog cart, and Mom holds the door and you go through, and it closes behind you with a sound like something clicking shut.
