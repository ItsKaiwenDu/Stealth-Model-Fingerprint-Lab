# The Letter in the Breast Pocket

The station restaurant smelled of boiled coffee and floor cleaner. A fluorescent tube above the counter flickered every few seconds, casting the room in a stuttering half-light. Outside the plate-glass windows, the London-to-Edinburgh service sat breathing diesel fumes, its doors closed against the cold. Twenty-three minutes until departure.

Nora took the seat facing the platform so she could watch the clock. Daniel sat across from her, still wearing his coat, the letter on the table between them like a third presence neither wanted to acknowledge.

A woman with tired eyes appeared beside them. "Kitchen's mostly closed. I can do toast, tea, a fried egg if you want."

"Two teas," Daniel said.

"Just tea for me," Nora said.

When the woman left, Daniel tapped the envelope once with his index finger. Cream-colored stock, their father's name in the return address of the nursing home, the seal unbroken. Three months since the funeral. Sixteen years since either of them had spoken to the man while the other one was in the room.

"You've had it since March," Nora said.

"Two months. He gave it to me in January. Told me to find you, said we had to read it together or not at all."

"That's dramatic."

"That's Dad."

Nora looked out the window. A conductor walked the length of the platform checking door seals, his hi-vis vest catching the overhead lights. In the reflection, she could see Daniel watching her.

"You could have written," she said.

"I could have."

"Or called."

"I could have done that too."

"You did neither."

Daniel spread his hands on the table. He'd aged in ways she hadn't expected. Not just the gray at his temples, which she'd predicted, but something around the jaw, something tightened. He looked like a man who ground his teeth.

"You left," he said. Quietly. Without accusation, or at least with the pretense of none.

"I was nineteen."

"I know how old you were."

The woman brought two mugs of tea, a little milk skin floating on the surface. Nora wrapped both hands around hers. The warmth felt like the only honest thing in the room.

"He knew what he was doing," Daniel said. "Giving us this. Making us sit together."

"He was always organizing things. Arranging people like furniture."

"You think he arranged us?"

Nora looked at the envelope. Their father's handwriting on the front read simply: *For Nora and Daniel. Do not open until you are together. Do not open until you are ready.*

"I think he liked the idea of us needing him," she said. "Even after."

"Even after what?"

She didn't answer. Daniel drank his tea and didn't press. This was the geography of their relationship—vast territories of silence bordered by brief skirmishes of words. The silence wasn't empty. It was full of the things they'd never been able to say to each other: that Nora had needed her brother to stay and he hadn't, that Daniel had needed his sister to understand why and she hadn't, that their mother's leaving had cracked them both open like ice and each had blamed the other for the cold that rushed in.

"What do you think is in it?" Nora asked.

"I don't know."

"You've thought about it."

"Of course I've thought about it."

"And?"

Daniel looked at her. "Maybe it's an apology. Maybe it's an explanation. Maybe it's the deed to a house I don't want." He paused. "Maybe it's just him being cruel. Leaving us one last thing we can't agree on."

Nora turned her mug. The tea was cooling.

"We could not open it," she said.

"We could."

"He said never to open it. We're already breaking that. Maybe the not-opening was the point."

"The point of what?"

"Of everything. Of him." She leaned forward. "He wanted us in the same room. We're in the same room. Maybe that's all he wanted. Maybe the letter is just a way of getting us here."

Daniel looked at her for a long time. "You're afraid of what it says."

"Don't tell me what I'm afraid of."

"You're afraid it'll say something that changes what you've been carrying around for twenty years. You've got your version. I've got mine. The letter picks a side."

"That's not what I—"

"Isn't it?"

The fluorescent tube buzzed and steadied. Somewhere on the platform, a man in a rail-worker's vest leaned against a pillar, checking his phone. The other table—seated earlier with a businessman eating toast—was empty now. Just crumbs on a laminated menu.

Nora said: "He called me. At the end."

Daniel's face did something she couldn't quite read.

"He called you," she repeated. "Not you. Me. I was the one who went to the hospital. I was the one who—" She stopped. Breathed. "He wanted to tell me something. He couldn't, by then. His words were gone. But he kept pointing at the drawer. I found the letter. I put it in his hands. He held it until the nurse took it."

"He told me you visited."

"Did he."

"He said you were brave."

Nora said nothing.

"He said that about you," Daniel continued. "Not about me. He never said that about me."

"Daniel—"

"I'm not asking for anything. I'm telling you what he said. He said you were brave and he said he was sorry and he said he should have written more letters and made fewer demands." Daniel's voice held steady but his thumb pressed hard against the tabletop. "He said all of that. And then he said he wanted you to have the letter because you'd know what to do with it."

"He didn't say that."

"He did."

"He would have told me himself."

"He couldn't. Nora—he couldn't."

The clock read nine minutes to departure. The diesel engine outside idled like something patient.

Nora pulled the envelope toward her. She held it in both hands. Light. A single sheet, maybe two. Their father's whole life, reduced to a weight you could barely feel.

"If I open it," she said, "whatever's in here—it replaces everything. Every story we've told ourselves. Every fight. Every Christmas we didn't come home."

"I know."

"If it's bad—"

"It won't be bad."

"You don't know that."

"I know it won't be worse than what we've been doing to each other."

She looked at him. He looked at her. Outside, the conductor blew two short blasts.

"We open it together," she said. "Whatever it says."

"Together."

She broke the seal. Inside, one sheet of paper, handwritten, dated the week before their father died. She read it. Then she handed it to Daniel.

He read it.

The woman came by. "Train's going soon. Anything else?"

"No," Nora said. "Thank you."

The woman cleared their mugs and left them alone with the letter and the silence and whatever it said, which was theirs now, and which neither of them would ever tell anyone.
