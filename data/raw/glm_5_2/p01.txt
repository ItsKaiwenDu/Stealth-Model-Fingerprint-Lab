# The Eleven Seconds

The first time it happened, Marta assumed it was the cold.

She had kept the town clock for seventeen years—the great four-faced clock in the square above the fish market, visible from the breakwater, the ferry terminal, and the streets where vendors laid out their silver-scaled catch each morning. She knew its temperament the way one knows an aging relative: which gears complained in January, which escapement needed oiling after the salt winds of March. She maintained it with a devotion that her ex-husband had once called clinical, which was perhaps why he was her ex-husband.

On a Tuesday in October, she climbed the interior stairwell at 7:00 a.m., as she did each morning, pocket watch in hand. The great clock read 6:59:49. Eleven seconds slow. She corrected it, made her notation in the logbook, and went about her day. A malfunction. A tremor in the mechanism. She examined the escape wheel, the pendulum, the gearing. Nothing.

The next morning, the same loss. Eleven seconds exactly.

She wrote in the logbook: *Cause unknown. Mechanism sound. Will monitor.*

By the third day, she had stopped sleeping well. She lay in her apartment above the hardware store and listened to the town breathe—the distant clank of boats against the pier, the wind wandering through the eucalyptus trees—and she thought about eleven seconds. Not ten. Not twelve. The specificity troubled her more than the loss itself. A machine degrades variably. A machine loses three seconds one day and fourteen the next. Eleven seconds, repeated like a heartbeat, was something else.

On the fourth day, she decided to watch.

She arrived at 6:30 a.m. and sat in the narrow room beneath the clock faces, where the gears turned with their slow, planetary patience. At 6:52, she heard it—a faint hitch, as if the mechanism had swallowed something. The pendulum completed its arc. The second hand, visible through the small inspection window, jumped backward. Not smoothly, not mechanically, but like a man stepping back from a curb. Then it resumed its forward motion, and the clock ran correctly, eleven seconds behind the truth.

She checked her watch. 6:59:49.

She climbed down and stood in the square. The fish market was opening. Old Jürgen was arranging wooden crates of mackerel beside his usual spot near the fountain. A woman Marta didn't recognize was speaking into her phone near the pharmacy, her face tight with something—determination, perhaps, or the precursor to tears. Across the square, two teenagers sat on the breakwater wall, sharing a cigarette and staring at the gray water.

Nothing had happened. No decision was made in that square between 6:30 and 7:00 that Marta could identify. No contract signed, no marriage ended, no lie spoken. The town had simply been waking up.

And yet the clock had flinched.

---

She began keeping two logs. The first was the official record: dates, times, corrections, mechanical notes. The second she kept in a separate notebook, hidden in the bottom of her tool kit. In it, she recorded everything she observed in the square during the minutes before each loss.

The losses were not daily. Some mornings the clock kept perfect time. Others, it lost eleven seconds twice before noon. She could not predict them. She could only witness them.

On October 14th, the clock lost eleven seconds at 9:17 a.m. At 9:19, a man Marta knew as the baker from Kliewer Street walked into the real estate office beside the pharmacy. She had seen him there before. He always went in smiling and came out looking like someone had described his own face to him.

On October 19th, the clock lost eleven seconds at 11:42 a.m. Marta was eating a bread roll on a bench near the fountain. She looked up and saw the woman from the pharmacy—the one with the phone—walking toward the harbor with a packed suitcase rolling behind her. The woman stopped at the edge of the pier, looked at the ferry, then turned around and walked back into town. The clock kept perfect time for the rest of the day.

The next morning, it lost eleven seconds at 6:48 a.m., before the square held anyone but Marta herself.

That was the one she couldn't explain. She had been alone, sitting in the gear room, drinking coffee from a thermos. No one in the square. No one visible from any of the four clock faces. And still: the hitch, the step back, the eleven seconds gone.

She wrote in her hidden notebook: *Was I the one who decided something?*

But she hadn't. She had been drinking coffee. She had been thinking about the mechanism, about the woman with the suitcase, about whether the baker would visit the real estate office again. Nothing. No decision. No regret. Not yet.

She crossed out the entry and wrote instead: *Something decided before the decider knows.*

---

She mentioned none of this to the town council. They cared about the clock the way they cared about the fountain and the cobblestones—as objects that either functioned or did not. When she requested funds to replace the escapement, they approved it without questions. She installed the new part on November 2nd. The clock ran perfectly for three days.

On November 5th, it lost eleven seconds.

She sat in the gear room and listened to the new escapement tick with its clean, factory-fresh voice, and she felt something she would not have called fear. She would have called it recognition. The mechanism was not the source. It had never been the source.

She began watching the square differently. Not for events, but for the moments before events—the half-second in which a hand reaches for a door, the intake of breath before a sentence, the way a person's weight shifts before they stand. She had always been a woman who understood delay: the delay between a gear's tooth and its mesh, between the pendulum's swing and the tick. Now she was watching for the delay between a thought and its consequence.

On November 12th, the clock lost eleven seconds at 3:30 p.m. Marta was in the square, buying tea from the vendor near the pier. She saw the loss indirectly—the tea vendor glanced up at the clock face and frowned, then looked at his own watch. Marta turned. A boy of perhaps twelve was standing at the edge of the breakwater, holding a wallet. He was looking at it with the particular stillness of someone making a calculation that would stay with them. Marta watched him open it, remove a folded bill, then hesitate. The hesitation lasted four or five seconds. Then he removed the bill, folded it into his pocket, and dropped the wallet into the water.

The clock had already lost its eleven seconds. The decision had not yet been made. The boy had only been standing there, holding the wallet, staring at it. Something had moved through him that neither he nor Marta could see, and the clock had registered it before his hand had reached his pocket.

Three weeks later, the boy's photograph appeared on the community board near the pharmacy—lost wallet, sentimental value, reward offered. Marta saw him occasionally after that, always checking the board, always looking at his own photograph with an expression she recognized. She had seen it on the baker's face. She had seen it on the woman with the suitcase, the day she turned back from the ferry.

---

By December, the losses were occurring four, five, six times a day. The clock was running minutes behind. The council called. Marta climbed the stairwell and corrected the time and told them she was investigating. She was. She had filled the hidden notebook with observations that described nothing, proved nothing, and explained nothing.

On the morning of December 18th, she arrived early and sat in the gear room with her coffee. It was still dark. The square was empty. The wind from the sea smelled of iron and distance.

At 6:14 a.m., the clock hitched and stepped back eleven seconds.

Marta set down her coffee. She had been thinking about the boy. She had been thinking about how his face would look in ten years, in twenty, when the wallet had become not a moment but a sentence he carried. She had been thinking about the baker, and the woman with the suitcase, and all the people whose small ruinous choices she had catalogued without ever speaking to them. She had been thinking about her ex-husband, who had left six years ago, and about the morning he had stood in their kitchen with a packed bag and asked her if there was anything she wanted to say, and she had said no, because she had believed it was the correct answer, the clean answer, the answer that kept the mechanism running.

The clock had already lost its eleven seconds.

She sat in the dark gear room and listened to the steady tick of the mechanism she had maintained for seventeen years, the mechanism she understood completely and could not explain. Outside, the town was waking. Boats clanked. Wind moved through the eucalyptus. Someone would soon walk into the square and make a decision that was already forming in the space behind their eyes, in the place where regret begins before the regretted act, and the clock would flinch, and Marta would write it down, and the clock would run eleven seconds behind the truth of what people do, which is always, always ahead of what they know.

She picked up her coffee. It had gone cold.

She drank it anyway.
