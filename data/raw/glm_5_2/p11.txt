TO: University Executive Cabinet and Library Steering Committee
FROM: Office of Student Affairs and Campus Services
DATE: October 24, 2023
SUBJECT: Feasibility and Recommendation for 24-Hour Library Access During Final Examinations

In response to recurring student government petitions and recent feedback regarding study space shortages, this memo evaluates keeping our campus library open continuously—twenty-four hours a day—during the final two weeks of the academic term. The following analysis considers learning benefits, staffing requirements, safety protocols, equity implications, and displaced costs associated with this initiative. Ultimately, I recommend that the university implement a strictly scoped overnight pilot program for the upcoming fall finals, contingent upon several explicit operational conditions.

Learning Benefits
Extending library hours enhances student learning outcomes during the semester's most high-stakes period. Final examinations often require prolonged study sessions, collaborative group review, and immediate access to physical reserve materials or late-night printing services. While students can study at home, the library provides a structured, academically focused environment that minimizes domestic distractions. For many students, the psychological association between the library and focused work fosters deeper concentration and productivity. Furthermore, overnight access accommodates non-traditional students who work during the day and only have late-night hours to dedicate to intensive academic review. By providing a dedicated space for these academic endeavors, the university signals a strong commitment to supporting students exactly when their academic stress and workload peak.

Staffing Considerations
Operating the library around the clock presents significant logistical and financial staffing challenges. Traditional librarian shifts cannot simply be extended without incurring steep overtime costs or burning out our professional staff. Therefore, a functional overnight model requires a tiered staffing approach. During the midnight to six a.m. window, professional reference librarians would not be required on-site. Instead, the library would be staffed by trained student workers who can manage circulation and basic technical support, overseen by a dedicated campus security officer. This model drastically reduces labor costs while still providing a necessary human presence. However, we must ensure that student workers are compensated fairly for the antisocial hours, which may require a shift premium to incentivize overnight shifts. The university must also coordinate with the registrar and academic departments to ensure that the library’s online databases and digital reference chats are supported by on-call staff, even if physical staff are limited.

Safety Concerns
Campus safety is the most critical factor in evaluating overnight library access. A deserted campus presents inherent risks, but an isolated library can also become a vulnerability if not properly managed. Keeping the building open requires coordination with Campus Safety to ensure regular patrols and a fixed security presence within the building. We must also consider the physical safety of students traveling to and from the library in the dark. Existing safety infrastructure, such as emergency blue-light phones and well-lit pathways, must be audited and upgraded if necessary. Additionally, the campus escort service must extend its hours to accommodate students leaving the library at three or four in the morning. By integrating security personnel directly into the library’s overnight operation, we can monitor building access, ensure only current students enter, and mitigate potential conflicts or emergencies, creating a secure environment rather than an unsupervised campus void.

Equity Implications
Extending library hours is fundamentally an issue of student equity. Not all members of our student body enjoy a quiet, private, or technologically equipped study space at home. Students living in shared dormitories, crowded apartments, or environments with unreliable internet access are heavily disadvantaged during finals week. The library acts as the great equalizer, providing reliable Wi-Fi, access to charging stations, and a quiet atmosphere regardless of a student’s socioeconomic background. Denying late-night access effectively penalizes students who lack the privilege of a dedicated home study environment. By keeping the library open, the university actively supports lower-income students and those from marginalized backgrounds who may rely entirely on campus facilities to achieve academic success. However, we must also ensure that the pilot program does not inadvertently create new inequities, such as pricing out student workers who cannot afford to work overnight due to local transportation limitations.

Displaced Costs
Beyond direct staffing and security expenses, the university must account for displaced and hidden costs associated with round-the-clock building operations. Keeping a large facility open twenty-four hours a day significantly increases utility expenses, particularly heating, cooling, and lighting. Janitorial services will also require schedule adjustments; cleaning large, populated spaces during finals week is disruptive, so custodial staff may need to work shifted hours, potentially incurring overtime. Furthermore, there is the issue of physical wear and tear on library infrastructure, as well as the phenomenon of students using the library as a temporary residence. Students may attempt to sleep in carrels, bringing in excessive personal belongings, which creates hygiene issues and disrupts the academic atmosphere. These displaced costs are not always immediately visible in a departmental budget but will impact facilities management and student affairs if not proactively addressed.

Pilot Evaluation
Before permanently adopting an overnight library schedule, the university must rigorously evaluate a pilot program. The evaluation should rely on both quantitative and qualitative metrics to determine viability and return on investment. Quantitatively, the library should track overnight gate counts, printing volume, and the frequency of security incidents. We must also track the direct costs associated with utilities, staffing, and custodial adjustments. Qualitatively, the Office of Student Affairs should distribute brief digital surveys to students exiting the library during the overnight hours, asking them to rate the usefulness of the access and the safety of the environment. A post-finals focus group, facilitated by the student government, can provide deeper context regarding whether the extended hours genuinely impacted academic performance or merely encouraged procrastination. Success should be defined by a threshold of consistent overnight utilization, zero major safety incidents, and positive qualitative feedback regarding equity and the study environment.

Recommendation and Conditions
Based on this analysis, I strongly recommend that the university implement an overnight library access pilot program during the upcoming fall finals week. However, this recommendation is explicitly contingent upon several strict operational conditions to ensure financial responsibility and campus safety. First, only the first and second floors of the library should remain open; the upper floors must be closed and secured to reduce custodial, lighting, and security footprints. Second, a uniformed security officer must be stationed at the main entrance for the duration of the overnight hours, funded jointly by the Library and Campus Safety budgets. Third, a strict "no sleeping" policy must be enforced by student staff and security to prevent the library from becoming a dormitory; students found sleeping must be asked to leave. Finally, the program must be strictly limited to a two-week pilot period, with a hard evaluation deadline in January to determine if the initiative will be repeated in the spring. Under these conditions, the university can safely and equitably support student learning without committing to an unsustainable, permanent operational expansion.
