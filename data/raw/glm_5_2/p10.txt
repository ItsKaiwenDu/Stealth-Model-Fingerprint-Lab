The Noonday Devotees

Dr. Elias Vance adjusted his wire-rimmed spectacles as he stepped into the humid, fragrant embrace of the Pendelton estate greenhouse. The glass structure was a masterwork of Victorian engineering retrofitted with modern climate control, a sprawling cathedral of foliage where exotic flora from three continents mingled in perpetual, climate-manipulated spring. At the far end, near the potting shed, Clara Pendelton waited, her silver hair pinned beneath a practical canvas hat.

"Thank you for coming on such short notice, Dr. Vance," Clara said, her voice tight with a gardener’s perpetual anxiety. "It started two weeks ago. Every single plant in here, regardless of species or natural inclination, bends toward that corner." She pointed a gloved finger toward the northwest corner of the greenhouse.

Elias walked down the central aisle, observing the phenomenon. It was bizarre. Mature Sago Palms, normally rigidly symmetrical, had fronds arching dramatically toward the empty corner. Hanging baskets of Boston ferns spilled disproportionately to one side, their fronds stretching like grasping fingers. A row of Phalaenopsis orchids, whose stems had been meticulously trained horizontally, were now twisting their new growth, straining upward and sideways toward the same invisible point in space. The corner itself was entirely empty, featuring only an old, weathered mahogany potting bench and a single, large panel of thick glass separating the interior from the outside grounds.

"It happens slowly," Clara explained, checking her gold pocket watch. "But by noon, they are all locked in place, leaning like pilgrims at a shrine. It’s almost unsettling. I’ve checked the soil, the pH, the fertilizer, and the watering schedule. Everything is perfectly balanced."

Elias began his investigation. Biology was rarely mystical; plants responded to physical stimuli. Gravitropism dictated root growth downward, phototropism dictated shoot growth toward light. The obvious, logical conclusion was that the plants were seeking light. But the greenhouse’s overhead grow-lights were uniformly distributed, humming with a steady, diffuse luminescence that left no shadows on the floor.

He approached the corner, stopping beside a large *Ficus lyrata*, or fiddle-leaf fig. Its massive, violin-shaped leaves were angled dramatically toward the corner panel. Elias reached out and touched one of the broad leaves. It was unexpectedly warm. In fact, it was hot to the touch, reminiscent of a pavement on a July afternoon.
