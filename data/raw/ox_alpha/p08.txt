# The Long Bench

The bench was made of wood the color of honey, and Nora had already learned not to slide on it because the third slat from the left had a rough spot that caught her tights and made a little pill of thread she could pick at with her fingernail. She picked at it now. It came off clean, a tiny gray curl, and she held it between her finger and thumb for a while before letting it fall to the floor, where it joined two other curls she'd made that morning.

The corridor smelled like floor polish and someone's cold coffee. The coffee smell came from a paper cup balanced on the windowsill across from her, next to a potted plant whose leaves were dusty on top but shiny underneath. The plant had been turned so its good side faced out. Nora thought about how the leaves must feel about that, getting light only from one direction, and then decided plants probably didn't have feelings about anything, which was what her teacher called anthropomorphizing, and you weren't supposed to do it in your science journal.

Down the hall there were three doors. Two had small rectangular windows in them, dark glass, and one didn't. The one without the window was where her mother had gone twenty minutes ago, or maybe thirty. Time in the courthouse moved differently than time at school. At school you could hear the clock over the intercom buzz at the ten-minutes-past mark, and everyone shuffled their feet at once. Here there was no buzzing. There was only a water fountain at the far end that made a sound like a long sigh every time someone used it, and the squeak of shoes, and the low voices coming through the door without the window.

She couldn't hear the words, exactly. She could hear her mother's voice going up and down, up and down, and another voice under it, smoother, a man's voice, though it wasn't her father's voice because her father had come through the other door an hour ago wearing the tie with the tiny sailboats on it, the one he only wore to weddings and funerals, and he'd crouched down and said, "Be a big girl, Nor," and squeezed her shoulders twice, which was their signal, and then he'd gone into one of the doors with the dark glass and hadn't come back out.

There was a woman behind a counter at the end of the hall. She had reading glasses on a beaded chain and she kept looking at papers and stamping things. The stamp made a sound like a bird hitting a window — thump — and each time she stamped something she said nothing at all, just moved the paper to a new pile. Nora counted eleven stamps. Then she lost count because a man in a suit came through the double doors at the entrance, talking loudly into his phone about a deposition, whatever that was, saying "I told him, I told him flat out," and his voice bounced off the high ceiling and made Nora feel like she was inside a bell.

On the wall opposite the bench there was a framed picture of a building that wasn't this building. It looked older, with columns, and underneath it said something in letters too small to read from where she sat. She got up once, walked the length of the corridor as quietly as she could, and read it: *Dedicated 1934.* That was all. A whole building, and all they wrote was the year. She stood in front of the picture for a moment, close enough to see the frame was gold paint over something cheaper, the corners worn down to gray, and then went back to her slat.

Her grandmother had tried to explain things last night, in the kitchen, while washing dishes even though the dishwasher worked fine. Grandma washed dishes when she was thinking hard. She'd said, "Tomorrow some people are going to talk, and they're going to decide things, and your mom and dad both love you very much." She'd said it like that, all in one breath, like the sentence was a rope bridge and if she stopped in the middle it would swing. Nora had asked what things. Grandma had said, arrangements. Nora had asked what arrangements meant. Grandma had handed her a towel and said dry these.

So now Nora sat on the bench and listened to the arrangement being made. The man's voice under her mother's voice rose once, briefly, and she heard a single word come through the door clear as a coin dropping: *alternating*. Her mother's voice answered, lower, and then there was a long quiet, longer than any quiet so far, and during the quiet Nora noticed that her own feet didn't reach the floor. She let them dangle. She swung them once, stopped herself, and put them still.

A boy came out of the door with the dark glass. He was older than her, maybe twelve, and he had a phone he wasn't looking at, and he sat down at the far end of the bench without acknowledging her, which was fine, because she didn't want to acknowledge anyone either. They sat like that for a while, two kids on a long bench, and then the boy's name was called by the woman at the counter — "Daniel Ferro?" — and he got up and went into the middle door and the door closed behind him with a click like a seatbelt.

Nora practiced being a big girl. Being a big girl meant sitting still. Being a big girl meant not asking the woman at the counter when Mom would be done, even though the woman looked nice and had a jar of peppermints on her desk, visible now that Nora leaned forward slightly — a glass jar, red-and-white swirls, maybe forty of them. Being a big girl also meant not crying in the car on the way here, which she had managed, barely, by looking at license plates and making words out of the letters, and she was a little proud of that even though pride felt strange in her chest, mixed in with everything else like raisins in oatmeal.

The door opened. Not her mother's door — her father's. He came out with the lawyer, a tall woman with a folder, and the two of them stood near the fountain and talked in real voices, actual words Nora could hear if she strained, and she did strain, and caught pieces. "Not unreasonable." "The school thing is the sticking point." "She'll adjust, kids adjust." Kids adjust. Nora turned the phrase over. It sounded like something you'd say about a fish being moved to a new tank. She looked down at her hands, at the hangnail on her index finger she'd been working on, and decided she did not want to be described as adjusting by the woman with the folder, and then realized nobody knew she could hear, and that this was a secret, and that secrets in courthouses were apparently allowed.

Her father glanced over at her. He raised his eyebrows in a question — okay? — and she nodded, and gave him the smallest smile she owned, and he smiled back, relieved, and went back to talking about the school thing.

Then her mother's door opened, finally, and her mother came out alone first, and stood in the corridor looking at the floor, pressing her fingertips together like she was holding something invisible between her palms. Behind her, the man's voice continued inside the room, addressing someone else now. Her mother saw Nora and crossed the hallway fast and sat down and pulled her close, smelling like her winter coat even though it wasn't winter anymore, and said into her hair, "Hi, baby. You were so patient."

"Did you decide?" Nora asked.

Her mother was quiet. Through the open door, the man's voice was saying something about dates, specific ones, months, and her mother's arms tightened around her, not painfully, just firmly, like a lid settling onto a jar.

"We decided some things," her mother said. "We're still deciding others."

"Oh," said Nora, who had picked the last pill off the bench slat hours ago and had nothing left between her fingers. She reached for her mother's hand instead and found it cold and laced her fingers through anyway, and they sat together on the bench, the two of them, waiting to find out what had been arranged.
