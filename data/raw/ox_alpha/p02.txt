# The Last Order

The station restaurant closed at eleven, but the woman behind the counter had stopped asking anyone to leave an hour ago. There were only two customers left: a man in a rumpled overcoat at the window table, and his sister across from him, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't touched since it went cold.

Between them on the formica surface sat a yellow envelope, sealed, softened at the corners by years of handling. Their father's handwriting on the front read only: *For both of you. Together.*

"You could just take it," Martin said. "You live closer to the family solicitor. You'd see this through properly."

"I'm not taking it alone."

"It's not a bomb, Ruth."

"No," she said. "It's worse. It's a decision."

The kitchen door swung and the waitress came out with two plates she'd kept warm out of pity or boredom—eggs, toast gone pale, a rasher of bacon each that neither of them had ordered. She set them down without comment and retreated. The fluorescent light above their table buzzed at a frequency that made Ruth's teeth ache, or perhaps that was something else.

Martin cut into his eggs. "He wrote it eight years ago. Before the diagnosis even. I found it when we cleared the flat."

"I know when you found it. You called me from the hallway while I was still standing at the graveside."

"That was a bad call. Timing-wise."

"Timing-wise," Ruth repeated. She finally drank the cold tea and grimaced. "Eight years he sat on this. Eight years you sat on it. And now it has to be tonight?"

"The solicitor wants it Monday. He needs to know whether it goes in the estate file or—" Martin gestured vaguely, knife hovering, "or wherever things go when they're never opened."

"Burned."

"Don't say it like that."

"How should I say it? We burn our father's last words because he told us to, and you want me to phrase it gently?"

A train announced itself over the tannoy—a delayed service to Carlisle, platform four—and neither of them moved. They'd been like this for three hours now. Arriving separately, as arranged by the solicitor, as though they were strangers negotiating a handover. In a way, they were.

Ruth turned the envelope over. Her thumb traced the seal without breaking it. "Do you remember when he caught us reading his letters from Mum?"

"I was nine."

"He didn't shout. That was the frightening thing. He just took them back and put them in the drawer and said, some things are mine to carry." She looked up. "And then he carried them for forty years and told nobody anything."

"So you think we should honor that."

"I think he taught us exactly one lesson about privacy and we're considering failing it."

Martin set down his fork. Through the window, a red signal changed somewhere far down the line, invisible but implied, the whole dark countryside organized around small lights telling people when to stop.

"When did you last speak to him?" he asked.

"You know when."

"Before or after my birthday?"

Ruth's jaw tightened. "Don't."

"Because if it was before, then the last three years of his life—the nursing home, the good days, the day he remembered the song from the wedding—you weren't there for any of it. And I've wondered ever since whether this envelope is really about his privacy or about yours."

The bacon congealed on both plates. Somewhere behind the counter a radio played very low, a station playing to no one.

"You want to know why I stopped coming," Ruth said quietly.

"I want to understand why I got eleven years of being the son who showed up and you got to be the one who was wronged. Because that's how he told it. Every time. *Your sister's got her reasons.* Reasons. Eleven years of reasons."

"My reasons were about you."

Martin blinked. "Me."

"You don't get to look surprised. You know what you did."

"I genuinely don't, Ruth."

She laughed then, a short bitter sound with nothing in it. "Of course you don't. That's the thing about you. Things happen around you and none of it lands. When Dad had the first fall and I said we should move him near me, you said fine, and then you moved him into Fernleigh instead, twenty minutes from your house, because it was easier for your visits. And I drove four hours every other weekend for two years until I couldn't anymore, and when I couldn't anymore, I became the one with reasons."

"Fernleigh was better care—"

"It was closer care. For you." She pressed her palm flat on the table. "And then when I said so at Easter, you told Dad I was being dramatic, and he believed you, because he always believed you, and I thought—I actually thought, sitting in that garden—if I never come back here, will anyone notice which chair is empty?"

The tannoy crackled again. Platform four. Nobody came to eat the cooling eggs.

Martin was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had lost its lawyerly steadiness. "I moved him to Fernleigh because I couldn't do what you did. Four hours each way, every fortnight. I knew I'd fail at that, so I built something smaller I could succeed at, and then I let him believe it was virtue instead of limitation. And he liked the story where I was the reliable one. So yes. I let him tell it." He rubbed his eyes. "Eleven years, Ruth. I thought you left because of the money. The loan I took against the shop. I've spent eleven years thinking you disowned me over twelve thousand pounds."

Ruth stared at him. "What loan?"

"The shop. Two thousand and fourteen. I borrowed against it to cover the care top-up fees before we sold the house. I told you in an email."

"I never got an email."

"There was an email. March. You replied, 'Noted.'" 

"Noted?" She pulled her phone from her bag, scrolled, and turned it toward him. Her sent folder ended in 2014. "I changed providers that spring. Everything before that went into an archive I've never opened. Noted must have been—" She stopped. "That wasn't about the money. That was about Fernleigh. I was acknowledging the argument was over."

They looked at each other across the ruined breakfasts, across eleven years assembled from two incomplete sets of records, each of them having lived inside a version of events the other had never seen. Outside, a freight train passed without slowing, hauling whatever it hauled through the dark.

"So the letter," Martin said at last.

"So the letter."

"Do we open it?"

Ruth picked up the envelope again and held it to the fluorescent light, as if the paper might turn translucent enough to show its intentions. It stayed stubbornly opaque.

"He said together," she said. "That's the instruction. Not never. He said *never* once, thirty years ago, and then he sealed this and wrote *together*, which means he changed his mind and wanted us to change ours. Or it means it's a trap. A final test of obedience."

"Dad didn't set traps."

"He set nothing else."

The waitress emerged again, keys jingling, and stood by the counter with the apologetic posture of someone who needed her restaurant back. "Last train's gone through," she offered. "You two waiting for the morning one?"

Neither answered immediately. Then Ruth said, "Could we have two more teas? Hot ones, this time. We'll be quick."

The waitress nodded and filled the kettle, and the sound of water heating filled the silence the siblings left deliberately empty, because some things needed the space of ordinary noise around them.

"If we open it," Martin said slowly, "and it's terrible—some confession, some name, something that makes him smaller than the man we buried—we can't unknow it. But if we burn it, we spend the rest of our lives writing it ourselves. And look how well we write."

Ruth almost smiled. "We'd make a mess of it."

"A catastrophe."

She held the envelope across the table. "Then together. Like it says. You hold one corner and I hold the other, and whatever falls out of it, we read it right here, under this awful light, and we don't leave until we've finished the tea."

Martin took his corner. His hands weren't steady, and he didn't pretend otherwise.

"For the record," he said, "I'm glad it was you at this table. Whoever else it might have been."

"It was always going to be us," Ruth said. "That was the whole problem."

The kettle began to sing behind the counter, and together, as instructed, they broke the seal.
