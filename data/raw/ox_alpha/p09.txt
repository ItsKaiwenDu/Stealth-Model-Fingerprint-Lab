Every human artifact that tells someone what to do faces the same underlying problem: reality is infinite in its detail, while any instruction must be finite. A map cannot record every pebble, a recipe cannot specify every vibration of the oven's heating element, and a contract cannot anticipate every future dispute. Each of these genres is therefore a compression of the world, and each compresses selectively, preserving what matters for its purpose and discarding the rest. The interesting question is not simply what gets left out, but what happens when the discarded material turns out to matter after all, because every compressed representation eventually collides with circumstances its maker did not foresee. Looking at a map, a recipe, and a legal contract side by side reveals three distinct philosophies of this problem: the map repairs ambiguity by grounding it in shared space, the recipe by grounding it in embodied skill, and the contract by grounding it in anticipated disagreement.

Consider one running example: a group of neighbors converting a derelict asphalt parking lot into a shared vegetable garden with raised beds, a rainwater tank, and a rule that anyone who works a shift may take home a share of the harvest. This modest project can be represented as a map, as a recipe, and as a contract, and each representation will capture a different slice of the same reality.

A map of the garden preserves spatial relationships above all else. It records where the beds sit relative to one another, where the water line runs, which corner receives morning sun, and how wide the paths are between beds. What it discards is nearly everything else: the smell of the compost, the fact that the northeast bed was built by the teenager on the block who is proud of it, the sound of traffic, the history of the lot as a place where people used to leave broken furniture. The map also silently discards time; it shows the garden as it is or as it should be at a frozen moment, with no trace of seasonal change or the slow spread of mint invading a neighboring bed. Yet maps have a distinctive virtue when ambiguity strikes: their claims are anchored to geography, and geography is checkable. If two readers disagree about whether the path between beds three and four is passable with a wheelbarrow, they can walk it together and settle the question against the terrain itself. The map's scale becomes a shared convention for repair; when the drawn width turns out to be wrong, users annotate, redraw, or simply learn to read the gap between the representation and the ground. The ambiguity is repaired by triangulation among the document, the territory, and a community of readers who agree that the territory is the final arbiter.

A recipe for the garden's soil mix, or for the tomato sauce made from its harvest, operates on entirely different principles. It preserves sequence and proportion: combine two parts topsoil to one part compost to one part coconut coir, wet it until it holds together when squeezed but crumbles when poked, fill the beds to within a hand's width of the rim. It discards almost all spatial information, since the sauce does not care whether it is made in this kitchen or another, and it discards most of the sensory world except as filtered through a few strategic cues about texture and color. Crucially, though, a good recipe also discards the pretense of complete specification. No recipe states the torque required to stir, the exact acoustic pitch of a proper simmer, or the millimeter tolerance for chopped onions, because these cannot be conveyed in words alone. Instead, the recipe presupposes a competent body on the other end, someone whose hands already know what "stiff peaks" feel like and whose nose knows when garlic has gone from fragrant to burnt. When ambiguity arises, say the instruction to cook until the sauce has "come together," the user does not consult the terrain as a map reader would; the user consults their own accumulated practice and, failing that, calls someone more experienced. Repair here is apprenticeship-shaped. Ambiguity is not eliminated but absorbed into tacit skill, and each failure becomes material for the cook's growing judgment rather than evidence that the document needs correcting.

The garden's third representation, a contract between the participating households, preserves obligations and their conditions: who pays for the water tank, what counts as completing a work shift, how shares are divided if someone contributes labor but no materials, and what happens when the project dissolves. It discards the sensory and the spatial almost entirely, and it even discards much of the relationship's warmth, translating goodwill into clauses. But it makes a distinctive addition the other two genres lack: it anticipates its own failure modes. A well-drafted contract contains its own repair mechanisms, definitions of terms to preempt quibbles, procedures for resolving disputes before they harden, and remedies for breach that specify what compensation looks like. When a neighbor insists that watering counts as a full shift while another considers it barely half, the contract's definition section is invoked; when genuine disagreement survives, the escalation clause points to mediation rather than to the terrain or to embodied skill. The contract repairs ambiguity procedurally, by having decided in advance who decides.

Set against each other, the three forms reveal complementary truths about instruction. The map is honest about the world's independence from us; it invites verification against a reality that does not care what we think. The recipe is honest about the limits of language; it quietly delegates its unfinished business to human judgment and tradition. The contract is honest about human conflict; it assumes readers may not share interests, let alone interpretations, and builds machinery for that eventuality. In the life of the garden, all three coexist and compensate for one another's blindnesses: the map settles where the shed goes, the recipe ensures next year's seedlings thrive, and the contract keeps the peace when the first perfect tomatoes appear and everyone suddenly cares intensely about fairness. None of them captures reality whole, and none pretends to. Their wisdom lies in knowing exactly which part of reality they are keeping, which part they are trusting their users to supply, and what shared anchor, land, body, or procedure, will be appealed to when the compression finally shows its seams.
