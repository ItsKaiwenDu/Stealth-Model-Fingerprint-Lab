# The Bridge at Half-Past Six

The lamplighter was still on his rounds when the first barge came down the river and stopped short of the draw. Old Kessel, who had poled coal barges into Marlow's Landing for thirty-one years, stood at the bow with his hat in one hand and stared across the water at the span. The draw was up. It had not been raised for maintenance in the eleven years since it was rebuilt, and it was not raised now for any boat — the river was empty in both directions as far as the morning haze allowed a man to see.

Kessel tied off at the lower wharf and walked the length of the dock to the harbormaster's shed, where a boy was sweeping the steps. "Bridge is shut," Kessel said.

"Shut how?"

"Draw's up. Chains across the road side, far as I can tell from the water."

The boy leaned his broom against the doorframe and looked at the bridge himself, squinting through the fog that sat on the river like wool. From the wharf, a man could see the raised leaves of the draw against the pale sky, and something else besides: two figures walking back and forth along the closed roadway, small with distance, stopping at intervals as though examining something laid on the planks.

"Engineer trouble," the boy said finally. "The iron gets soft spots. My uncle said they'd have to re-rivet her someday."

"Your uncle drives a milk wagon," Kessel said.

By seven o'clock there were perhaps forty men on the south bank who knew the bridge was closed, and each of them knew a different reason. The rivets, said the boy's version, passed along the rope walk. A piling had settled, said the oyster dredgers, who had felt something in the water two nights past and were not above claiming credit for noticing. A man had jumped from the middle of the span before dawn, said a deckhand off the *Cora B*, who had it from the night watchman's wife, who had heard it from the watchman himself, though the watchman, when pressed later, would say only that he'd been told to keep everyone off the approach roads and that he didn't ask why, because asking got a man replaced.

At Hartmann's coffee house on Water Street, where the dockworkers took their rolls standing at the counter because sitting cost a penny more, the closure was being weighed like cargo. Hartmann himself had crossed the bridge at half-past five to buy flour and had been turned back by a policeman he did not recognize — not one of the town constables but a tall man in a blue coat with brass buttons too new for this district.

"Turned back how?" asked Vogel, the tally clerk.

"Told the bridge was closed. I asked till when. He said till further orders. I asked whose orders. He looked at me as if I'd asked him the price of tea in Canton."

"So it's orders." Vogel nodded slowly, satisfied, as though the word itself were a plank he could stand on. "Orders come from above. Above means the county, or the railway company, or both. You mark me — there'll be an inspection. There's always an inspection before they raise the tolls again."

This theory traveled fast because it flattered everyone. The millers liked it because it suggested the bridge would open. The shopkeepers liked it because a county matter was legible, and legible things could be waited out. By eight, Mrs. Aldous at the chandlery was telling customers that the railway intended to reroute freight through Fenwick and wanted the Marlow crossing quiet while the paperwork was drawn, and she had improved the story in the telling without knowing she had done so — the original version, overheard third-hand near the customs house, had mentioned only "papers."

The papers themselves did exist, or something like them. At nine, a clerk from the county engineer's office arrived on the morning packet, a young man named Pruitt with mud on his boots and no information in his leather case. He had been sent to look at the bridge and report back; what he found there, and whether anyone let him onto it, he did not say. He took a room at the Anchor and was seen at ten o'clock standing at the corner of Bridge Street with a notebook, writing nothing, watching the crowd watch the bridge.

The crowd grew the way crowds do, not all at once but in layers. First the curious, who came because something was happening and stayed because nothing was explained. Then the practical: farmers with wagons bound for the market on the north bank, who now faced either the long ferry queue or the twelve-mile detour by the ford road. Then the merchants, whose goods sat in warehouses on the wrong sides of the river. A drayman named Cobb, whose entire livelihood consisted of hauling crates across that span four times a day, stood at the barrier chain for an hour, talking to other draymen, and by noon had concluded — from the newness of the policeman's buttons, which he had examined closely — that men had been brought in from outside, and that whatever was wrong with the bridge was therefore serious enough to be kept from local eyes, or shameful enough, or political enough. He offered these alternatives freely and committed to none.

At the customs house, the inspector, a careful man named Brecht, attempted to construct an official account and discovered he had almost nothing official to put in it. His telegram to the county seat went out at ten-forty. The reply came back at noon, eleven words: **BRIDGE CLOSED BY ORDER PENDING EXAMINATION FURTHER INSTRUCTIONS FOLLOW**. No signature beyond the office name. Brecht posted a notice to this effect on the customs house door, and the notice made things worse rather than better, because "pending examination" confirmed every rumor that involved examination and contradicted none that did not. Within the hour, three separate versions of the notice were circulating, each slightly longer than the original, each adding a clause somebody felt must have been left out.

The travelers suffered most quietly. On the ferry landing, a woman in a gray traveling dress with a carpet bag and a birdcage covered in cloth waited for the second crossing of the day, having already missed the first. She had come down from the north intending to catch the evening train from Marlow's south-side station. Now the station might as well have been in another country. She asked the ferryman when the bridge would open. He told her what he had heard, which varied depending on whom he had last spoken with, and she understood, being a traveler, that this was the normal condition of the world and that patience was the only currency accepted everywhere.

By late afternoon the light came low and gold across the water, and the draw still hung open against the sky. The two figures had gone from the span. Kessel came off his barge, bought his roll, and listened to the day's accumulated intelligence: rivets, pilings, a jumper, tolls, papers, outside men. He considered them the way he considered weather reports — useful chiefly for knowing what people feared.

"They'll open it when they open it," he said, and finished his roll, and went down to check his lines, because the river did not care what the bridge was doing, and neither, until it opened, could anyone at Marlow's Landing afford to.
