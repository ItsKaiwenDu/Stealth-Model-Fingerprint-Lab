# Eleven Seconds

The clock in Pell's Harbor had been losing time for as long as anyone could remember, and for as long as anyone could remember, the town had agreed not to mind. Tourists set their watches by it anyway, because it sat high on the stone tower above the harbor mouth, its white face catching the last light long after the water went gray. The fishermen joked that the clock ran slow the way the tide did, out of habit rather than fault.

Maren Holt knew better. She was the fourth keeper in her family, and the ledger in the tower office — a fat book bound in cracked green cloth — explained everything the tourists never thought to ask. Whenever a person in Pell's Harbor made a decision they would come to regret, the clock lost exactly eleven seconds. Not ten. Not twelve. Eleven, each time, entered in the ledger in four generations of handwriting, most of it her grandfather's slanted pencil, then her father's careful ink, then her own.

*June 3, 1958 — 11 seconds. The Aldridge girl turned down the scholarship.* Her grandfather had written that in the margin, though how he'd known, Maren never learned.

Her father had been less inclined to gossip. His margins held only dates and weather.

Maren climbed the tower twice a week, Tuesdays and Fridays, to wind the mechanism, oil the escapement, and correct the hands against the speaking clock she dialed on the yellowed telephone mounted by the door. The corrections were the true work. Between them, she let the clock run as it pleased. It seemed wrong, somehow, to force it back to true before the ledger caught up with whatever the town had done to itself.

She had grown used to reading the losses like weather. A cluster of entries in March meant exam results were out. A single entry on a Sunday evening meant someone at the Anchor had said yes to a second marriage, or no to a third. The town made its decisions, the clock absorbed them, and Maren recorded both.

The anomaly came in October, on a Tuesday, at twenty minutes past three in the morning.

She hadn't been there to see it. She found it Friday, when the correction came out wrong — the clock eleven seconds behind where her calculations said it should be, even accounting for the week. She checked her figures twice, then went back through the log of automatic readings she kept now, a small digital recorder wired to the movement, because her eyes weren't what they'd been. There it was: 3:21 a.m., Tuesday. Eleven seconds gone between one tick and the next.

At 3:21 in the morning, Pell's Harbor was asleep. Nobody decided anything at 3:21 in the morning except whether to get up and use the toilet, and Maren had never seen a loss for that, though God knew half the town regretted the coffee.

She wrote it in the ledger without a marginal note. But she started checking the recorder more often.

The pattern, when it emerged, was worse than ghosts. On the twelfth of November the clock lost eleven seconds at dawn, and two days later Kjeld Brandt announced at the Anchor that he was selling his boat, the *Marta Lee*, forty years on the water, done. Maren stood in the back with her cider and felt the floor tilt slightly. The loss had come *before*. Whatever Kjeld decided, he had apparently decided it two days before he knew.

Then Mrs. Okafor, who had sworn since her husband died that she'd never leave the house with the blue door, lost eleven seconds from the town's time on a Thursday, and put her house on the market the following Wednesday, and cried at the kitchen table of the young couple buying it, everyone said, though she smiled through it.

Maren began keeping two ledgers. In the second one, private, she wrote the losses first and the explanations after, leaving blank lines where no explanation arrived yet. The blank lines multiplied. Losses came in fog, in rain, at hours when the streets held nothing but cats. Decisions followed like weather arriving after the barometer fell: a resignation, a reconciliation, a letter finally mailed, a dog taken to the vet for the last visit. Always eleven seconds. Always in advance.

She asked old Tom Ellery, who had fished until his knees quit, whether his father had ever said anything about the clock running ahead of people. Tom considered this over his tea.

"Clocks don't run ahead," he said. "People run behind. Your grandfather knew that."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Tom agreed pleasantly, and looked out at the water, and Maren understood she'd gotten everything he had.

She did not tell the council. What would she say — that the clock had begun registering regrets before the decisions that caused them? That the town's future remorse was arriving ahead of schedule, like gulls before a storm? The council had enough trouble with the seawall assessment.

In December the losses came more often. Nine in one week. Maren stopped trying to match them to events. She climbed the tower in the cold and wound the mechanism and watched the pendulum swing with the patience of something that had outlasted every explanation offered to it.

Her own life sat in a drawer beneath the ledger, and had for nineteen years: ferry tickets to the mainland, one bought every spring since Anders left, every one unused. She told herself each year it was practical — good to have, in case. Nineteen tickets, soft as cloth now at the folds. Anders had gone east for work and asked her once, plainly, on the phone, to come. She had said the tower needed keeping. He had not asked twice, and eventually the calls thinned to cards, and then the cards thinned to nothing, and the tickets kept accumulating like sediment.

She had never entered those tickets in the ledger. It seemed clear to her that the decision to stay had cost the clock its seconds long ago, on whatever afternoon she'd said *the tower needs keeping*, and there was no sense auditing a debt that old.

On the morning of the solstice, Maren climbed the tower before light to correct the clock for the year's end. Frost feathered the office window. The recorder showed a loss at 5:14 that same morning — eleven seconds, gone, while she'd still been asleep.

She stood in the cold office with the ledger open and understood, with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have, that the loss was hers. No one else had been awake to decide anything. The whole town lay dark under frost, and the clock had taken its eleven seconds anyway, and there was nowhere else the debt could have come from.

But she hadn't decided anything. That was the thing she kept circling. She had decided nothing — no call placed, no ticket taken out, no word spoken to anyone. She had only woken in the gray dark with the strange weight she got every winter, the one she'd stopped examining years ago, and lain there listening to the wind work at the harbor mouth.

Unless the decision wasn't waiting to be made. Unless it had been made long ago, in some room of herself she didn't visit, and the clock — patient, exact — had simply gotten around to billing her for it now, nineteen years late, or nineteen years early. She thought of the blank lines in her private ledger and saw that she had assumed the losses pointed forward. They might point backward just as easily. The clock might not be predicting regret at all. It might be collecting it, tardily, from places in a person so deep that the person found out about the decision the way the town found out about the weather: after the fact, looking at the damage.

Or it might mean nothing. She wrote the entry and left the margin blank, because she honestly did not know, and the not-knowing felt, after sixty-eight years, like the honest condition of the job.

She corrected the hands to true. Then, instead of locking the office, she went down the spiral stair and along the frozen quay to her house, and took the newest ticket from the drawer — this spring's, bought in April, still crisp — and the oldest one too, because it seemed wrong to abandon it, and put both in her coat pocket.

The morning boat to the mainland left at nine. She walked down to the dock with the frost squeaking under her boots and stood at the rail-end where the tickets were sold, and the story does not record whether she boarded, because Maren Holt never told anyone, and the harbor was empty, and the gulls keep what they see.

What is known is this: that evening, at her Tuesday-and-Friday hour, she climbed the tower and dialed the speaking clock, and found the mechanism running eleven seconds *fast* — the first gain in the ledger's hundred and forty years, in any handwriting. She sat a long while with her pencil poised over the open book.

Then she closed the ledger unmarked, and left the clock as it was, running ahead of everyone in Pell's Harbor — which, if old Tom Ellery was right about people running behind, meant that somewhere, at last, someone had gotten in front of their own regret, and the tower clock was doing the only decent thing it could.

It was waiting for the rest of the town to catch up.
