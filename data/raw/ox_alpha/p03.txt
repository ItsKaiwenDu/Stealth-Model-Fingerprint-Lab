When a city's water consumption jumps suddenly and no pipe is visibly gushing water onto a street, the temptation is to leap straight to a favorite theory—drought, a big new customer, theft—and start acting on it. The disciplined approach resists that instinct. An investigation should be built like an experiment: define what you know, quantify what changed, generate competing explanations, rank them by plausibility and cheapness of testing, and only then commit resources. The goal is not to confirm the first story that comes to mind but to eliminate stories systematically until one survives every honest attempt to kill it.

The investigation begins before any hypothesis is formed, with the question every good investigator asks: is the rise even real? Apparent surges in consumption are frequently artifacts of measurement rather than changes in actual use. Before mobilizing crews, the utility staff should reconstruct exactly how the number was produced. Was the figure derived from actual meter readings or from estimates interpolated between readings? Did the billing cycle that just closed contain an unusual number of days? Has any upstream software been updated—a new meter-data-management system, a changed rounding rule, a recalibrated estimator for accounts whose meters failed to transmit? Comparing raw, day-level production figures from treatment plants and pumping stations against billed consumption over the same period separates physical reality from administrative noise. If plant output rose in lockstep with billing, water is genuinely moving; if they diverge, the mystery may live entirely inside the accounting system.

Once the increase is confirmed at the production level, the next task is to characterize it precisely, because the shape of the curve is itself evidence. A rise confined to certain hours suggests irrigation or a large industrial process running on schedule. A flat, round-the-clock elevation suggests a leak somewhere in the distribution network or continuous draw by a single large user. A step change on a specific date points to an event: a main repressurized after repair, a valve configuration altered, a new customer connected, a fire hydrant flow test conducted without logging it. The analyst should also normalize for weather. Hot, dry weeks drive outdoor watering dramatically; a jump during a heat wave may be entirely unremarkable. Fitting recent consumption against temperature and rainfall history lets the city ask whether the residual—the part the weather cannot explain—is actually abnormal.

With the phenomenon characterized, hypothesis generation can begin, and the list should be written down explicitly rather than held in people's heads. The major families are: real leakage somewhere in the network; increased legitimate demand (weather, a new large customer, population influx, a construction project using water for dust control or concrete); unauthorized use (fire hydrant tampering, illegal connections); metering error at customer premises or at the master meters themselves; and data or billing artifacts. Each hypothesis carries a prediction about where and when the excess water appears, and each can be tested with different instruments and costs. Leakage predicts elevated night flows in specific pressure zones; district metered areas—zones isolated by valves and instrumented with a single flow meter—are the natural search grid here. Stepping through zones one at a time, ideally between two and four in the morning when legitimate demand is minimal, localizes a leak far faster than acoustic surveys of the whole city. Legitimate demand predicts concentration in particular customer classes, which billing data can reveal by comparing this month's largest consumers against their own histories and against class-wide norms. Metering error predicts discrepancies between what a customer's meter says and what physics allows: a check on a suspiciously huge account, comparing its meter against a temporary calibrated meter installed in series, settles the question directly.

Controls matter throughout. The city should track at least one comparable zone or neighboring utility whose conditions match—same weather, similar rate structure—as a reference that did not experience the surge. Without such a control, there is no way to distinguish a city-specific cause from a regional one, such as a hot spell affecting everyone equally. Within the analysis, controls also mean guarding against confirmation bias: assign someone on the team the job of arguing against the leading hypothesis, and pre-commit to numerical thresholds that will count as falsification. If the leading theory is "one industrial customer expanded operations," decide in advance what magnitude of verified expansion would be required to account for the volume, and abandon the theory if the arithmetic does not close.

This is where explicit decision points earn their keep. A sensible sequence gates spending on evidence. Gate one: if the anomaly disappears after correcting cycle lengths and estimate handling, stop; no field work needed. Gate two: if weather-normalized consumption is normal, close the investigation with a monitoring plan rather than a hunt. Gate three: if night flows in every district metered area are within historical norms, leakage becomes unlikely enough to deprioritize, and attention shifts to customer-side and master-meter hypotheses. Gate four: if a single zone shows anomalous night flow, concentrate acoustic leak detection and pressure testing there before considering anything else. Each gate converts an open-ended mystery into a bounded choice, and prevents the common failure mode in which crews dig up streets based on a huddle's consensus rather than on measurements.

Among the hypotheses, one deserves special mention because it usually sounds absurd when first voiced, yet it fails the test of plausibility less often than intuition suggests: that the meters themselves are fine, the pipes are sound, the customers are innocent, and the entire surge is a units or scaling error introduced into the reporting chain—gallons read as liters, cubic feet misconverted, a decimal shifted in a dashboard query. It looks foolish because thousands of meters would have to "be wrong" simultaneously. But that is precisely the point: none of them need be wrong at all. A single line of code, a changed database field definition, or an analyst summing values that used to be daily averages and are now daily totals can multiply every number downstream by a constant factor. The diagnostic signature is elegant and nearly free to check: if the ratio of this month's reported consumption to last year's same-month figure is suspiciously close to a clean conversion factor—3.785 for gallons-to-liters, 748 for cubic-feet-to-gallons, 10 for a slipped decimal—the foolish-sounding theory graduates instantly to prime suspect. Cities have chased phantom leaks for weeks before someone noticed that the "surge" was exactly 1.6 times normal. Testing it costs minutes; ignoring it risks weeks of misdirected digging.

Finally, whatever conclusion survives, the city should document the full reasoning chain—which hypotheses were considered, what evidence eliminated them, which thresholds triggered which actions—and fold the lesson back into routine practice: better district metering coverage, logged hydrant use, automated sanity checks that flag implausible ratios in reporting systems before they reach decision-makers. A sudden consumption spike handled this way costs a few days of disciplined attention. Handled by hunch, it can cost a season of chasing water that was never lost—or missing water that truly was.
