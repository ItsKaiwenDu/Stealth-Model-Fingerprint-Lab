**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Office of the Provost and the Deans' Council
**FROM:** Office of Student Academic Success
**DATE:** [Current Date]
**RE:** Recommendation on 24-Hour Library Operations During the Examination Period

---

**1. Summary Recommendation**

We recommend that the university conduct a **one-semester pilot** of overnight library access during the final examination period, running from the Monday of reading week through the last scheduled exam. The pilot should be deliberately limited in scope, instrumented for evaluation, and contingent on the conditions outlined in Section 6. The objective is not to affirm a permanent policy, but to test whether overnight access produces measurable academic benefits for students at a cost and risk profile the institution can sustain.

**2. The Question Before Us**

The suggestion to keep the main library open through the night during exams has surfaced repeatedly in Student Senate resolutions and residence-hall feedback. The intuition is straightforward: students report a need for quiet, well-resourced study space in the final stretch of the term, and the current 11:00 p.m. closing time forces them onto coffee shops, common rooms, and 24-hour fast-food venues. The question is whether the university should formalize overnight access as a service, and if so, under what terms.

**3. Learning Benefits**

The case for overnight access rests on the recognition that examination periods concentrate students' cognitive demand. A quiet, equipped, supervised study environment is pedagogically superior to the alternatives most students will find. Research on study behavior consistently links access to appropriate study environments with improved retention, deeper engagement with material, and reduced procrastination, particularly when combined with structured services such as reference support and quiet-study enforcement. Even if only a fraction of students use the space overnight, the option itself may reduce stress and signal institutional investment in academic success, which carries indirect reputational benefits.

We should be cautious, however, about overstating the case. The students most likely to use overnight hours are those who already demonstrate strong study habits; the service may therefore reinforce existing patterns rather than rescue struggling students. Any pilot should be evaluated with this caveat in mind.

**4. Staffing**

Overnight operations cannot be improvised. At minimum, the library would need a professional librarian or senior staffer on call, a security officer or trained student-safety attendant, and facilities support for HVAC, restrooms, and any required technical troubleshooting. We estimate that a fully staffed overnight shift during a three-week exam window would add approximately [estimated cost] to the library's seasonal operating budget, though exact figures should be confirmed with Human Resources and Public Safety before pilot launch.

A lighter model, in which the building is open but unstaffed beyond a roving patrol, is not advisable. Unstaffed academic buildings at small institutions generate disproportionate risk relative to the savings captured, and the loss of reference support substantially weakens the educational value of the space.

**5. Safety**

Safety is the single area in which a failed pilot would carry the highest cost. Late-night operations require coordination with campus security: clearly marked entrances, controlled access, visible staff presence, adequate exterior lighting, and a documented check-in protocol for any student who remains past a defined late hour. We also recommend a clear policy on food, sleep, and prohibited items, communicated to students in advance. The pilot should not proceed unless the Director of Public Safety confirms in writing that staffing and response protocols are in place.

There is also a duty-of-care dimension. Sleep-deprived students operating heavy equipment in adjacent facilities, or commuting home at 4:00 a.m., present risks that the overnight library does not directly cause but may indirectly enable. The pilot should be paired with a brief, optional well-being communication reminding students of available health services and transportation options.

**6. Equity**

Equity considerations cut both ways and deserve careful thought. On one hand, overnight access may disproportionately benefit students who lack quiet private study space at home, including first-generation students, commuter students, and those in less expensive housing. On the other hand, the students most likely to use the service may be those with the fewest competing demands, which can also correlate with socioeconomic advantage. A well-publicized, free-of-charge overnight service, advertised through multiple channels and accessible without a car, is more likely to function as an equity intervention than a privilege.

We recommend that any pilot be paired with expanded late-night transit or safe-ride options, so that access is not contingent on personal vehicle availability.

**7. Displaced Costs**

It is worth acknowledging openly that the university already absorbs the indirect costs of students studying in unregulated environments. Late-night facilities requests at residence halls, increased custodial loads, and student-health visits linked to exam-period exhaustion all carry cost. An overnight library does not create new demand so much as relocate it to a more appropriate venue. A credible cost analysis should compare the marginal cost of overnight library operations against the marginal cost of the status quo, rather than against zero.

**8. Evaluating the Pilot**

A pilot without a measurement plan is not a pilot; it is a delayed permanent decision. We propose the following evaluation structure. Pre- and post-pilot surveys of students, segmented by year, major, and residence, should capture use patterns, perceived academic impact, and perceptions of safety. Objective data should include hourly door counts, incident logs, and a comparison of grade distributions and DFW rates for high-enrollment courses against the same courses in the prior year, with appropriate caveats about confounders. Staff should log qualitative observations about the kinds of work students are doing overnight, which will inform whether the service is being used as intended.

A formal review at the end of the semester, with representation from Academic Affairs, Student Affairs, Library Services, and Public Safety, should weigh the evidence and recommend continuation, modification, or discontinuation.

**9. Conditions for Proceeding**

The pilot should proceed only if the following conditions are met. Public Safety must confirm readiness. The library must secure a sustainable staffing plan, not a one-time reassignment. A communication plan must reach commuter and off-campus students. Late-night transportation support must be coordinated. An evaluation budget of approximately [estimate] must be allocated. And the timeline must be limited: a single semester, with a go/no-go decision before the following examination period.

**10. Conclusion**

Overnight library access during exams is neither a luxury nor a self-evident good. It is a service decision with real costs and real risks, and it deserves to be made on evidence rather than on the strength of the most vocal constituency. A carefully scoped, well-instrumented pilot is the responsible path forward. We recommend proceeding on that basis, with the conditions above treated as prerequisites rather than aspirations.

We are available to brief the Council at your convenience and to coordinate with Library Services and Public Safety on implementation details.
