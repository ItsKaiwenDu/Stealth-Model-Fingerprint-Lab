# The Weight of a Single Word

My name is Ezra Vanek, and I make my living in the spaces between languages. I am a sworn translator, registered with the court in three jurisdictions, and I have rendered confessions, treaties, and last words into English from tongues that span the globe. My reputation depends on a single, unfashionable virtue: fidelity. I do not interpret; I translate. The distinction matters, because the people who hire me are often the same people who never read what I produce until the moment they wish they had.

The Karpov envelope arrived on a Tuesday. The Volkov envelope on the following Friday. Both were delivered by courier, both contained retainer checks drawn on respectable banks, and both asked for the same deadline: the upcoming Monday at noon. I had been retained by the Karpov family to translate a formal apology from Russian into English, to be read aloud in a public forum by their eldest son, Pyotr, before a delegation of the Volkov family. The Karpovs, I gathered, had caused some grief in a matter involving permits, accusations, and a construction crane. The Volkovs had been wronged. The apology was a condition of settlement.

I did not know, when I accepted the Karpov commission, that the Volkov family had also retained me. The Volkov envelope contained a translation request in the opposite direction: the Volkovs wished to render into Russian a formal apology they had written in English, to be read aloud by their own representative before the Karpov delegation. The circumstances, the families' positions, the timing, the venue — all matched the Karpov materials. Someone, I realized quickly, had engineered a spectacle in which both families would apologize to each other, perhaps to satisfy a regulatory body, perhaps to satisfy their own pride.

I should pause here to note something about my profession. A translator of standing does not normally receive the source text of an apology written in the family’s own language. The Karpovs handed me a Russian draft. The Volkovs handed me an English draft. In each case, I was to produce the other language. The drafts were, in their respective languages, models of contrition. The Karpov apology acknowledged negligence, expressed deep regret, and pledged restitution. The Volkov apology acknowledged contributory oversight, expressed deep regret, and pledged cooperation. The translators in Moscow and New York who had drafted these documents had done competent work. The rhetoric was conventional. The sentiments were conventional. The structures were, in a way that I would only appreciate later, identical.

I began with the Karpov text. Russian is my first language. I know its cadences the way a pianist knows scales. The draft used the word *vinovaty*, which the dictionaries render as "guilty," but which in this register, within this construction, meant something closer to "at fault" or "culpable." It is a clean word, uncontroversial. I translated it as "culpable." I moved on. The next unusual word was *pomiloslovye*, an archaic term referring to a desire for forgiveness, a seeking of grace. I rendered it as "entreaty for grace." The rest of the text was straightforward, and by Saturday evening I had a clean English version on my desk, printed, initialed, and ready for delivery.

Then I opened the Volkov materials.

The English draft contained a phrase I had just translated. The Volkovs' English drafter, working in good faith from notes supplied by the family, had written, "We acknowledge that we were *culpable* in failing to verify the load-bearing specifications of the shared wall." A second phrase appeared later: "This apology is offered as an *entreaty for grace*." The two English words — "culpable," "entreaty for grace" — were not coincidence. They were, in the small but exacting world of sworn translation, a fingerprint. I knew, with the cold certainty that my profession occasionally provides, that I had been hired to translate the same document twice, and that I had done so successfully without yet knowing it.

The situation clarified itself. Two powerful families, each advised by counsel, had chosen the only path available to them: mutual apology. Each had commissioned its own draft. Each had hired, independently, the same translator, hoping that the English version heard by the Volkovs would match the Russian version heard by the Karpovs — and vice versa. The symmetry was the point. If the apologies aligned in form and substance, the settlement would hold. If they diverged, the recriminations would begin again.

I sat at my desk for a long time on Sunday morning and considered the implications. The drafts were aligned in everything except one word. The Karpov draft, in Russian, contained the word *pokayaniye*. The Volkov draft, in English, contained the word "remorse." In context, *pokayaniye* does not mean "remorse." It means something like "the act of turning away from a fault, the full reorientation of conduct, the metanoia." It is a heavier word than "remorse." It implies, in the Orthodox tradition in which the Karpovs were raised, a transformation of the soul. "Remorse" is a feeling. *Pokayaniye* is a vow. I had translated *pokayaniye* as "remorse" in the Karpov text, because that was the natural English equivalent and because no one had asked me to do otherwise. The Volkovs, meanwhile, had written "remorse" in their own English draft, intending to be carried over into Russian as some form of *rasskayaniye* or *sokhaleniye* — the milder words for regret, not *pokayaniye*.

This was the asymmetry. The Karpovs were prepared to confess something larger than the Volkovs believed they were hearing. The Volkovs, reading the English text on Monday, would believe the Karpovs had offered a conventional apology, when in fact the Karpovs had offered — through my translation — something closer to a spiritual surrender. Conversely, the Karpovs, hearing the Russian text on Monday, would receive from the Volkovs an apology considerably softer than the one the Volkovs believed they had given.

A translator of lesser conscience would have simply delivered both documents and taken both checks. The families would have signed the settlement. The lawyers would have celebrated. The asymmetry, if it was ever noticed, would be explained away as a quirk of language. I do not work that way. My word is my instrument, and a translator who allows two different audiences to hear two different meanings from the same act of contrition is not a translator at all. He is a confederate of one side or the other, or simply a fraud.

I drove to the Volkov residence on Sunday afternoon. I introduced myself to the eldest son, Anton, who received me in a sitting room hung with icons. I explained, with the professional calm that has served me in courtrooms and consulates, that I had identified a single word in their English draft that would not translate cleanly into Russian. I suggested, on my own initiative, a substitution. Anton asked what the original word implied. I told him. He was silent for a long time. Then he said, quietly, that if the Karpovs had been prepared to use the heavier word in Russian, the Volkovs would not be outdone in their own language. He asked me to ensure the Russian version used *pokayaniye*. I agreed, and I noted the change in my register, signed and dated, as the law requires.

I drove to the Karpov residence that evening. Pyotr received me in a study lined with law books. I explained the same situation in reverse. I told him that the English version of the Karpov apology, as written, did not carry the full weight of the Russian *pokayaniye*. I said I could either soften the English to match "remorse," or I could clarify for the Volkovs what the Karpovs had actually meant. Pyotr studied me for a moment and then said that the Karpovs had meant every syllable. He asked me to ensure the English version, when read on Monday, would convey the full meaning of *pokayaniye*. I agreed, and I noted the change.

On Monday at noon, the two families sat across from one another in a neutral conference room. Pyotr read my English version aloud. Anton read the Russian version I had prepared. The words aligned. The apologies landed. The settlement was signed in the hour that followed.

I will say one last thing, because I think it is the only thing that needs saying. A translator is not the author of the words he carries. A translator is the bridge. When the bridge holds, the traffic passes safely. When the bridge bends, the traffic still passes, but the people on the other side arrive at a destination they did not intend. On Monday, two families arrived at the destination they had, in their hearts, already chosen. My job was simply to make sure the bridge held.
