# The Quiet Span

The bell at Stonemarch Quay rang a quarter past five, as it did every morning, though only three of the men waiting on the wharf stepped toward it. Captain Holler, who ran the *Ida Reeves*, swung his lamp in a slow arc and muttered that the rope had been cut again. The other two, Ben Avery and a young Cornishman whose name no one had learned yet, kept their eyes on the bridge.

The bridge was a three-span iron thing, raised in ’78 and painted the color of a bruise every spring. From below, between the storehouses and the chandler’s loft, you could see the central span only as a long, dark line against the paling sky, and you could see that the line had not moved. No lanterns moved along it. No early cart had begun its wooden complaint across the decking. The tollhouse window, where Mr. Petros usually sat with his coffee, was shuttered, and the small flag that flew when the bridge was open for river traffic had been taken in at some point during the night.

“Closed,” said Holler.

The young man asked, since when.

“Since when, he says,” Holler answered, and spat into the water. The spit took a long time to reach the river, which meant the tide was still coming in. Holler’s *Ida Reeves* was a dumb barge poled by four men and a mule; she did not need the bridge open, only a clear path along the embankment. But the embankment ran beneath the bridge’s southern approach, and if the approach were barricaded, his men could not reach her.

Ben Avery was the foreman for the Tressider warehouse, and he had keys to the loading bay but no appetite for rumor. He walked the few yards to the chandler’s shop, where the lamps were already lit and old Mrs. Lyle was sweeping her step. He asked her, as he did most mornings, whether she had heard anything.

She had heard the watchman’s boots at half past three, she said. Four of them, perhaps, though the lane carries sound strangely when the river is high. She had heard a horse that did not stop at the tollhouse. She had heard, later, a sound like a great door being closed, and then nothing.

“Nothing is a kind of answer,” Ben said.

“It is the worst kind,” she answered, and went back to her step.

Across the river, on the Far Quay, the omnibus was already late. The omnibus was always a little late, but today its driver stood in the road with the reins loose on his shoulder, looking up at the bridge the way a man looks at a stalled horse. The passengers inside — two women with market baskets, a clerk from the brewery in a straw boater, a drummer for a Nottingham lace house who had been sleeping in his collar — began to murmur. The driver got down and walked to the rail. He came back and said there were men on the far side with a cart, and that the tollhouse was closed, and that he would not put his horses across the timber footway, not for any of them, not this morning.

The footway, a narrow planked strip along the upstream side of the bridge, was meant for pedestrians only. Children used it. So did draymen leading laden mules, though the bylaw forbade it. The clerk from the brewery, who was small and impatient, said he would walk. The drummer, who was larger and less certain of his boots in the early chill, said he would not. The two women with the baskets were already climbing down, because the ferry steps were only a hundred yards along the embankment, and a penny saved was a penny earned.

The ferry, the *Clemency*, was poled by a man named Tibb and his nephew, and was licensed to carry six. She could carry ten, in the way of river craft, if no one asked. Tibb had been awake since four, poling a barge-load of ice from the cold store above the weir, and he had heard nothing, seen nothing, intended nothing, except to be paid. He took the two women, and then the clerk, and then a man with a portmanteau who would not say where he was going, only that he wished to be there before the office opened. The clerk, on the short crossing, said to the man with the portmanteau that the bridge had been closed by order of the county engineer, that there was a crack in the central pier. The man with the portmanteau said he had heard it was a body. Tibb, poling, said nothing at all, which was his custom.

By half past six the town was awake enough to be unhelpful. The baker’s boy, cycling up from the lower town with a basket of cottage loaves, had been turned back at the southern approach by a constable he did not recognize. The constable was in uniform but had a band on his arm, and he stood in front of a sawhorse painted in alternate stripes. The boy asked the constable why. The constable said, “On account.” The boy cycled back to the bakery and said, “On account,” and the baker said, “On account of what,” and the boy could not tell him, so the baker sent the loaves round by way of the ferry, which cost him a halfpenny per dozen and made him late for the early trade, and he was not pleased.

In the mayor’s parlor, above the guildhall, the mayor was not yet up. His wife was, and she was reading a letter. The letter had been delivered by hand sometime before dawn, folded into a plain envelope, and bore no name. It said, in three lines, that an inspection of the central span was required, that no traffic was to be permitted on or beneath the bridge until further notice, and that the county engineer had been informed. It was signed by a name no one in the parlor recognized, but the seal was the right one, or nearly the right one, or close enough that the mayor’s wife, who was careful in these matters, set it on the silver tray and went to wake her husband.

Down at the wharf, Holler had given up on the bell and was lighting his pipe. Ben Avery came back from the chandler’s and reported what Mrs. Lyle had said. The young Cornishman said he would swim it, if it came to that, and Holler told him, mildly, not to be a fool. A boy from the printshop came down the lane with a handbill, but the handbill was for a coming sermon and not for the bridge, and the boy was sorry he had nothing better, and Holler bought the handbill for a halfpenny and used it to roll a cigarette.

By seven, the footway was open. A constable stood at each end and allowed pedestrians to pass, but no carts, no horses, no loads of any kind. The tollgate at the southern approach remained shut, and a small crowd of draymen and market women gathered there to grumble and to be told, by a constable who did not know any more than they did, that the bridge was closed until further notice. Further notice was the phrase that did the work. It was a phrase that could mean an hour, or a day, or a week, and it allowed everyone to imagine a different length of trouble, and to argue about it, and to feel that the trouble was being managed, even if the management was elsewhere and not speaking to them.

The ferry did a brisk trade. Tibb took the morning’s wages in small coins and a coupon from the brewery, which he would spend at the chandler’s. The clerk from the brewery reached his office at twenty past seven, hot and damp, and told the brewer that the bridge was closed because a man had thrown himself from it in the night. The brewer, who was a careful man, sent the clerk back to the ferry to cross again and find out, if he could, whether the man had been pulled out. The clerk, who had been wrong about the crack in the pier, was glad of the errand. He went back to the ferry, and Tibb took him over, and the clerk asked a constable on the far quay whether a man had thrown himself from the bridge. The constable said he had heard nothing of the kind. The clerk went back to the brewery and reported that the rumor of the body was false. The brewer, who was a careful man, nodded, and then sent word to his agent in the next town that the bridge might be closed for some days, on account of a crack, which was the version he preferred.

By eight, the town had three versions, and by nine it had five, and by ten it had so many that the versions began to cancel one another out, and what was left was simply the fact of the closure, unexplained, and the fact of the footway, open, and the fact of the ferry, busy, and the fact of the sawhorse, striped, and the fact of the constable, who had a band on his arm, and who stood, and who would stand, until he was told not to.
