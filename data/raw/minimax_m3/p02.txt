# The Last Order

The fluorescent tube above their table had been flickering since eleven. By half past, it had settled into a steady, sullen hum that made Mira think of a hymn her mother used to hum in the kitchen, the one that always went flat on the high notes. The dining car of the station restaurant was nearly empty. A trucker in a reflective vest was asleep over his coffee at the far end. A couple by the window were sharing a slice of pie and a silence that looked comfortable, the way silences between people who have known each other a long time often do.

Mira and her brother Lev were not like that. They had known each other thirty-four years and had not spoken in eleven months.

The sealed envelope sat between them on the Formica, the same envelope that had arrived in a plain brown sleeve two weeks after their father’s funeral. He had been specific in the note that came with it, the kind of specific he got when he was being theatrical, which was often. *Not to be opened until both of you are together. Both. Together.* Their father had underlined the word *together* three times and drawn a small, impatient arrow in the margin, as if he could not trust the paper to carry his meaning.

Lev had driven three hours from his house in the suburbs. Mira had taken a train. They had agreed to meet at the restaurant because it was the only place equidistant from the two of them that didn’t require either to cross the threshold of the other’s life. Their father would have appreciated the arithmetic of it. He had been a schoolteacher who believed, sincerely, that most family arguments could be solved by a well-drawn map.

A waitress passed, her apron stiff with the smell of fryer oil. She refilled their water glasses without asking and did not ask if they were ready to order, either. She had the practiced eye of someone who could tell when a table was not a table but a negotiation.

“I didn’t think you’d come,” Lev said. He was turning a spoon over and over in his fingers, the way he used to turn a pen in class when he was working out a problem on the board. He taught high school math now, at the same school their father had taught at, in the same room, with the same desk. Mira had never been able to decide whether that was devotion or a kind of slow-motion haunting.

“I almost didn’t,” she said.

He set the spoon down. Outside, a freight train went through, the kind that doesn’t stop, and the windows vibrated in their frames. The trucker at the far end didn’t stir.

Their father had been a man who liked to be obeyed. He had also been a man who liked to be understood. When those two impulses disagreed, he had a habit of writing things down, as if ink could do what his voice could not. Mira had been the one to find the sealed envelope, tucked inside a hollowed-out book of Chekhov on his shelf, wedged behind *The Lady with the Dog*. Lev had been the one to call her, eleven months ago, to say he thought they should open it, and then the call had turned into something else, the way calls between them always did, the way a small fire turns into a larger one when you keep feeding it the wrong kind of wood.

She had said he only wanted to open it because he was afraid of what was inside.

He had said she only wanted to keep it sealed because she was afraid of what was outside.

The waitress drifted back. “Y’all want menus?”

Lev looked at Mira. Mira looked at the envelope. It was a creamy color, the kind of paper their father used to buy at the stationery shop on Sixth, where the owner wrapped purchases in tissue and tied them with a thin gold cord. Their father had liked ceremony. He had liked the feel of things in his hands. He had liked the way a good pen made a sound on good paper, a sound like a small, satisfied animal.

“Mira?” Lev said.

The waitress, reading the room with the fluency of long practice, withdrew.

“Coffee,” Mira said, without looking up. “Two coffees.”

The waitress nodded and was gone.

Lev pushed the envelope a quarter inch toward Mira with one finger, then pulled it back.

“Who was it addressed to,” he said, though he knew. He had known since the funeral, when their father had come to him in the dream and said, in the clipped voice he used when he was being unfair, *Don’t you dare open that without her.* “Both of you. Not one. Both.”

Mira looked up. Her eyes were the same gray as their father’s, and they had the same habit of softening at the corners when she was trying not to cry, which made her look, in moments of strain, uncannily like him.

“I read it,” she said.

Lev’s hand stopped in midair over the envelope.

“You read it,” he said.

“Last week. After the call with Lena. After you said — what you said.”

He swallowed. “What did I say.”

“You said I was using the letter as a wall.”

She had been, and she knew it, and she hated that he had known it too, and she hated that he had said it out loud, and she hated most of all that he had been right.

The coffee came. Two white cups, a small steel pot, two creamers in a little ceramic dish shaped like a leaf. The waitress set them down with the concentration of a surgeon and disappeared again.

Mira poured for both of them, the way their mother had always poured, the cup tilted, the stream thin, the pot lifted at the last moment so the drips fell back into the pot and not onto the cloth. Their mother had been dead fourteen years. Her hands had been the hands of a woman who was always cold, even in summer. Mira had her hands.

Lev wrapped his around the cup and did not drink.

“What does it say,” he said.

Mira looked at him across the flickering light. The trucker shifted in his sleep. The couple by the window laughed softly at something, a small private sound.

“It says,” Mira said, and then she stopped, because what the letter said was not, in the end, the thing that mattered, and what mattered was not, in the end, a thing that could be said. The letter said their father was sorry. The letter said he had not known how to be sorry in his life, only in his writing, and that he was sorry for that too. The letter said the house should be sold and the money divided, though not equally, because he had left the dining room table to Lev and the Chekhov to Mira, and he wanted them to argue about that, because arguing about small things had always been easier than talking about the large ones. The letter said, in the second-to-last paragraph, *I did my best. I know that isn’t enough. I’m giving it to you anyway, the way I gave you everything, which is to say, with my eyes mostly closed, which is the only way I knew how to love.*

But the last paragraph was the one that had made Mira put the letter back in its sleeve and carry it around in her bag for a week, taking it out in parking lots and on park benches, smoothing the crease in the envelope where she had bent it in her hand.

*The last paragraph said, Do not open this until you are both ready to forgive me, and do not be ready to forgive me until you have both forgiven each other, because I know which of you I wronged worse, and it was not the one you think.*

Lev was watching her. He had the look he used to get in the back seat on long drives, when they were children and their parents were fighting in the front, the look that said *I am here, I am listening, I am not going to make this worse.*

“Mira,” he said, and his voice had the careful gentleness he used with his students when they were on the edge of getting an answer. “Did he — did he say anything about —”

“Yes,” she said. “He said a lot of things.”

She could see his shoulders tighten under his jacket. He had put on weight in the last year, or maybe he had always been this size and she had only ever seen him in the forgiving light of their father’s kitchen, where everything was softened, even the truth.

“Tell me,” he said.

She shook her head. “No.”

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. “You read it without me. You won’t even — Mira. I drove three hours.”

“I know,” she said. “I took a train.”

The fluorescents hummed. The freight was gone and the windows were still again. Outside, a man in a reflective vest was hosing down the platform, and the water made a sound like a whisper that couldn’t quite make itself heard.

Lev picked up the envelope. He held it in both hands, the way their father used to hold a book he loved, with the spine toward his chest and the pages fanned gently open. He looked at Mira over the top of it.

“If I open it,” he said, “will you tell me what it said?”

“If you open it,” she said, “you’ll know what it said.”

He almost smiled. Almost. The corner of his mouth moved, the way a flag moves in a wind that isn’t quite strong enough to be called a wind.

“You’re impossible,” he said.

“Learned from the best,” she said, and then, because the hour was late and the coffee was cooling and they were, after all, two people who had come from the same place, she added, “Dad would have hated that we met here.”

Lev looked around the dining car, at the booths with their cracked vinyl, at the clock on the wall that was twelve minutes slow, at the little ceramic leaf with its two creamers, at the waitress reading a paperback at the counter, her finger marking her place.

“No,” he said. “He would have loved it. He would have said it had *atmosphere*.”

She let out a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sob, and he reached across the table and took her hand, and she let him, and they sat like that for a long moment, the envelope between them, their father’s last sealed sentence waiting in its creamy paper for the only thing it had ever required, which was not courage, exactly, but its quieter cousin, the willingness to be seen.

The waitress glanced up from her book. She did not bring the check. She had the sense, born of long nights in long rooms, to know when a table was not yet finished with its meal.
