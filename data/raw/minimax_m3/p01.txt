# The Weight of Eleven Seconds

Mara had wound the public clock of Vellmouth every Tuesday and Friday for nineteen years. She knew it the way a mother knows the sound of a particular cough in another room: the slight hitch in the second hand as it passed the nine, the way the minute hand dragged near the three like a man reluctant to leave a warm place. The clock was not beautiful. It was brass and iron, bolted to the south face of the old customs house, and it had kept the town’s hours since the year her grandfather was born.

On a Tuesday in late October, she arrived at half past six with her ladder, her oils, and her rag. The keeper before her, a man named Sullivan, had been dead three years. No one had replaced him because no one had thought to. Mara was the lighthouse keeper’s daughter; she had time, and patience, and no children to mind her.

She climbed. She wound. She checked the hands against her pocket watch, which she set every morning by the postal clock in the square. Eleven seconds slow. She frowned, wound it again, set it precisely. She descended. She signed the logbook in the customs house basement. She went home to her tea.

On Friday, the same: eleven seconds slow. She set it again. She checked the escapement. She oiled the pivots. The mechanism was sound. The weight hung true. The chimes struck the quarter hours without hesitation.

The following Tuesday, again. Eleven seconds.

She told no one, because in Vellmouth a person does not trouble others with what cannot be seen. She told the clock.

"All right," she said, her hand on its warm face. "We will not speak of this."

The losses kept their rhythm: exactly eleven seconds, twice a week, the days she wound it. She began to suspect herself. Perhaps her hand was less steady than she believed. Perhaps she was winding against a tooth that had shifted. She borrowed a level from the carpenter. She checked the plumb line. The clock was true. The losses continued.

In December, the losses began on the days she did not come. A woman at the post office remarked that the clock had been “fussy” over the weekend. Mara went on a Sunday. It was nine seconds slow. On Monday, without her touching it, eleven.

She sat on the sea wall that evening and watched the gulls argue over a fish head and tried to think clearly. The clock was a thing of gears. It could not know a thing. It could not know a person. It could only measure what was given to it. And yet the losses had a quality she could not name: they came in clusters, then lapses, then clusters again, as if whatever was being measured arrived in waves, like weather, or grief.

She began to keep a notebook. She wrote nothing in it. She only made a small mark for each loss, and beside the mark, the date and the time. She took to sitting in the customs house basement in the hours when the clock was slowest, listening to its muffled tick through the floorboards above. Once, she fell asleep there, and dreamed she was inside the mechanism, and the great brass wheel was a kind of sea, and the pallets were fish that opened and closed their mouths around the escapement, and the time between them was a long held breath.

She woke, and went up, and the clock had lost fourteen seconds.

In January, a boy fell through the ice on the harbor. He was pulled out alive, blue and screaming. That week the clock lost eleven seconds on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday. Mara wrote the dates in her notebook. She wrote nothing about the boy.

In February, the council decided to sell the customs house. The meeting was held in the schoolroom. Mara was not invited, but she was told by a woman who mended nets. The decision was made on a Wednesday evening. The clock lost eleven seconds at three in the morning, before anyone in the town was awake.

This is when she began to be afraid.

She went to Father Edden, who was old and not unkind. She told him there was something wrong with the clock. He said clocks were not his province. She told him it was losing time before things happened. He looked at her for a long moment, the way one looks at a child who has said something strange. He told her to come back if she saw a ghost. She went home and did not sleep.

In March, her daughter Aileen, who lived in the city, came for a visit. Aileen was thirty-four and worked in an office and had a careful way of speaking, as if language were a thing that could cut. She said, Mother, you look tired. Mara said, I am fine. Aileen said, the clock looks tired, too. Mara said nothing.

On the Sunday before Aileen was to leave, they walked along the front. The sea was the color of old tin. Aileen said she had been offered a position in another city, farther away. She said she had not yet decided. Mara heard herself say, You will not take it. Aileen said, I have not decided. Mara said, You will not. The words were in her mouth before she knew she would speak them. She heard the wrongness of them. She knew, even as she said them, that they were the kind of thing one says in order to make a person do the opposite of what one wishes.

Aileen left on the Monday. The clock lost eleven seconds that night, though Mara had not wound it, and no one in the town had made any decision she knew of.

She went to the customs house. She climbed the ladder. She put her hand on the brass face and felt the cold of it, the weight of it, the patience of it. She thought: the clock is not measuring what people do. It is measuring what they will wish they had not done. It is measuring the shape of a future regret, and the shape has a mass, and the mass slows the mechanism, the way a tide slows a ship.

She thought: and so it was my own regret, even before Aileen decided. The regret was already there, in me, the moment I spoke. The clock felt it, and answered it, and lost its eleven seconds.

She thought: or perhaps the clock is the town, and the town is the clock, and we have all been wound together so long that it knows us the way we know our own hands.

She descended. She sat in the basement. She took her notebook from her coat. She turned to the page where she had marked the day of the council meeting. She had written nothing beside the mark. She took her pen.

She wrote: *Sold to the Hargreaves brothers. They mean to turn it into a fish shop. I did not go to the meeting. I did not speak. But I knew, before they decided, that I would grieve.*

She read the words. She thought of the boy in the harbor, and the way his mother had screamed, and the way the scream had not yet happened on the day the clock first lost its eleven seconds for him. She thought of the woman at the post office, whose husband had left her on a Tuesday, and who had stood with her hand on the counter as if the counter were a shore.

She thought of her own mother, who had said once, on a day like any other, I will not go to the wedding. And had not gone, and had regretted it every day of her life, though the bride was Mara’s sister and the wedding was in the next town and the missing was small and ordinary. The regret was not small. The regret was a weight, and the weight had a name, and the name was *I knew.*

Mara closed the notebook. She folded her hands on it. She sat in the basement of the customs house and listened to the clock above her, ticking its patient, slowed tick, and she understood, at last, that the clock was not losing time. It was keeping a different kind of time. The kind that begins before the deed, in the body of the one who will wish it undone. The kind that has no chimes, because it cannot announce itself. The kind that only the mechanism knows, and the mechanism only, and the woman who winds it, who has been winding it for nineteen years, and who has, in all that time, never once wound it forward.

She went home. She made tea. She watched the sea from her window. The clock in the square struck six, and the sound was a little late, and a little heavy, and she did not correct it.

In the spring, the customs house was sold. The clock remained, by order of the council, who could not agree on its removal. Mara continued to wind it. The losses continued. She kept the notebook. She told no one. The town kept its hours, and the hours were a little less than they had been, and no one could say why, and no one asked.
