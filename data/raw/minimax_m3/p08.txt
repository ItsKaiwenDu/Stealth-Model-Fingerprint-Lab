# The Long Hall

The light in the hallway was the color of paper that had been left out too long. Not yellow exactly, more the white that comes after yellow, when the sun has had a long time to do what it wants. There were no windows along the part where my mother sat, so the light came only from the bulbs in the long fixtures above, each one behind a plastic cover that had gone gray at the edges. The bulbs hummed a little. I could feel the hum in my teeth if I pressed my tongue against the roof of my mouth.

My mother had told me to stay on the bench. She had said this three times. The first time we sat down, the second time when she stood up to look at the paper taped to the wall by the water fountain, and the third time when a man in a dark coat walked past us and she took my hand and then let go of it again. After the third time, she went through one of the doors, and I heard the latch click behind her like a small silver thing being set down on a table.

The bench was made of wood that had been painted so many times the paint had become its own kind of wood, a hard, slick layer on top of the other layers, and if I ran my fingernail along the edge I could peel a tiny curl of it up. I did this for a while. I made a small pile of curls on the bench next to me. They looked like shavings from a very small animal. I put them in a row and counted them. There were eleven. Then there were twelve, because one of them broke in half when I tried to pick it up.

A woman came and sat down on the other end of the bench. She had a purse that she set on her lap, and the purse had a metal clasp that caught the light when she shifted. She did not look at me. She looked at the doors. There were four of them down the hall, all the same, all closed, each with a small window of wire glass in the upper half. Behind the glass of the first one I could see the top of someone's head moving. Behind the second one I could see nothing. Behind the third one someone was standing with one hand on a chair back. Behind the fourth one I could see a sliver of my mother's coat, the dark red one with the buttons that looked like small flat stones.

I knew which one was my mother by her coat. I knew her by the way the coat moved when she breathed. I had been watching my mother breathe for as long as I could remember, and I knew the exact height of her shoulders when she was listening, and the way her chin tilted when she was about to say something she had already decided to say. I knew the way she looked at her hands when she was afraid, which was the same way she looked at her hands when she was tired, which was the same way she looked at her hands most of the time lately, as if her hands were something she had been given to hold and was not yet sure of.

The woman with the purse shifted again. The metal clasp made a small sound. A man came out of the second door, holding a folder against his chest, and walked past us without looking at either of us. His shoes made a sound on the floor that was not quite a sound, more a feeling under my shoes when he passed, a small vibration that came up through the floor and into my legs.

I got up from the bench. I was not supposed to, but I went to the water fountain and pressed the bar and a thin stream of water came out that was cold in a way that surprised me each time, even though I had done it four times now. The water hit the metal basin and made a sound like a small bell being struck with something soft. I drank three times and wiped my mouth with the back of my hand. The back of my hand smelled like the inside of my pocket, which was a smell I knew very well, a warm small smell.

I went back to the bench. The woman was still there. She had taken a tissue out of her purse and was holding it folded in her lap. She still did not look at me. I thought about saying something to her, but I could not think of anything that was true, so I said nothing.

The third door opened, and a man came out. He was the man who had walked past us earlier. He went to the woman with the purse and said something to her in a voice that was low and even, the kind of voice that is meant to be heard only by the person it is directed at. The woman nodded. She put the tissue in her purse and closed the clasp. They went through the third door together, and it closed behind them.

I was alone in the hallway. The humming of the lights was louder now, or maybe it was just that there was no other sound. I could hear a clock somewhere, but I could not see it, and I could not tell where the sound was coming from. It ticked in a way that did not match the humming, the way two people walking together sometimes do not match their steps.

I peeled another curl of paint from the bench. I made it the thirteenth. I lined it up with the others. I thought about my mother behind the door, and I thought about what she was doing, and I did not know. I knew she was in a room with other people. I knew there was a table in the room because I had seen the table from the hallway, the edge of it, the wood a darker color than the bench, with rings on it from cups. I knew there were papers. I knew my mother did not like papers, but she carried them anyway, in a folder that was green and had a bend in one corner from being overfull.

I knew my mother was afraid. I knew this because of the way she had held my hand and then let it go. People hold on to things they are afraid of losing. I had learned this from watching her hold my hand in parking lots, in stores, in the doorway of our apartment when the hallway light was out. She held on tight when she was afraid. When she let go, it meant she had decided to do something, or she had been surprised, or she had run out of the kind of strength that holding requires.

The fourth door opened. My mother came out. She was not looking at the floor this time. She was looking ahead, at the hallway, at the place where I was sitting on the bench with my small pile of paint curls. She walked toward me and she did not hurry, and she did not slow down. When she reached me, she sat down on the bench next to me and she did not put her arm around me. She sat with her hands in her lap, the same way the woman with the purse had sat, the same way all the people in this hallway seemed to sit, as if their hands were objects they were keeping safe.

She looked at the curls on the bench.

She said, "What are those."

I said, "Paint."

She said, "I see that."

I said, "I peeled them off."

She said, "I see that too."

We sat there for a while. The clock ticked. The lights hummed. I could hear, through the wall, the sound of someone turning pages. I could hear, further away, a phone ring twice and then stop.

My mother said, "We have to wait a little more."

I said, "Okay."

She said, "There's a vending machine downstairs. Do you want something."

I said, "What kind."

She said, "I don't know. The kind they have."

I thought about this. I did not know what kind they had. I thought about going to see. I thought about the stairs, which I had seen when we came in, the wide ones with the metal strips on the edges. I thought about my mother sitting on the bench by herself while I went. I thought about the paint curls, which I would have to leave behind, or put in my pocket, which I did not want to do because they would get crushed.

I said, "Maybe later."

She said, "All right."

She did not ask me what I had done while she was gone. She did not ask me if I had been scared. She did not ask me anything. She sat with her hands in her lap and her shoulders at the height I knew, and she looked at the doors, all four of them, each one with its small window, each one with its own piece of what was happening on the other side.

I looked at the doors too. I could see, through the first one, a hand resting on the table. I could see, through the second, the back of a chair. I could see, through the third, a shadow moving across the wire glass. I could not see through the fourth anymore. Someone had pulled a blind down on the inside.

I put my hand next to my mother's hand on the bench. I did not put it on top of hers. I put it next to hers, so that our smallest fingers were almost touching, and the paint on the bench was cool under my palm, and the bench went on underneath both of us, the same bench, the same hard slick paint, all the layers of it, the old ones underneath and the new ones on top, and the light in the hallway stayed the color it had been, and the clock kept not matching the lights, and somewhere a door opened and closed and opened and closed, and my mother did not move her hand, and I did not move mine, and we waited.
