**The Still Bridge**

The fog had not yet lifted from the water when Thomas Grady reached the foot of the bridge. He was carrying two coils of new rope over one shoulder, meant for the barge *Elsie May*, which was supposed to load barley at the north dock by seven o'clock. He did not expect to find a locked gate across the bridge entrance, nor a police constable standing behind it with his hands folded across his chest.

"Can't cross," the constable said.

Grady set the ropes down on the cobblestones. "What do you mean, can't cross? I've got cargo."

"Bridge is closed until further notice."

"By whose order?"

The constable shifted his weight and looked past Grady toward the empty street. "That's all I've been told, Mr. Grady. Closed until further notice. You can take the ferry around the bend if you need to get to the north side."

Grady stared at the bridge. It was a long iron truss structure, five years old, the pride of the port commission. It had been open the night before. He could see the far side through the fog—the warehouses, the flour mill, the stack of new lumber beside the chandlery. Nothing seemed amiss. No damage. No work crews. Just the gate and the constable.

He picked up his ropes and walked toward the wharf.

Down at the east dock, the morning was already well underway. Steamers from upriver had arrived in the dark, and the air smelled of coal smoke and wet hemp. Dockworkers were unloading crates of canned peaches from the *Thomas Hardy*, and the foreman, a thick-necked man named Callahan, was counting them off a clipboard.

"The bridge is shut," Grady said, setting the ropes on a barrel.

Callahan did not look up. "What?"

"The bridge. They've locked the gate. Constable says it's closed until further notice."

Callahan finally looked at him. "That's a joke."

"It's not. I just came from there."

Callahan chewed the inside of his cheek. Then he called over to a man named Piatek, who was rolling a handcart toward the gangway. "You hear anything about the bridge?"

Piatek shook his head. "No. Why?"

"It's closed," Grady said again.

Piatek looked out across the river as if he might see the bridge for himself. He could not; the warehouses blocked the view. He shrugged and went back to his cart. The information moved no further through him. He had cargo to move, and the bridge was not his problem.

By half past six, the story had begun to change.

At Maren's Grocery, on the corner of Second and Canal, old Mrs. Maren was opening her shutters when a drayman named Ellison pulled his wagon up to the curb and asked if she had heard about the bridge. She had not. He told her it was closed, that he had seen a police officer guarding the gate, and that he had heard—though he could not say from whom—that a section of the ironwork had cracked in the night.

"Cracked?" Mrs. Maren said.

"That's what I was told."

She looked at him with the particular expression she reserved for information she did not trust. "Well," she said. "I suppose someone will tell us when it's safe."

Ellison bought a tin of coffee and drove off toward the south warehouse district, where the closure was not yet known. When he arrived at the loading yard and told the men there, he said the bridge had been shut because the iron was failing. A man named Voss, who had worked on the original construction, said that was nonsense—the bridge was sound, he had helped rivet the main span himself. Another man said he had heard the closure was ordered by the port commission, not the police, and that it had something to do with a survey. Someone else mentioned a rumor about a boat striking a pier in the night, though no one could say which pier or which boat.

The men argued about it while they hitched their teams. The argument did not resolve anything. They went to work.

At the customs house, on the south end of town, a clerk named Avery was sorting manifests when his supervisor, Mr. Dawson, came through the door and asked if the north dock manifests had arrived. They had not. Avery explained that the bridge was closed and that several clerks lived on the north side.

Dawson set his hat on the desk. "Closed? Who closed it?"

"No one seems to know, sir."

"Is it the commission?"

"I couldn't say. There's been no memo."

Dawson frowned. He walked to the window and looked out at the river. A steamer was tying up at the government wharf. Two men in dark coats were descending the gangway. Dawson did not recognize them. He watched them cross the yard and disappear toward the post office. He turned back to Avery.

"If the manifests don't come through by nine, send someone down to the ferry. And send a note to the commission asking for clarification."

"Clarification about what, sir? We don't know what the order is."

Dawson considered this. "Then ask what the order is."

The note would not reach the commission until after ten o'clock. The commission's secretary, a careful man named Hall, would read it and reply that no formal order regarding the bridge had been issued by the commission. This reply would satisfy no one, because it contradicted what everyone had seen with their own eyes: the gate, the constable, the silence from the far side.

By midmorning, the ferry was overcrowded. The ferryman, a German named Kolb, was making twice his usual trips and charging nothing extra because he did not want trouble. Passengers pressed together on the open deck—women with market baskets, a boy leading a mule, two traveling salesmen with sample cases. Most of them had heard some version of the story. The iron had cracked. The bridge was being inspected. The bridge was condemned. A man had fallen through a plank. The police had locked it for reasons they would not state.

No one knew the truth, because there was no single truth to know. There was only the locked gate and the officer who would not explain it. Every retelling added a detail or removed one, shaped the rumor to the teller's own fears. The ironworkers feared it was the structure. The merchants feared it was permanent. The travelers feared they would be stranded. The officials feared they would be blamed.

At noon, the fog burned off and the river turned bright and indifferent under the sun. From the north dock, a man could stand at the end of the wharf and see the bridge clearly—its long, clean lines, its unbroken deck, the locked gate shining in the light. He could see the constable, still standing there. He could see nothing wrong.

Across the water, the town went on with its business as best it could. Carts were rerouted. Goods were ferried. Conversations continued in doorways and behind counters, and each one carried the same quiet, unsettled question, unspoken but shared: what did it mean that something so solid could be taken away so suddenly, with no word and no reason, and that no one in authority could—or would—say why?

The question hung in the air like the last of the morning fog. It found no answer. The bridge stayed shut, and the town moved around it the way water moves around a stone.
