**The Last Supper Car**

The fluorescent lights of the Coda Station Bistro hummed with the particular loneliness of 2 a.m. Most of the tables stood like empty thrones under the glare. Out the plate-glass windows, the tracks gleamed wetly under platform lamps, converging into a dark, singular vanishing point.

Mara arrived first. She wore the same efficient trench coat she’d worn to their father’s funeral five years ago, her suitcase parked primly by her ankles. She ordered black coffee. When Leo shuffled in, ten minutes late, he wore a faded band t-shirt and carried a backpack that slumped like a tired animal. He smelled faintly of diesel and cheap cigarettes.

“Bourbon,” he said to the waitress, without looking up. “Neat.”

Mara’s mouth tightened. “We’re not here to get drunk, Leo.”

“Aren’t we?” He finally met her eyes across the Formica table. “You called. Said Dad left… instructions. A cryptic summons to the middle of nowhere to discuss a piece of paper. Sounds like a drinking occasion to me.”

The waitress set the drinks down. Mara wrapped both hands around her mug, as if for warmth. The letter lay between them on the table, a pale rectangle in a pale envelope. It was unaddressed, unsealed, but held closed with a single strip of yellowed tape. Their father’s handwriting, a bold, impatient scrawl, covered the front in three words: *FOR MARA AND LEO*.

“He was explicit,” Mara said, her voice low and measured. “We open it here, together. After that, we decide. Together.”

“Decide what? Whether to finally put him in the ground properly?” Leo took a gulp of his bourbon. The insult hung in the air, sharp as broken glass. They’d had him cremated, as per his own pre-paid plan. There was no ground. It was a pointless barb, and they both knew it.

“Whether to honor his last wish,” Mara corrected, her gaze fixed on the letter. “Which was for it to remain sealed if we couldn’t be in the same room, civilly, to open it. If we failed… he wanted it destroyed. Unread.”

Leo laughed, a short, ugly sound. “So this is it? The final test? The bastard’s grading our civility from beyond the grave? What’s the pass mark? Do we have to shake hands? Sing ‘Kumbaya’?”

“It’s not a test,” Mara said, though her knuckles were white around the mug. “It’s an act of trust.”

“Trust?” Leo leaned forward, the table creaking. “Trust? He didn’t trust us to run the business. He didn’t trust us with the house. He didn’t trust either of us to be happy, not really. But he trusted us with *this*?” He gestured at the envelope with his glass. “What could possibly be in there that’s so important it requires this… theatrical séance in a train station?”

“That’s the point, Leo. We don’t know.” Mara pushed the letter an inch closer to the center of the table. It was a microscopic retreat. “That’s why he did it this way. To make us stop. To make us be here.”

They fell silent. The only sounds were the hum of the lights and the distant, mournful clatter of wheels on track switches. A cleaning crew drifted past the window, their bright jackets like ghosts.

Leo sighed, the anger seeming to deflate out of him, leaving something tired and older. “He was always like this. Grand gestures. Theatrics. Remember the time he bought that condemned lighthouse? Said it was our legacy. He never even climbed the stairs.”

“He had ideas,” Mara said softly.

“He had demands.” Leo drained his glass and signaled for another. “He demanded we run the factory like it was 1950. He demanded we live in that drafty old house. He demanded we be… him. And when we weren’t, he just… turned us off. Like a faulty machine.” He looked at her, a raw edge to his voice. “You know. You were his golden child. Until you weren’t.”

Mara’s face went still. “I was the one who stayed. I was the one who listened, and learned, and tried to do it his way. It wasn’t golden, Leo. It was just… quieter. And when I finally said I couldn’t, that I wanted to sell the plant, to sell everything… the look he gave me. It was the same one he gave you the day you left. I just got it later.”

This was the silence they always circled around, the unspoken geography of their estrangement. The factory, the house, the entire gravitational pull of their father’s world. Leo had shattered against it at eighteen and run. Mara had absorbed it for twenty years, trying to bend it to her own will, before it broke her too. They were two satellites who’d orbited the same dying star, flung into opposite voids.

“Maybe it’s a confession,” Leo murmured, his eyes on the damp tracks. “Maybe he embezzled his own company into bankruptcy. Maybe he killed the neighbor’s dog in ’87.”

“Or maybe it’s just a letter,” Mara said. “An explanation. Or an apology. Or nothing at all. Does it matter what’s inside? The decision is the same. Do we open it, or do we let him win one last time? Do we let the rule—the seal—keep us apart, even now?”

Leo stared at her. He saw the fine lines of fatigue around her eyes, the stubborn set of her jaw. It was his jaw, too. He saw the brother she’d once called on the phone every Sunday, and the stranger he’d let her become.

“Win?” he repeated. “What does winning even mean, Mara? We’re sitting in a cheap diner at three in the morning, arguing over a piece of paper from a man who’s been dead for half a decade. There’s no winning. There’s just… this.”

He reached out. His finger hovered over the edge of the envelope. “You want to open it. You need to. Don’t pretend this is for me.”

“And you want to throw it in the first incinerator you find,” she countered. “Don’t pretend you don’t. You need to reject it. To reject him.”

“You’re damn right I do.” His hand clenched into a fist on the table. “He spent my whole life telling me I wasn’t good enough. That I was weak. That I’d fail. And this… this is the ultimate trick. ‘Here’s a puzzle, kids. Solve it together!’ He didn’t think we could. He knew we’d tear each other apart fighting over it. That was the point. To prove, from the grave, that we were the failures he always said we were.”

The waitress brought Leo’s second bourbon. They watched her leave, a bubble of normalcy in their strange tableau.

“What if you’re wrong?” Mara’s voice was barely a whisper. “What if the whole point was to finally give us something we had to do together? Something he couldn’t control the outcome of? He couldn’t open it. He had to let go. Let go of the decision. Let go of *us*.” She took a breath. “I’m so tired of his rules, Leo. This one included. I don’t want to destroy it. But I don’t want to open it alone, or not at all. I just… I want it to be our choice. Not his. Ours. For once.”

Leo looked from her face to the letter. The seal. The simple strip of tape that held all their history, all their father’s gravity, in place. He could feel the old, familiar rebellion bubbling in his chest. The urge to rip, to burn, to run.

But he looked at his sister. Really looked. He saw not the enemy, but the other casualty. The one who’d taken the slow hits instead of the clean break.

He picked up the butter knife from the place setting. The metal was dull, scratched. He turned it over in his hands.

“A wise man—or a very drunk one—once told me,” Leo said, his voice rough, “that sometimes the only way to stop a ghost from haunting you is to give it what it wants. And then tell it to go to hell.”

Mara watched him, not breathing.

He slid the dull edge of the knife under the tape. It resisted for a moment, then gave way with a dry, tearing sound. The sound of a rule being broken. The sound of a lock turning.

He didn’t open the envelope. He pushed it, with the knife’s tip, across the wet ring on the table until it sat directly in front of her.

The choice. The first, real one.

Mara looked at the opened seal, then at her brother’s face. He gave a single, weary nod.

Her fingers trembled slightly as she reached for the flap. She pulled out a single sheet of paper, also folded. It was heavier than standard stationery, with a faint watermark. She unfolded it.

Leo leaned in, the smell of bourbon and diesel suddenly sharp. The note was short, written in that same bold, impatient script.

It was not an explanation. It was not an apology.

It was a list. Three names, followed by phone numbers and brief notes.
*Dr. Aris Thorne – Pain Management – He helped me. Might help you.*
*Elizabeth – Cafe Le Soleil, Marseille – Tell her Leo sent you. She’ll have room.*
*Mr. Henderson – Lawyer – Enclosed is the deed to the house. Do with it what you will. It is yours.*

At the bottom, a single line:
*I was wrong about a great many things. Be happy.*

The silence that followed was different. It wasn’t empty. It was full of the weight of a simple, staggering gesture. An apology written not in words of regret, but in the act of finally, truly letting go. Not to a shared idea of family, but to the two flawed, hurting individuals he’d left behind.

Leo read it again. He thought of Marseille. Of a name he didn’t know. Of a pain he did.

Mara folded the letter carefully along its original creases and placed it in her trench coat pocket. She looked out the window. The first grey hint of dawn was softening the edge of the sky, turning the tracks from silver to steel.

“It’s not enough,” she said quietly.

“No,” Leo agreed. “It’s not.”

“Is it a start?”

He drained the last of his second bourbon. He thought of his father’s hands, big and calloused, teaching him how to hold a wrench. The memory was no longer a chain.

“It’s something,” he said. He pulled a few crumpled bills from his pocket and tossed them on the table. He stood, shouldering his backpack. “The 5:15 to the coast goes both ways. You heading that direction?”

Mara looked at him, a long, evaluating look. “I don’t know,” she said. And for the first time, it didn’t sound like an evasion. It sounded like the truth.

“Okay,” Leo said. He nodded, a gesture that encompassed the table, the letter in her pocket, the hum of the lights, the vast, quiet distance between them. “Well. I’ll be on the platform. If you’re there, you’re there.”

He turned and walked out of the Bistro, not looking back. The bell above the door jingled his exit.

Mara sat alone for a long time. She touched the pocket where the letter lay. She watched the tracks, waiting for the sun.
