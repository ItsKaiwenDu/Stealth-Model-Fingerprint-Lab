**The Soft Paper of Official Things**

The carpet in the hallway was the color of oatmeal and felt rough through the thin soles of Maya’s school shoes. She scuffed her toe along a dark line where the fibers were crushed flat. The corridor stretched on in both directions, long and bright under humming tubes of light. Doors of heavy wood were set into the walls, each with a small, tarnished number. Most were closed.

She could smell old paper and something lemony, like the cleaner Mom used on the kitchen counter.

Dad stood a few feet away, looking at his phone. His thumb moved back and forth, fast. His suit jacket was on, buttoned, and his shoulders looked high and stiff inside it, like a hanger was still in there. He kept pulling at the cuff of his left sleeve, the one that was a little too long.

Mom was on the bench beside Maya. She wasn’t looking at her phone. Her hands were folded in her lap, tight, and her ring glinted. She smelled different today. Not like her usual soap, but like flowers that were trying too hard.

A man in a dark suit came out of a door far down the hall. His footsteps made a sharp, clapping sound. He nodded at Dad, not at Mom. Dad put his phone in his pocket and walked over. They didn’t shake hands. They stood very close and spoke. Maya couldn’t hear the words, only the low, humming sound of their voices. The man in the suit kept touching the side of his own neck.

“Maya, sit up straight,” Mom said, not looking at her.

Maya pulled her knees together. She watched a scuff mark on the far wall that looked like a rabbit. The air was quiet except for the humming lights and the distant echo of other voices.

The man in the suit said something, and Dad turned his head a little, his eyes flicking toward Mom and Maya. Then he turned back. The man in the suit smiled, but it was a small, tight smile that didn’t touch his eyes. He put his hand on Dad’s shoulder for a second. Dad’s shoulders went down a little.

The man went back through his door. Dad walked back to them, but he didn’t sit down. He stood looking at the floor.

“Was that Mr. Henderson?” Mom asked. Her voice was very smooth.

Dad just nodded. He kept his hands in his pockets.

Another door opened, farther away. A woman with a thick file under her arm hurried past them, her heels clicking. She didn’t look at them at all.

“Can we go now?” Maya asked. She had to use the bathroom, and her legs were falling asleep.

“Soon,” Mom said. She reached over and smoothed Maya’s hair back from her forehead. Her fingers were cold. “Just a few more minutes. Be good.”

She took her hand away. Maya wanted to wipe her forehead where her mom had touched it.

The heavy door right next to them opened with a soft *thunk*. A different man came out. He was older, with gray hair and glasses on a chain around his neck. He held a folder. He looked at them, his eyes resting on each of their faces in turn.

“Mr. and Mrs. Liu?” he said.

Dad and Mom stood up at the same time. “Yes,” Dad said.

“This way, please. Maya, you can come in too.”

The room inside was smaller than Maya thought it would be. There was a big table that was shiny and dark, and three chairs behind it. But they didn’t sit there. Two chairs were on one side of the table, one on the other. The man with the glasses sat on the other side. He put the folder down. It made a soft, heavy sound.

“Sit,” he said, so they did.

Maya sat between them. The chair was big and leather, and it squeaked when she moved. She looked at the table. There were little scratches in the wood that looked like tiny rivers.

The man opened the folder. He took out papers. Lots of pages. Some had writing. Some had boxes. He slid one across the table. “Here are the latest terms,” he said.

Dad leaned forward. He picked up the page. Mom leaned in too, her hair brushing Dad’s arm. They read it together, their heads close. The page was very white.

“This is almost the same as last week,” Mom said, pointing to a spot near the bottom. Her finger had a little bit of red polish chipped off the nail.

“Paragraph four,” the man said. “We’ve adjusted the figure there. And the schedule on page six.”

Dad took a pen from his jacket. His hand was shaking, just a little. The tip of the pen hovered over the page. He looked at Mom. Mom looked at the page. She gave a tiny nod, not up and down, but a little forward, like she was moving something heavy an inch.

Dad wrote his name. The sound of the pen was loud in the quiet room. Then he pushed the page over to Mom. She wrote her name below his. The pen stopped. Her name looked very small.

“Good,” the man said. He took the page back. “And this one.” He slid another paper over. This one had a diagram on it, a circle with lines coming out of it.

Maya leaned closer. The lines connected the circle to boxes with words in them. The circle was labeled **Maya Liu**. Her name. Her eyes looked at the boxes. One said **Residential Parent**. She sounded the word out in her head. *Res-i-den-tial*. She didn’t know what it meant.

Mom took the pen again. She wrote something next to one box. Then she slid it to Dad. He wrote next to another box.

“This is just for the interim,” the man said. “We can revisit this once the final order is in place.”

*Interim.* Maya had heard that word. It was like a temporary thing. Like a band-aid before the real fix.

“What about summer?” Dad asked. His voice was quiet. “The camp is already paid for.”

“That would fall under section three,” the man said, flipping a page. “The agreed-upon vacation rotation.”

*Vacation rotation.* The words were soft and official, like the soft paper they were signing. They weren’t loud words like “go” or “stay” or “when can I see you.” They were words that folded time and people up into neat little lines on a page.

The man was talking about dates now. Months. “September through December.” “First and third weekends.” “Alternating holidays.” He was building a cage out of dates and weekends, and her name was in the center of it.

Maya slid down in the big chair. She could see her knees. She watched a piece of dust float across the shiny table, carried by a breath she couldn’t feel.

Then the man slid one last paper over. It was a long list. “Just to confirm the ancillary details,” he said. “Personal effects, the joint account closure schedule, forwarding address protocols…”

Mom and Dad didn’t read this one carefully. They just signed where the man pointed. Their pens moved fast, a scratchy dance. *Scratch. Scratch.* It was the sound of things being divided.

The man gathered all the papers. He tapped them on the table to make a neat stack and put them back in the folder. “That should be everything for today,” he said. He stood up. “We’ll file these this afternoon.”

Everyone stood up. The legs of the chairs screeched against the floor.

“Thank you, Mr. Chen,” Mom said.

In the doorway, Mom turned back to Maya. Her face was smooth. “Let’s go get your things from Grandma’s.”

“Okay,” Maya said.

As they walked back into the bright hallway, Maya took one last look into the quiet room. The three empty chairs. The shiny table with its tiny river scratches. The air that smelled like lemon and old paper.

The future was in that folder. It had lines and boxes and dates. It was soft, official paper. She could almost hear it rustling in the man’s hands, a quiet sound like wings, flying away without her.
