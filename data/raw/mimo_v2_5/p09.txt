A map, a recipe, and a legal contract each take a slice of the world and fold it into a compact set of symbols that can be handed from one mind to another. They are all acts of compression, yet the texture of what they keep, what they leave out, and how the people who read them fill in the gaps varies dramatically. By imagining a single concrete situation—a neighborhood that decides to turn a vacant lot into a shared garden, to cook a communal feast from its harvest, and to draft an agreement about how the food and the work will be divided—we can see how each representation carries its own logic of simplification and how its users must reconstruct the missing pieces.

The map of the garden is a visual shorthand. It reduces the tangled mess of soil, weeds, pathways, and surrounding fences to a set of lines, colors, and symbols that fit on a sheet of paper. What it preserves is spatial relationship: the relative size of the plot, the location of the water tap, the direction of the sun, the proximity of the street, and the boundaries that separate the garden from neighboring properties. These elements are crucial for anyone who needs to decide where to plant, where to walk, or where to place a bench. The map discards the texture of the soil, the smell of the earth, the sound of birds, and the history of the lot. It also strips away the temporal dimension—there is no indication of when the water runs dry in late summer or how the garden will change as plants grow. Users repair the ambiguity by walking the site, feeling the ground under their feet, and adjusting the plan when a shadow falls where a vegetable bed was imagined. A gardener might notice that a low spot collects water after rain and decide to dig a drainage ditch that the map never hinted at, thereby enriching the representation with lived experience.

A recipe translates the garden’s bounty into a sequence of actions that will turn raw ingredients into a dish. It preserves the essential chemistry of transformation: the quantities of each herb, the temperature of the oven, the timing of each step. The language of measurements and the order of operations are the core of what the recipe keeps, because they enable a cook to recreate a flavor profile that a group of neighbors agreed upon. What the recipe discards is the individual character of each leaf or fruit—whether a tomato is a bit more acidic, whether a carrot is sweeter than average, whether a basil leaf has a bruise. It also ignores the physical space of the kitchen, the availability of tools, and the personal preferences of the diners. Ambiguity in a recipe is repaired through tasting, adjusting, and improvising. If a cook discovers that the garden’s basil is more pungent than the recipe assumed, they may reduce the amount or add a pinch of sugar to balance the flavor. The act of tasting becomes a feedback loop that fills in the gaps left by the static text, turning the abstract instruction into a lived performance.

The legal contract is the most abstract of the three compressions. It reduces the garden project to a set of rights, duties, and remedies. It preserves the intent of the parties—who will own the plot, who will be responsible for weeding, how the harvested vegetables will be shared, what happens if someone fails to fulfill their obligations. The contract also captures the consequences of breach, the mechanisms for dispute resolution, and the duration of the arrangement. What it discards is the sensory reality of the garden, the personal relationships that evolve over seasons, and the informal gestures of goodwill that often keep a community together. It also omits the day‑to‑day variability of weather, pests, or sudden illness that can affect performance. Ambiguity in a contract is repaired through interpretation, negotiation, and, when necessary, judicial reasoning. If the contract states that each family will receive an equal share of the harvest but does not define what “equal” means when one family grows only lettuce while another grows tomatoes, the parties may refer to the original intent, consult the map to see the size of each plot, or bring in an external mediator. In this way, the contract’s users must constantly translate the sparse language back into the concrete details of the garden and the lives of its caretakers.

Consider the concrete example of the neighborhood garden that spans a modest quarter‑acre, bordered by a low fence on the north and a sidewalk on the east. The map shows a rectangular plot divided into four quadrants, a central path, a water spigot near the southeast corner, and a small tool shed in the southwest. The recipe calls for a summer salad that uses lettuce, radishes, carrots, and basil, each measured in cups and tablespoons, to be tossed with olive oil and lemon juice, then chilled for fifteen minutes before serving. The contract stipulates that each household will tend one quadrant, contribute labor for the weekly watering schedule, and receive an equal portion of the salad at the end of the season, with any surplus donated to the local food bank.

When a family looks at the map, they see that their quadrant lies on the side that receives the most afternoon sun, which suggests they should plant heat‑tolerant lettuce varieties. The map also shows the water spigot is a short walk away, so they plan to lay a hose that will reach their beds. However, the map cannot tell them that the soil in their quadrant is compacted and will need a layer of compost before planting. They discover this by turning the soil with a spade, a physical act that fills the gap left by the graphic representation. The recipe, in turn, tells them how many cups of lettuce they need for the salad, but it does not mention that their lettuce may be slightly bitter because of the sun exposure. By tasting a leaf, they decide to add a touch of honey to the dressing, an adjustment that the written instructions never foresaw. The contract, meanwhile, promises each family an equal share, yet it does not address the possibility that one family’s quadrant may produce a larger volume of lettuce due to better soil. When the harvest arrives, the families meet, compare the actual yields, and decide to balance the portions by exchanging surplus carrots for extra lettuce, thereby repairing the contract’s silence with a negotiated solution that reflects the lived reality of the garden.

Through this single scenario we see that the map compresses space into symbols, the recipe compresses time and process into a stepwise narrative, and the contract compresses social expectations into a legal framework. Each discards dimensions that are not central to its purpose—sensory texture in the map, individual variability in the recipe, and personal relationships in the contract. Yet none of these representations is self‑sufficient. Users must constantly move between the abstract and the concrete, using physical inspection, tasting, and negotiation to fill the gaps that the compression leaves behind. In doing so, they demonstrate that the act of following an instruction is never a passive reception of a pre‑made plan; it is an active reconstruction that blends the simplified model with the messy, ever‑changing world it seeks to describe. The three ways of compressing reality therefore function not as static decrees but as starting points for a dialogue between intention and experience, each one inviting its readers to become co‑authors of the lived outcome.
