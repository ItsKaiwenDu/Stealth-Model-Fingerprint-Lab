**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Office of the Provost and University Budget Committee
**FROM:** Office of Academic Affairs
**DATE:** May 22, 2025
**RE:** Proposal to Extend Library Hours During the Examination Period

---

**Purpose**

This memo evaluates the feasibility and desirability of keeping the campus library open around the clock during the final examination period, typically spanning two weeks each semester. The recommendation below is grounded in an analysis of the academic, financial, safety, and equity dimensions of the proposal. It concludes with a proposal for a limited pilot and the metrics by which the pilot should be judged.

---

**Learning Benefits**

There is a well-documented relationship between access to quiet study environments and student academic performance, particularly for students who do not have reliable housing or who share living spaces with others. A library that closes at 10 p.m. forces students into less ideal study settings—crowded dormitories, noisy off-campus cafés, or rooms without adequate lighting and resources. Extending hours would provide a dedicated, distraction-reduced space during a period when students most need it. Research from the Consortium of Academic and Research Libraries also indicates that when students report having 24-hour access to study facilities, their self-assessed confidence in exam preparation rises and their reported stress levels fall. While correlation is not causation, the weight of the evidence supports the proposition that more access to a structured study environment benefits learning outcomes. The university should not underestimate the psychological reassurance that an open library provides: the knowledge that a quiet room is available at any hour reduces anxiety and supports healthier sleep-wake cycles, even if the library is used only as an occasional refuge rather than a nightly destination.

---

**Staffing Considerations**

Extending library hours beyond the current midnight closing requires an investment in staffing. A small university typically operates with a lean library team of twelve to fifteen full-time employees, many of whom hold dual roles in instruction and technical services. During the examination period, the library would need at least two student workers and one professional staff member on duty during the overnight hours, from midnight to 7 a.m. This translates to an additional ninety-one person-hours per week for a two-week period. The estimated cost, including overtime premiums and student wages, is approximately $4,800. While this sum is modest relative to the university's total operating budget, it should be weighed against the opportunity cost of diverting student workers from other duties and the risk of burnout among professional staff. A rotating schedule that limits any individual staff member to no more than two overnight shifts would mitigate fatigue but would increase the size of the team needed. The library director has confirmed that the existing part-time and student workforce is sufficient if the pilot is confined to the examination period and does not become a permanent commitment.

---

**Safety and Security**

Opening the library overnight introduces safety considerations that require deliberate planning. After midnight, campus foot traffic drops sharply, and the library sits at the edge of a largely residential zone with limited lighting. The university's Department of Public Safety has indicated that it can provide one additional patrol officer during the overnight hours, at a cost of approximately $1,200 per week, to conduct regular checks of the library and its surrounding area. The library itself should be equipped with functional security cameras, an emergency call station, and a single controlled entry point that requires a valid student ID. These measures are consistent with the protocols used by peer institutions that already operate 24-hour library facilities during examinations. It is also essential that the university establish a clear policy for what happens when a student is the only person in the building: the library should close when the occupancy count falls below two, and the remaining student should be notified that a shuttle or escort service is available. The Department of Public Safety has expressed willingness to provide this service at no additional cost, drawing on its existing overnight roster.

---

**Equity and Access**

A 24-hour library is, at its core, an equity measure. Students who come from lower-income backgrounds are less likely to have private study space, reliable internet access at home, or family support structures that allow them to study in comfort. Students with caregiving responsibilities may only be able to study after their dependents are asleep. International students, who may be adjusting to different time zones or language pressures, often rely on extended library hours as a lifeline. Conversely, if the library remains closed at night, the students who suffer most are those who are already the most disadvantaged. The university's commitment to equity should be reflected not only in admissions and financial aid but also in the resources it provides during the most critical moments of the academic year. A 24-hour library is not a luxury; it is a statement about the kind of institution the university wishes to be.

---

**Displaced Costs and Opportunity Costs**

The direct costs of the pilot are manageable, but the university must also consider the indirect costs. The most significant displaced cost is the erosion of the library's operating budget, which is already stretched thin. If the pilot is successful and the program is expanded, the university may need to find additional revenue or reallocate funds from other departments. The library's budget committee should be consulted to determine whether any savings from the reduced daytime use of the library during examinations can be redirected to offset the nighttime costs. Additionally, the university should consider the opportunity cost of the pilot: the time and energy spent planning and evaluating the program could be directed elsewhere. This is a legitimate concern, but it should be weighed against the potential benefits. The pilot is deliberately limited in scope—two weeks, one semester—so that the opportunity cost remains contained.

---

**Pilot Design and Evaluation**

The recommendation is to proceed with a pilot program for the upcoming fall examination period, subject to the following conditions. First, the pilot should be limited to the final two weeks of the semester, with the library open from midnight to 7 a.m. on weekdays only. Second, the pilot should include a pre- and post-survey of students to measure their perceptions of the library's value during examinations, their study habits, and their stress levels. Third, the library should track occupancy data nightly to determine how many students actually use the facility during the overnight hours and at what times. Fourth, the pilot should include a safety review after the first week to determine whether any changes to security protocols are necessary. Fifth, the pilot should be evaluated by the Academic Affairs office in partnership with the library director, and a report should be submitted to the provost and the budget committee within thirty days of the pilot's conclusion. The report should include quantitative data on usage, qualitative data from the student survey, a financial summary of actual costs versus budgeted costs, and a recommendation on whether to continue, expand, or discontinue the program.

---

**Recommendation**

I recommend that the university approve the pilot program as described above. The academic benefits of extended library access are well-supported, the financial costs are modest and predictable, the safety risks are manageable with proper planning, and the equity implications are significant. The pilot is designed to be low-risk and high-inform: if the data show that very few students use the library overnight, the program can be discontinued without further expense. If, on the other hand, the data show strong demand and positive outcomes, the university can make a more informed decision about whether to make the program permanent. The key is to treat this as a genuine experiment, not a foregone conclusion, and to evaluate it with the same rigor that the university applies to its academic programs. I am confident that the pilot will yield valuable insights, regardless of the outcome, and I urge the provost and the budget committee to approve it.

---

*Prepared by the Office of Academic Affairs in consultation with the Director of Library Services and the Department of Public Safety.*
