**The Ten Seconds of Thomas Finch**

The clock of Port Blossom was a civic treasure, though not a beautiful one. It was a large, white-faced instrument mounted in a grey stone tower that overlooked the harbour, built in an era when municipal pride was measured in granite and gears. Its hands were black and simple, its numerals stark. For forty-three years, Elias Thorne had been its keeper.

The town had a folk belief about the clock, a piece of coastal whimsy Elias had never bothered to correct. It was said that whenever a person in Port Blossom made a decision they would later truly regret, the clock lost eleven seconds. Not a minute, not ten, but eleven. Elias, a man of pendulums and precision, knew this was impossible. A clock’s time was a continuous thing, a river. It could gain or lose through mechanical fault, but not in discrete, integer packets. Still, the coincidence was noted. The postmistress would say, “Ah, the clock’s down eleven seconds. Someone’s bought that lemon of a boat from the Kellens.” Or the publican: “Another eleven gone. Young Miller must have signed those papers after all.”

Elias would hear these comments and feel a familiar, weary irritation. The clock’s drift was his responsibility. He kept a logbook, a thick, ledger-bound volume where he recorded the date and time of every adjustment he made. The eleven-second myth was just noise, the town projecting its own regrets onto indifferent machinery.

Until April.

He noticed it first as a series of anomalies. The clock, which he had serviced with religious regularity, was losing time in a pattern that defied his diagnostics. He would calibrate it on a Monday, locking the escapement with a precise click. By Wednesday, his log would show it was behind by eleven seconds. He would correct it. By Friday, another eleven seconds gone. He dismantled the movement, cleaned every pinion and pivot, checked the beat with a stethoscope pressed to the cool brass. The mechanism was perfect.

He began to keep a different kind of log. In the margins of his technical entries, he started noting the town’s rhythms. April 5th: eleven seconds lost. Also on April 5th, a headline in the *Port Blossom Gazette*: “Fischer Contract Decision Expected This Week.” April 7th: another eleven-second loss. The same day, Elias saw Mrs. Gable, who sold her late husband’s fishing boat for a pittance, her face a mask of quiet stone. The stories always came after.

The data was undeniable, but it was the *sequence* that began to unnerve him. The eleven seconds were appearing in his log *before* the decision was made public, sometimes by a day or two. On April 11th, he recorded the loss at 8:15 a.m. He saw the regret on the face of young Thomas Finch only three days later, when the boy sat on the seawall, staring at the gulls, his shoulders slumped with the weight of a university acceptance he’d just declined to take a job at the cannery.

Elias felt a cold draft in the clean, oiled air of the clock tower. He was a man who believed in causality, in the linear flow of time from action to consequence. The clock, it seemed, was operating on a different principle.

He tried to speak to someone about it. Not about the myth, but about the mechanism.

“It’s the humidity, Elias,” said Janet, from the council office, not looking up from her paperwork. “Sea air. It plays tricks.”

“It’s not the humidity, Janet. I’ve compensated for it. This is different.”

She finally looked at him, her gaze sympathetic. “Elias, it’s a clock. It tells time. Sometimes it gets a bit off. That’s your job, isn’t it? To set it right?”

He was a clock keeper, not a philosopher. He couldn’t articulate the thing that was growing in his mind: that the clock was not a recording device, but some kind of pre-echo chamber. That it wasn’t measuring regret, but the formation of it, the invisible, weightless moment a choice solidified from possibility into inevitability.

The ambiguity was the worst part. Was it the decision itself, the instant the mind made its choice, that caused the temporal glitch? Or was it the regret that would eventually flower from it, a future event reaching backward to snag the present? Was the clock a symptom or a cause? Did its loss of time somehow make the regret inevitable?

He became obsessive. He bought a pocket watch, a fine Swiss chronometer, and compared it to the tower clock. They agreed perfectly. The loss was only in the public time. Only in the town’s shared heartbeat. He started watching people. He’d sit on a bench below the tower, his logbook in his lap, and observe. He saw the micro-expressions, the slight tightening of a jaw, the flicker of doubt in an eye just before a person turned from the harbour with a new, settled expression. He saw the moment, the breath, where a path closed behind a person, forever.

But he could never be sure. He never saw the decision happen. He only saw the eleven seconds vanish from his log, and then, hours or days later, the evidence on a human face.

In May, the losses accelerated. Eleven seconds became twenty-two, then thirty-three, in a single week. The town’s talk shifted from whimsy to low-grade anxiety. The daily ferry, which relied on the clock for its schedule, was arriving visibly late. Ships in the harbour began to sound their horns at slightly different times. The rhythm of the town, so reliant on the communal landmark, was fraying.

Elias was asked to attend a council meeting. He stood before the Mayor and six councillors in the stuffy town hall, his logbook open on the table.

“It’s the movement, Elias,” Mayor Dunlop said, a man who believed every problem had a simple, bureaucratic solution. “We’ll authorize funds for a full replacement. Modern quartz mechanism. Atomic sync.”

“Mayor,” Elias said, his voice quieter than he intended. “It’s not the mechanism. I’ve replaced every part. The clock itself is… it’s being affected by something external.”

A brief, uncomfortable silence.

“External?” Councillor Kellen asked, leaning forward.

“I believe,” Elias said, the words feeling foolish even as he spoke them, “that the time losses are correlated with, with decisions being made in the town.”

The silence that followed was of a different quality. It was thick with dismissal.

“Elias,” the Mayor said, his tone gentle now, the tone one uses with the elderly. “You’ve served this town well. Forty-three years. Perhaps it’s time to consider a rest. We’ll handle the clock. We’ll get a new one. A reliable one.”

He wasn’t being fired. He was being retired, kindly and firmly. They thought he was losing his mind, attributing agency to a machine. And standing there, Elias felt the first, faint tremor of doubt in his own certainty. Was he the village crank? Had the precision of his work for so long driven him to see patterns where there were only random failures?

He went back to the tower one last time. He didn’t adjust the clock. He just sat on the worn wooden bench amidst the smell of oil and old dust, listening to the steady, powerful tick-tock that had been the metronome of his life. The sound was the same. The mechanism was perfect.

He took out his old logbook, the one with the forty-three years of meticulous entries. He flipped back to the beginning, to his first year on the job. He’d been a young man then, fresh from the horology school in Switzerland, so proud of this post. The early entries were all technical, but as the years went on, he’d started adding those marginal notes, the town’s gossip he’d once dismissed.

A thought, cold and sharp, occurred to him. He flipped to the very first page. The very first entry.

**March 15th, Year 1. Commissioned. Set to Greenwich Mean. All mechanisms nominal. Beat is perfect.**

He stared at it. The date. The commissioning. He remembered that day. He’d stood on this same bench, a nervous young man in a new suit, the Mayor clapping him on the back. “Time is yours now, Elias,” the Mayor had said. “You’re the keeper.”

A keeper. Not just of the mechanism. Of the time itself. Of the town’s time.

He went back to the page and looked at the marginalia of that first week. There was nothing. Of course there wasn’t. He hadn’t started writing the town’s stories in the margins yet. That habit came later. But the page felt different now. It felt… expectant.

He went back to April, to the week of the accelerations. He’d logged thirty-three seconds lost in seven days. He thought of the new cannery manager who’d been hired that week, a man from away. He thought of the vote on the new fishing permits, the one that had split families. He thought of Thomas Finch, and the decision that wasn’t just his, but his girlfriend’s, his parents’, the whole town’s future written in his slumped shoulders.

Then Elias looked closer at the entry for April 11th, the day of Thomas’s quiet despair on the seawall. The day the clock had lost eleven seconds before the boy had even made his choice. Elias had logged the loss at 8:15 a.m. Thomas Finch had been seen on the seawall at 10:45 a.m.

But Elias’s own log entry had a small, smudged fingerprint in the corner of the page, right next to the time. He hadn’t noticed it before. It was the thumbprint of a young man, the whorls and ridges distinct in the dried ink. He remembered who he had asked to hold the logbook open for him that morning while he made a delicate adjustment to the hour wheel.

It was Thomas Finch. Thomas, who had been working a summer job as Elias’s assistant, eager to learn about the old clock. Thomas, who had lingered by the logbook after Elias was done. Elias had left him there for a few minutes, to tidy the tools, while he went downstairs for tea.

He had left the boy alone with the open book.

Elias stared at the fingerprint. At the time he himself had recorded. He had logged the loss of time. He had written it down. He had, in a sense, *declared* it. And then he had watched the town scramble to find its cause, its regret.

He slowly closed the logbook. The ticking of the great clock filled the silence. It was steady. Unchanging. Perfect.

He looked at his hands, the hands of a keeper. Had he been keeping time, or setting it? Was he measuring the town’s regrets, or had his meticulous, lifelong recording of them been the very thing that called them into existence, shaping the river of time he thought he was merely observing? The loss of eleven seconds wasn’t a symptom of a regretted decision. It was the seed. The space that had to be made for the regret to grow into its perfect, eleven-second shape.

He had spent forty-three years trying to set the clock right. He now understood, with a calm, absolute certainty, that it was always right. It was always exactly where it needed to be.

He stood, left the logbook on the bench, and walked down the stone steps for the last time. He did not look back at the white face. The town’s time was no longer his to keep. It had never been.
