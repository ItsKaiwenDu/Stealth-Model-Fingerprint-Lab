# The Barricade at Low Vane

The fog had not yet lifted from the mudflats when Jesse Ward brought his two-horse dray to a halt at the foot of the High Arch. Across the broad approach of crushed river stone, three heavy timber sawhorses stood lashed together with hemp rope, their wet grain gleaming in the yellow light of an oil lantern. Beside them stood Constable Varner, his blue municipal coat buttoned to the throat against the damp, clutching a cedar nightstick in one mittened hand.

"Turn about, Jesse," Varner said, his voice flat with cold. "No crossing this morning."

Jesse leaned over his reins, spitting dark tobacco juice into the wheel rut. "The flour mill expects these sacks by six, Varner. The ferry won’t take three tons of wheat without two hours' notice and a silver dollar extra."

"The ferry’s your business," the constable replied, eyes fixed straight ahead into the gray murk. "The bridge is shut."

"By whose say?"

"Orders from the Borough Marshall. Delivered at four."

"Did the middle pier shift again?" Jesse asked, peering through the lifting vapor toward the iron lattice that spanned the half-mile of slate-colored water. Three weeks of spring freshets had piled uprooted birch trees against the river pilings, and every drayman in Low Vane had been waiting for the timber cribbing to give way.

Varner did not answer. He merely laid his hand upon the uppermost timber of the sawhorse, an official gesture meant to substitute for knowledge.

Within twenty minutes, four more wagons had backed up along the river road, their iron-shod wheels grinding the river shingle into paste. A teamster hauling kegs of salt pork passed the word back to a boy with a milk cart: *The masonry under Pier Three had cracked clean through beneath the morning freight train.*

The boy left his cart with an older brother and ran down the slippery stone steps to the coal quays.

By six o’clock, the rumor had reached the river gang at the South Basin. Forty men in coarse wool and tallow-rubbed boots stood around an open iron brazier, waiting for the daily hiring whistle from the steam cranes. The milk boy’s report had already shed its tentative phrasing.

"They brought two engineers off the downriver express before dawn," the boy told a knot of lightermen who were splicing hawser. "They found a buckle in the tie-rods. Said the whole center span dropped two inches when the midnight mail rolled across."

"Nonsense," said an older docker named Mallow, who had worked on the bridge caissons fifteen years earlier. "Those spans are set on limestone down to the bed-slate. You couldn't drop that center with an artillery battery. It’ll be the union."

"The union?" asked a younger man, blowing into his raw knuckles.

"The brakemen on the northern spur," Mallow said with quiet conviction, tapping his clay pipe against the side of the brazier. "They were talking about a general stoppage over the wage reduction. If they've wedged the switch-points on the north abutment, the company won't risk sending an engine over till they’ve cleared the line with private guards."

Within ten minutes, the hiring whistle blew, but the men moved sluggishly toward the cranes. If the trains were not running across the High Arch, there would be no empty boxcars to take the anthracite arriving on the coastal schooners; if there were no cars, the day’s wages would be cut in half before noon.

A half-mile inland, behind the glass-fronted offices of the Customs House, Mrs. Corley’s coffee room began to fill with travelers who had been turned away from the omnibus depot. The air inside smelled of burnt chicory, wet broadcloth, and stale pipe smoke.

A commercial traveler in a bowler hat, his brass-bound sample cases stacked between his boots, leaned over the zinc counter. "They aren’t letting the passenger carriages through either," he said to a clerk from the Marine Insurance Office. "My driver tried the lower footway. Said there are three armed county deputies standing by the tollgate with Winchesters."

The clerk paused with his tin cup halfway to his mouth. "Deputies? Not Borough police?"

"County men," the drummer insisted, though he had seen only Varner and a night watchman from fifty yards off. "And a man in a black coat with a yellow ribbon in his lapel. Looked like a health officer."

A murmur moved along the counter. Two weeks earlier, a grain packet from downriver had been held at the quarantine station for three days following the sudden death of a cabin steward. The word *cholera* was not spoken aloud, but it settled immediately into the room like coal soot.

"They’ve cordoned the north bank," a second clerk whispered, setting down his pen. "My sister’s husband lives in Highfield. He said three families took sick on Tuesday after drinking from the town pump."

By seven, Mrs. Corley was quietly taking down her slate board and doubling the price of fresh bread and salt butter. If the north-bank market carts were cut off, the town’s larder would last three days at most.

Back at the bridge approach, the crowd had swelled to more than two hundred people. The sun was now a pale, disc-like blur behind the river fog, casting a flat, cold sheen over the water. A line of thirty draft horses stood head-to-tail along the embankment, their breath rising in pale plumes.

At seven-fifteen, a small black brougham with yellow wheel-spokes forced its way through the press of carters. The door opened, and Deputy Commissioner Thorne stepped down onto the muddy stones. He was a thin man in a beaver hat, carrying a leather dispatch case tucked tightly under his arm.

The crowd pressed forward immediately, voices rising in a discordant wave of demands:

"Is the iron broken, Thorne?"

"Are the freight lines running by noon?"

"Is there quarantine on the Highfield side?"

Thorne raised both hands, his kid gloves gray with the morning damp. He conferred briefly with Constable Varner, who handed him a folded paper sealed with red wax. Thorne broke the seal, read it under the dim light of the carriage lamp, and his expression remained as blank as the river itself.

"The bridge will remain closed to all pedestrian, vehicular, and locomotive traffic until further notice," Thorne announced, his voice carrying thin and sharp across the gravel.

"Why?" shouted Jesse Ward from atop his dray.

"Precautionary assessment," Thorne said smoothly, already turning back toward his carriage door. "A formal declaration will be posted outside the Town Hall before the mid-day bell."

"Is it the fever?" cried a woman with an infant wrapped in an oilskin shawl.

Thorne did not reply. The carriage door clicked shut, the iron tires ground into the wet stone, and the brougham turned slowly back toward the municipal buildings, leaving the crowd to watch the dark, silent span of the High Arch stretch across the river—perfectly still, entirely intact, and completely impenetrable.
