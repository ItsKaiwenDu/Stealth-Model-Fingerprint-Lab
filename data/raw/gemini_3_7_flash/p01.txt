# The Stumble

The tower stood on the spit of shale where the harbor channel met the grey expanse of the Reach. It was built of millstone grit, pitted by fifty winters of Atlantic salt, and its four frosted faces looked toward the four quarters of Mallow: the fish-wharves, the coal-yard, the railway siding, and the open sea.

Silas Vane had tended its works for twenty-six years. He was a man of narrow habits, gray-templed and spare, whose woolen jackets smelled permanently of whale oil and lamp-black. Every morning at seven, he climbed the eighty-four stone steps with a linen rag, a brass oiler, and his pocket chronometer—a silver-cased John Poole with a split-second hand, which kept the sidereal time of the Greenwich observatory with absolute, unpitying fidelity.

The public clock was less faithful, though it obeyed a civic rhythm the town had long since accepted as ordinary. Whenever a man or woman in Mallow made a choice they would subsequently come to regret, the great four-legged gravity escapement hesitated. The scape-wheel hitched on its arbor, the pendulum seemed to drag through water, and precisely eleven seconds fell out of the day.

In the keeper’s lodge at the tower’s base, forty leather-bound volumes recorded these subtractions. Each entry was ruled in red ink: the date, the exact second of the stutter, the weather, and, where known, the townsperson responsible. Most entries were mundane: *March 4: 11s. Miller signed for the rotten oats; July 19: 11s. Young Mrs. Cleary gave her ring to the curate’s brother.* Silas never moralized in the margins. He merely calculated the daily drift and adjusted the small brass rating-nut beneath the pendulum bob to pull the town back into rhythm with the sun.

On the very first leaf of his earliest ledger, dated the twelfth of October, twenty-six years prior, there was a solitary notation in his own youthful hand: *6:14 P.M. 11s lost. Pendulum settling on the suspension knife-edge.* It had been his first evening on the spit, having moved his sea-chest into the damp stone chamber below the bells. He had thought nothing of it then; new iron always required time to seat itself against the masonry.

The disturbance began on a Tuesday in late October. Silas was on the north gallery, replacing a lead counterweight on the minute-arbor of the seaward face. The air was thin and smelled of dead bladderwrack. 

At 9:14 in the morning, the mechanism behind him gave its characteristic wet shudder—*clack-thrum*—and the heavy pendulum lagged. Eleven seconds evaporated.

Silas took out his Poole and noted the time. He leaned over the iron parapet to see who had stumbled.

The square below was all but empty. Down by the slipway, old Captain Gidley sat on an upturned herring barrel, whittling a plug of pipe-clay with a blunt clasp-knife. He was entirely alone. A mongrel slept by the wall of the custom-house. In the alley behind the ship-chandler, two boys were throwing horse-chestnuts at an iron hoop. No one argued; no papers were passed; no hand was offered in bad faith.

Silas watched for twenty minutes, his fingers resting on the cold salt-crust of the railing. Nothing occurred. Gidley merely blew the grey dust from his clay and slipped the knife into his trousers.

It was not until two in the afternoon, while Silas was buying smoked mackerel from the curing-shed, that he heard the shouts by the pier. Captain Gidley had taken his son’s new dory out past the breakwater, misjudged the mid-day swell, and cracked the oak garboard against the outer beacon. The boat had foundered in three fathoms, taking five hundred yards of drift-net with it.

"He knew the rip was running," the son was shouting on the cobbles, his voice hoarse with salt-gall. "He knew it before he ever put his oar in the rowlock, and he went anyway."

Silas stood by the shed wall, the parcel of fish cool under his arm. He drew his watch. The dory had struck the beacon at ten minutes past two. The clock had lost its eleven seconds nearly five hours before the old man had untied the painter.

Over the following three weeks, Silas kept watch with a vigilance that left his eyes ringed with red. The discrepancy was not an isolated error. It repeated itself, always in advance.

On a Thursday, at eleven in the morning, the escapement stumbled. Silas trained his brass telescope into the high parlor of the apothecary across the square. Master Callow was sitting quietly with a cup of steeped chamomile, reading a volume of sermons. At dusk, six hours later, Callow poured three drops of sweet oil of vitriol into his partner’s cough draught, mistaking the phial in the dim light, and ruined the man’s stomach for life.

On Saturday, the clock slipped at dawn—5:02 A.M. The town was grey, buried in coastal fog; not a single chimney smoked from the High Street to the marsh. At three the following afternoon, Martha Beale sold her mother’s rosewood spinet to an itinerant tinker for six shillings, only to weep upon the empty floorboards before the tinker’s cart had reached the toll-gate.

Silas dismantled the train. He spent four days living on dry biscuits and rainwater, inspecting every tooth of the great wheel, the intermediate pinion, the anchor, and the deadbeat pallets. He rubbed the steel journals with rottenstone until they shone like quicksilver. He checked the leaden plumb-lines for wind-sway. There were no burrs on the iron, no grains of grit in the lantern pinions, no binding in the brass bushes. The machinery was immaculate.

The fault did not belong to the iron.

He sat on his three-legged stool by the central drum, the four bevel-gears ticking rhythmically above his head. The clock was no longer recording the town’s small miseries; it was anticipating them. Or perhaps, he thought, his pulse tightening, the sorrow did not begin with the deed at all. Perhaps an unspoken grief possessed its own weight, an invisible atmospheric pressure that drifted into Mallow hours before a hand was raised or an ink-pot dipped, dragging the brass wheels backward by the sheer inevitability of what was to come. 

Or perhaps the sea air had simply eroded the boundary between yesterday and tomorrow, leaving the mechanism to register echoes of things that had not yet taken place. The cause remained dark to him, as opaque as the deep water off the spit.

In the fourth week of November, a northern gale set in, driving foam over the sea-wall until the stones of the tower turned black with brine. Silas sat by his cast-iron stove, surrounded by the forty years of leather-bound ledgers.

He began to tabulate the intervals.

In the recent volumes, the slip preceded the act by four hours, then six, then twelve. In the ledger from five years ago, the slips had arrived twenty minutes before the recorded griefs; ten years ago, only two or three minutes—intervals so slight that he had always attributed them to the natural delay between a man’s silent resolution and his outward action.

The distance had been stretching backward through the years, like a shadow lengthening toward winter.

Silas turned the stiff, mildewed pages back through his own life. His handwriting changed as the years receded: the firm, heavy hand of his middle age giving way to the tight, eager script of his twenties.

He came at last to the first volume, bound in pigskin that had cracked along the hinges.

He opened to the first page: October 12, twenty-six years before.

*6:14 P.M. 11s lost. Pendulum settling on the suspension knife-edge.*

He sat motionless in the drafty room, the roar of the Atlantic surf vibrating through the floorboards.

He remembered that evening with the sudden, sharp clarity of lightning on water. He had arrived on the afternoon coach from the south, carrying his canvas kit-bag and his newly signed warrant from the town commissioners. He had turned down the apprenticeship at the locomotive works in Swindon; he had left the girl whose letters he would later leave unopened on his dresser until the postman ceased bringing them. He had climbed the spiral stairs, lit the oil lamp, and felt the iron shudder through the stone floor beneath his boots. He had pulled out his notebook and written the line about the knife-edge, believing himself fortunate, believing he had chosen a life of quiet utility, safe from the entanglements of the world below.

In all the twenty-six years that followed, he had lived alone in the tower. He had never felt lonely. He had never looked upon his narrow cot, his solitary meals, or his grease-stained ledgers with anything resembling sorrow. He had believed himself entirely content.

Silas looked down at the yellowed page under the lantern’s wick. 

The eleven seconds had not belonged to the iron. They had been spent twenty-six years ago, waiting in the cold brass for this exact night, when he would finally turn the leaf and understand what he had lost.
