The Third Floor North

The floor was made of pale green terrazzo, speckled with chips of dark gray stone that looked like crushed gravel under water. If Leo dragged the rubber sole of his sneaker across the surface, it made no sound at all, but the leather heels of the men’s shoes struck it with sharp, hollow snaps that echoed all the way up to the barrel-vaulted ceiling.

Leo sat on the dark oak bench with his heels hooked over the lower wooden rung. His mother had told him three times to keep his feet down so he would not scuff the varnish, but whenever she turned her head to speak to Mr. Henderson, Leo let his knees drift back up toward his chest. His knees were knobs through the thin denim of his trousers. The air in the corridor smelled of floor wax, damp paper towels, and the menthol pastilles his mother kept loose in the bottom of her handbag.

Forty feet down the hall, standing beside a heavy brass-rimmed trash receptacle, his father was looking at a folded newspaper. He was not reading it; his thumbs were pressed against the outer margins, holding the paper taut against his belt buckle, and his head stayed perfectly level. His father wore his dark blue suit, the one with the slight shine across the elbows, and a stiff white shirt that squeezed a ridge of pink skin up over the collar. A woman with short, iron-colored hair stood next to him. She wore a charcoal skirt and carried an accordion folder bound with a thick black elastic band.

Leo took a strip of silver foil from his pocket. It was the wrapper from the spearmint gum his mother had given him when they got off the elevator. He smoothed it against the curve of his kneecap using the edge of his thumb, trying to press out all the tiny diamond-shaped creases until it shone like a new mirror.

“Mr. Henderson,” his mother said, her voice dropping into the thin register she used when she spoke to doctors or bank tellers. “The forty-five-minute gap between the end of sixth period and the five o’clock train. That hasn't been accounted for.”

Mr. Henderson did not look up from his legal pad. He was drawing a series of neat, parallel brackets in the left margin with a blue felt-tipped pen. “We can stipulate that the school library remains open until five-thirty on instructional days,” he said softly. “It’s a standard provision. We’ve used it in three other matters this month.”

“He doesn’t like the library,” his mother said. Her fingers pulled at a loose loop of thread in the cuff of her cardigan, winding it around her index finger until the tip turned purple. “He gets headaches under those long lights.”

“It’s four blocks from the central platform,” Mr. Henderson said. His voice was entirely flat, like the recorded voice that told you the number you dialed was no longer in service. “The alternative is fifty minutes on the county line bus, which puts the arrival past six on alternating Thursdays.”

Leo looked at the yellow pad resting on Mr. Henderson’s knee. Across the top of the page, written in large block letters, was a calendar grid drawn with a ruler. Some of the squares were shaded with a pink highlighter, and some were left blank. In the blank squares, Mr. Henderson had written a tiny letter *M*, and in the pink ones, a letter *F*.

Leo thought of the blue plastic toolbox in the basement at home. He wondered if this was about the insurance money from when the water pipe had burst behind the downstairs shower in November. For weeks after that, there had been men in paper booties walking through the kitchen with clipboards, pointing at the baseboards and talking about structural integrity and the cost of replacement per linear foot. His father had kept all their estimates in a manila folder on top of the microwave, held down by a salt shaker so the draft from the back door wouldn't blow them away.

“Can I get a drink?” Leo asked.

His mother didn't look at him, but her hand reached out and touched his shoulder, her palm warm through his shirt. “The fountain is right there by the elevator, Leo. Don’t go past the fire doors.”

Leo stood up. The backs of his thighs were stiff from the hard wood. He walked slowly along the green floor, his sneakers silent, keeping his eyes on the brass threshold strip that divided the main corridor from the elevator bay.

As he passed the alcove where his father stood, the woman with the accordion folder spoke. Her voice was louder than Mr. Henderson’s, carrying a crisp, metallic snap that bounced off the frosted glass panels of the office doors.

“The distance between the two addresses is seventeen miles,” she said. “The commute is entirely manageable provided the morning handoff occurs at the junction rather than the residence.”

His father cleared his throat. “He leaves his trumpet at the school on Fridays anyway.”

“Then there is no physical reason the transfer cannot occur prior to eight-thirty,” the woman said. She snapped the elastic band back around her folder with a sharp *thwack*. “We are not conceding the alternate holidays simply because of the driving distance. The statute is very clear on equity.”

Leo stopped at the water fountain. The basin was heavy, chilled porcelain, stained with a thin yellow ring around the drain. He pressed the heavy brass button with both thumbs. The water came out in a cold, low arc that smelled faintly of iron pipes. He leaned down, his mouth barely touching the stream, but he didn't drink. He watched his own reflection in the chrome dome of the spout—his nose wide and curved, his eyes two dark, watery spots.

*The trumpet.* He hadn't played the trumpet since the spring concert in May; it was sitting on the floor of his closet behind his winter boots, its case covered in a light fur of gray dust. If they knew about the trumpet, they must have been inside his room. Or his mother had made a list.

He let go of the button. The water died with a small, sucking gasp down the drain.

When he turned around, the woman in the charcoal skirt had walked over to Mr. Henderson. The two of them stood in the center of the hallway, under the long, buzzing fluorescent tubes, with their yellow pads held between them like shields. They did not look angry. They looked like two clerks checking off items on an inventory sheet at the hardware store.

“The dog remains at the current location,” Mr. Henderson said, tapping his pen against his collarbone. “That is non-negotiable. The child’s routine depends on the animal.”

“The animal is twelve years old,” the woman said. “We are not contesting the animal. We are contesting the three-day weekend in October.”

Leo walked back to the bench, his shoes suddenly feeling very large, as if the leather had stretched while he was at the fountain. He slid onto the bench beside his mother.

Her hand was still resting on the wood where his leg had been. She looked down at him, her eyes searching his face for something—a smudge of dirt, a loose eyelash—and then she reached up and smoothed his hair flat behind his left ear. Her fingers were very cold.

“Did you get enough?” she asked.

“The water tasted like pennies,” he said.

Mr. Henderson stepped back toward them. He tore the top sheet off his pad with a slow, tearing sound that seemed to take a very long time. The paper had a row of tiny, fuzzy white teeth along the top margin where it had come away from the binding. He handed the sheet to his mother.

Leo looked at the yellow page resting on her wool skirt. Across the bottom, below the colored boxes, were three short lines of handwriting. He could read his own name in Mr. Henderson’s sharp, slanted script: *Leo shall be delivered to the south curb of the municipal library no later than 5:15 P.M.*

On the other end of the hall, the father was putting his folded newspaper into his briefcase. He clicked the two brass latches shut—one, then the other—with a sound like two dry twigs breaking in the woods.

A tall man in a brown uniform opened the heavy double doors of Room 304. He held a clipboard against his stomach and did not look at any of them as he spoke into the empty air of the hall.

“Matter of Miller,” the man said.

His mother stood up immediately, her knees cracking with a small pop under her skirt. She gathered her handbag by its strap and looked down at Leo, holding out her left hand with the palm turned upward.

“Leave the gum wrapper on the bench, Leo,” she said.

Leo picked up the silver square he had smoothed flat. He folded it once, then twice, until it was small enough to fit under his fingernail, and tucked it deep into the watch pocket of his jeans before he took her hand.
