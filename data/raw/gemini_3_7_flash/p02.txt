**A Clean Break**

The overhead lights in the station buffet buzzed with the low, monotonous drone of an appliance on the verge of failing. Outside, through the tall arched windows filmed with grease and engine grit, the wet rails of Track 3 caught the amber pulse of a distant signal light. It was twelve minutes past two.

Between them on the ribbed zinc tabletop, resting equidistant from a half-eaten plate of scrambled eggs and a cup of lukewarm tea, lay the envelope. It was heavy, linen-laid paper, the color of wet bone, sealed across the back with a strip of brown water-activated tape. On the front, their father’s draftsman hand had rendered six words in permanent black ink: *DO NOT OPEN. BURN WITHOUT READING.*

Julian pushed his glasses up the bridge of his nose, his thumb catching on the plastic frame. He had not taken off his overcoat, though the steam radiators against the wall hissed furiously.

"The three-fifteen to Crewe is delayed," he said. He didn't look at the departure board hanging above the cash register; he looked at the salt shaker.

"I know," Maeve said. She was peeling the paper label off her bottle of mineral water, stripping it away in precise, vertical ribbons. "They announced it while you were in the washroom."

"Did they say why?"

"A signal fault near the junction."

Julian nodded once, a brief, jerky concession to information he had no use for. He picked up his fork, turned it over in his hand to inspect the tines, and laid it down again on his paper napkin. "You should have ordered something hot. The tea is mostly chicory."

"I’m fine."

"You haven't eaten since noon."

"I’m fine, Julian."

A waitress in a faded smock wiped the counter ten feet away, dragging a damp rag over the laminate in wide, sluggish arcs. Aside from a sleeping postal worker in the corner booth, they were the only people in the room.

Julian shifted his weight. His coat rustled—stiff, wool-blend fabric that still smelled faintly of the house on Orchard Road: mothballs, damp masonry, and the menthol ointment their father had rubbed into his chest twice daily for twenty years.

"The lawyer said there’s nothing in the will that mentions it," Julian said.

"Lawyers only care about deeds and bank accounts."

"I told him we found it behind the bureau in the study. In the drawer with the insurance policies."

Maeve stopped peeling the label. Her thumbnail stayed pressed against the glass. "You told him?"

"I asked about procedure. Hypothetically."

"You shouldn't have said a word."

"He was the executor, Maeve. I had to know if it carried any legal weight."

"It’s an envelope with handwriting on it. It carries whatever weight you let it have." She looked down at the bone-colored paper. The edges were sharp, untouched by moisture or pocket wear; Julian had carried it down from the platform in a cardboard folder. "Why didn't you just put it in the stove before you called me?"

Julian didn't answer immediately. He reached out, his fingertips hovering a millimeter above the tape, then withdrew them and folded his hands in his lap. "It was addressed to both of us. His handwriting. On the back, in pencil—our names."

"He knew you wouldn't do it alone," she said.

"And you would?"

"I didn't spend nine years waiting for permission to throw away garbage."

The words landed without violence, but Julian flinched anyway, his chin dropping an inch toward his chest. He looked at the window, where rain had begun to smear the grime into long, yellowed tears. 

"I couldn't leave the roof," he said quietly. "The slates on the north side were slipping. If the water had gotten into the rafters, the whole ceiling of the dining room would have come down."

Maeve looked at him—at the graying hair behind his ears that had grown too long, at the dark, bruised crescents under his eyes that mirrored her own. "The house sold for the land, Julian. The surveyor didn't even look at the roof."

Julian’s mouth twitched. "Someone had to stay."

"Nobody asked you to."

"Nobody had to ask."

The silence returned, filling the booth like cold water. The waitress clicked off the glass display case that held three curling ham rolls, plunging the counter into half-shadow. The station clock ticked forward with a sharp, mechanical clack.

Maeve picked up the butter knife. It was dull stainless steel, rounded at the tip. She balanced it across her index finger, watching the way the ceiling light caught the scratched metal. 

"He wrote it the month after mother’s sister stopped visiting," she said.

Julian’s eyes flicked up, sharp and defensive. "You don't know that."

"Look at the pen. He only used that blue-black ink when he was doing the parish audit in the winter of eighty-eight. After that, he switched to the disposable ones from the stationer."

Julian stared at the writing. "It doesn't matter when he wrote it."

"It matters if it's about her."

"She died in hospital in Leeds," Julian said quickly, his voice flat, rehearsed. "There was nothing else to say about it. You have the certificate. I gave you the copy."

"I have the certificate," Maeve agreed. She did not raise her voice. "And you have the trunk in the attic you wouldn't let the clearance men touch."

Julian’s hands tightened in his lap. "Those were his private effects."

"They were her clothes, Julian. Twelve boxes of them, taped shut and labeled with the year she left for the sanatorium. She was only there three weeks before—"

"I don't want to talk about Leeds," Julian snapped. The postal worker in the corner shifted, grunting in his sleep, and Julian lowered his voice until it was barely a whisper over the radiator’s hiss. "We are here about this. Not the house. Not twenty years ago. Just this."

"All right," Maeve said. She laid the knife flat beside the envelope. "Let’s open it."

Julian stared at the blade. "He told us not to."

"He's dead, Julian. He's three feet under wet sod in St. Jude’s, and the stone isn't even carved yet."

"It was his last wish."

"It wasn't a wish. It was an order. He never wished for anything in his life; he just gave instructions and waited to see who would break first." Maeve leaned forward, her voice dropping into the quiet cadence of absolute certainty. "If he wanted it destroyed, he would have dropped it in the kitchen grate himself while he still had the use of his arms. He left it where you’d find it because he knew you’d keep it. He knew you’d bring it to me."

Julian shook his head, but his eyes remained fixed on the white square between them. "And what if it's something we can't undo?"

"Like what?"

Julian didn't answer. He didn't say the name of the doctor in Leeds. He didn't mention the missing pages from the household ledger, or the fact that their father had never once visited the cemetery after the winter of eighty-eight. He didn't speak of the long evenings in the kitchen, sitting across from a man who ate his porridge in absolute silence while the clock on the mantelpiece beat out the seconds of an empty house.

"If we open it," Julian said, his voice thick, "he wins. He makes us look."

"And if we burn it?" Maeve asked. "You'll spend the rest of your life wondering if the one thing you needed to hear was in there."

"There is nothing in there that I need," Julian said.

Maeve reached out. Her fingers touched the rough paper of the envelope. She felt its thickness—two sheets, maybe three, folded twice. No photographs. No keys. Just paper.

Outside, the station tannoy crackled to life, a burst of static that made both of them startle. 

*“The three-fifteen service to Crewe, calling at Hartford, Acton Bridge, and Crewe, is now boarding at Track 3. Passengers for the north should cross via the footbridge.”*

Neither of them moved. The waitress pulled on an olive-green anorak and took a ring of heavy brass keys from behind the till.

"We're closing up," she said, not unkindly, looking past them at the rain. "Station master locks the waiting room at half past."

Maeve looked at her brother. Julian’s face was completely still, hollowed out by the yellow fluorescent tubes above them. 

"Julian," she said.

He reached into his overcoat pocket and produced a small, silver box of matches—the cheap kind sold behind the counter at newsagents. He set it on the table beside the salt shaker.

Maeve took the matches. She did not pick up the letter. Instead, she slid the box across the zinc surface until it touched his knuckle.

"Your train is on Track 3," she said.

Julian looked down at the matches, then at the envelope, and finally at her. For the first time that night, his gaze didn't slide away.

"You're taking the four-ten south?" he asked.

"Platform 1," she replied.

Julian stood up. His knees made a dull cracking sound in the damp air. He picked up his canvas weekend bag from the floor, slung the strap over his shoulder, and turned toward the glass doors that led out onto the dark, rain-swept platform.

The envelope remained on the table, resting between the cold teapot and the salt shaker, under the buzzing white light. Neither of them looked back as the door swung shut behind him.
