**The Green Chairs**

The floor was the color of pea soup that had been left out too long, a speckled linoleum that caught the light in greasy swirls. My sneakers made a sticky sound every time I shifted on the green plastic chair. The chairs were in a row, all identical, all cold against the backs of my thighs even through my jeans. I had counted them twice already. There were fourteen.

My mother sat two seats away, her back straight, her hands folded in her lap. She wore her blue dress, the one with the little white flowers, the one she usually saved for Sunday. She wasn't talking to me. She was talking to the man with the gray mustache who smelled like coffee and old smoke. His suit was the color of a rain cloud, and he had a folder fatter than my school backpack. He was nodding at everything my mother said, but his eyes were on me. That made me feel like a puzzle piece that had landed in the wrong box.

Across the corridor, on another row of green chairs, sat my father. He had a beard now, which I hadn't seen in pictures. He was wearing a jacket I didn't recognize, but his shoes were the same scuffed brown ones he always wore. He was talking to a woman with sharp shoulders and hair the color of polished pennies. She didn't smile. She wrote things on a little pad with a pen that clicked every time she pressed it. Click. Click. Click. My father looked at me too. He tried to smile, but it was a tight, small thing, like he was holding his face together with pins.

I had a toy car in my pocket, a red one with a chipped wheel. I rolled it back and forth along the seam of the green chair, making a soft *rrrrr* sound. The wheel caught on a rough piece of plastic. I pushed harder. It didn't matter. The adults were talking in low, serious voices. They weren't laughing. My mother wasn't doing the thing she does where she tilts her head back and lets out a sound like wind chimes. My father wasn't telling his dumb jokes about the chicken and the lightbulb. Everything was quiet, except for the click of the penny-haired woman's pen and the hum of the vending machine near the water fountain.

I tried to listen. Not because I was nosy, but because I wanted to know if we were going to get ice cream after this, or if my dad would take me to the park with the swings that went so high I could touch the leaves. I caught a word. "Schedule." Then another. "Weekends." And then, from the man with the mustache, "Primary placement."

I didn't know what those words meant. They sounded like rocks in a jar, hard and rattly. I thought about the word "primary." That's a color, like red and blue. But my mom's face was white when she said it back. She said, "I want primary," and her voice shook just a little, like a leaf on a thin branch.

I stopped rolling the car. I looked at my mother. She was staring at the floor, at a coffee stain shaped like a rabbit. The man with the mustache put his hand on her arm. The woman with the pennies wrote something and underlined it.

"Where will she go to school?" the woman asked. Her voice was flat, like a ruler.

My mother's chin trembled. "She's been at Maplewood since kindergarten."

My father, across the way, leaned forward. His jacket was too tight around the shoulders. "Maplewood is fine," he said, but his voice was louder than the others. He was talking to the penny lady, but he was looking at me. "But she could go to the one on Fifth Street. It has a better art program. She likes to draw."

I do like to draw. I keep my crayons in a shoebox under my bed. But I didn't know why that mattered right now. I didn't know why he was talking about my art program like it was a secret code. The penny lady tapped her pen on the pad. "The court will decide based on best interest," she said. The word "court" hung in the air like a moth. I knew that word. It was from TV shows where people wore black robes and hit little wooden hammers. But there was no hammer here. Just green chairs and a humming vending machine.

My mother started whispering again, her words disappearing into the mustache man's ear. He wrote things down too. Everyone was writing things down about me. But I was sitting right here. I could have told them. I like the park near my mom's house because the slide is fast. I like my dad's spaghetti because he makes the meatballs tiny. I wanted to say it, but my throat felt like it was full of peanut butter.

The clock on the wall was round and white, and its hands moved in tiny, reluctant jerks. I watched it for a long time. The second hand got stuck at the top sometimes, like it was trying to decide if it really wanted to go another round. I felt the same way.

The penny lady stood up. Her heels made a sharp *click-clack* on the pea-soup floor. She walked over to the mustache man. They shook hands. It wasn't a friendly handshake. It was a handshake that meant they were done fighting for a little while. My father stood up too. He shoved his hands in his pockets and looked at the floor. My mother took a deep breath and let it out slow.

A door at the end of the corridor opened. A tall woman with gray hair and glasses stood there. She didn't have a robe. She had a plain gray suit and a clipboard. She looked at me, then at my parents. "We’re ready now," she said. Her voice was soft, not like the penny lady's.

The mustache man touched my mother's shoulder. She stood up. My father walked across the empty space between the chairs. He didn't step on the coffee rabbit. He stopped in front of me.

"Hey, kiddo," he said. His voice was gentle, but his eyes were tired. He knelt down so his face was level with mine. "We're just going to talk to some nice people for a bit. You'll be in the room with us. It'll be okay."

I looked past him at my mother. She was standing by the door, her purse clutched to her chest like a shield. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the floor again.

The toy car was still in my hand. The wheel was chipped. I put it back in my pocket.

"Are we getting lunch after?" I asked. My voice sounded small, even to me.

My father's smile flickered. "Maybe, kiddo. Soon."

The tall woman with the clipboard gestured for us to come in. The room inside was smaller than the hallway. It had a big wooden table and more green chairs. My mother sat on one side. My father sat on the other. I was given a chair at the end, between the gray-haired woman and the mustache man. The penny lady sat across from them.

There were stacks of paper on the table. I could see my name printed at the top of one. My full name. The tall woman picked up a pen. She looked at my mother, then at my father. "We're here to discuss the long-term arrangement," she said.

They started talking again, using those rock-words. "Parenting time." "Shared responsibility." "Stable environment." I watched their mouths move. I watched my mother's fingers twitch. I watched my father's foot tap under the table. They were all looking at papers, at each other, at the tall woman. Nobody was looking at me.

I realized then, with a cold feeling that had nothing to do with the green chair, that I was not a person in this room. I was a thing. A topic. A list of words on a paper. They were talking about my future, but it was like the future was a map they were drawing on the table with invisible ink, and I wasn't allowed to see it. I was just the destination.

The red car was a hard lump in my pocket. I pulled it out and set it on the table. The tall woman stopped talking. My mother looked at the car. My father looked at the car.

"Her favorite color is red," my father said, very quietly.

The tall woman nodded. She picked up the car and handed it back to me. Her fingers were warm.

I put the car on my knee and rolled it back and forth. The chipped wheel made a soft *thump* against my leg. The adults went back to talking. The words rolled over me like water over stones. "Holidays." "Custody." "Best interest."

I didn't understand most of it. But I understood the weight of it. I understood that the way my mother's voice got tight and the way my father's foot kept tapping weren't about a trip or a visit or a dinner. They were about waking up. They were about which house had my toothbrush. They were about who would take me to get new shoes when these got too small.

The clock on the wall in this room was different. It was smaller, but it ticked the same way. I watched the second hand go around and around. Each tick was a tiny decision. Each tock was a piece of something being locked into place.

I didn't cry. I didn't say anything. I just sat in my green chair, holding my red car, and waited for the adults to finish drawing the map that I would have to walk on. The coffee rabbit was out in the hallway, waiting for someone to step on it. I wished I were out there too, counting the chairs again. Fourteen. Fourteen hard, cold, green chairs. But now I knew they weren't just chairs. They were markers. Each one meant a different possibility, and I was sitting on the end of all of them.
