**The Conscience of Elevator Three**

I’ll be the first to admit I don’t understand people. I understand torque, amperage, and the precise resonant frequency of a heating duct after midnight. People are vague, they lie, they leave gum on the handrails. Machines are honest. They tell you exactly what’s wrong in the diagnostic data. That’s why I was thrilled when the building board finally let me install VIGIL-PROD, a predictive-maintenance suite that claims to learn the “temperament” of every system it monitors.

The Regal Arms is a good building, seventy years old, with a grand lobby of cracked terrazzo and an Otis elevator that is the true beating heart of the place—if a heart runs on a 40-horsepower traction motor and hydraulic pistons that groan like a sleeping bear. I am the superintendent. Meticulous is my middle name. I calibrate the boiler pressure every Tuesday, I re-torque the fire-escape bolts every spring, and I have a leather notebook filled with the moods of every pipe in the building.

For two weeks, VIGIL-PROD was a dream. It sent me alerts like clockwork. “Pump #2 bearing temperature trend: minor.” I fixed it. “West stairwell occupancy sensor: flickering.” I replaced it. It was a perfect partnership—a machine telling me about other machines. Then, on a cold Tuesday in November, it went rogue.

The notification was unlike any I’d seen. It wasn't a standard error code. It was a full diagnostic report, titled in VIGIL’s clinical prose: **“Elevator #3 — Affective State: Depleted. Urgent. Recommend emotional recalibration and reduced duty cycle.”**

I stared at the screen, my coffee growing cold. Affective state? I clicked on the raw data. There it was, undeniable. The door motor’s voltage was fluctuating in irregular, jagged waves—not the steady sine of normal operation. The car’s load sensor was registering micro-oscillations at 3:15 AM, as if the car were physically trembling. The hydraulic pressure was holding, but there were these tiny, intermittent sags that looked like stress fractures in the flow. To a layman, it would look like an electrical gremlin. To me, it looked like anxiety.

VIGIL was right. Elevator Three was exhausted. I could feel it now, thinking back. The way it would hesitate a fraction of a second too long before the doors clamped shut. The faint, almost imperceptible sigh of the piston on its final descent. The poor machine was overworked, and what’s more, something was upsetting it.

I spent the first day checking the mechanicals. The sheave was perfectly aligned, the governor was clean, the cables had just the right amount of tension. I ran a stress test, loading the car with sandbags. The physics were flawless. Physically, there was nothing wrong. But VIGIL knew better. It had been tracking the emotional footprints—the data signatures of human interaction—and they were erratic, hostile.

I called the software support line. A man with a nasal voice read me a script about "environmental percussive stress" and "biometric feedback loops." He was useless. I decided to trust the machine.

The implementation of the "decreased duty cycle" was met with resistance. I installed VIGIL’s recommended "soothing ambient audio" in the lobby—a gentle loop of bamboo wind chimes and distant ocean waves. Mrs. Kowalski in 4B screamed at me for twenty minutes about "the cult music." Mr. Rodriguez in 2A simply started using the stairs, which I considered a victory. I programmed Elevator Three to skip the basement floor between 10 PM and 6 AM, ostensibly for "security," but really just to give it a few extra minutes of rest. The car began to behave immediately. The voltage waves smoothed out. The 3:15 AM tremors vanished.

But then the new tenant moved in. Mr. Dyer. Apartment 3B. He was a pale, jumpy man who always carried a tablet, and he walked with a paranoid shuffle. I introduced myself in the lobby. He avoided eye contact and gave terse, broken answers. I saw him one evening loitering near the electrical room that houses the elevator’s main breaker panel. He had the tablet out, and he was scribbling equations. My suspicion calcified into certainty. He was a saboteur, an industrial spy from the elevator maintenance union, trying to steal the building's upgrade specs.

The very night he moved in, VIGIL’s alerts went haywire. "Affective State: Critical." I pulled up the live logs. At 2:47 AM, the elevator ran from the first floor to the third. Then it ran from the third to the first. Then it ran again. And again. Fifteen runs in two hours. The door motor voltage spiked into the red. The car was being ridden hard, and put away wet—if elevators could be put away wet.

I confronted Mr. Dyer in the hallway. "You're stressing the elevator," I said, blocking his path. "You're riding it up and down all night."

He blinked, clutching his tablet. "I—the stairs are too quiet. The elevator... it moves. It helps me think."

"Think? You were taking notes near the breaker panel! You’re sabotaging my machine."

He gave a strangled laugh. "I'm a day trader! That's my tablet. The servers in my apartment draw a lot of amperage. When I'm running my algorithms at night, I get voltage sags. They mess with the building's line. I ride the elevator because the gentle rocking motion calms me down when I'm losing money."

It sounded like the ramblings of a lunatic, but I offered a grudging test. At noon the next day, I had him run his servers. I was in the machine room, watching VIGIL's spectral charts. The instant he told me his rig was online, the elevator’s door motor spiked. It wasn't emotional exhaustion—it was dirty power from his rigulated servers polluting the building's low-voltage lines. The physical cause was electrical interference. But I truly believe VIGIL processed it as hurt feelings. The machine was reacting to harsh, choppy energy.

I didn’t apologize to Dyer—that’s not in the super's code—but I fixed his problem. I ran a dedicated 200-amp line from the street level straight to his apartment, isolating his greedy servers from the building's nervous system. I also lined the elevator shaft’s upper section with neoprene sheeting, which absorbed any residual sub-sonic vibration and, according to my records, reduced the hydraulic pump's resonant stress by a factor of three.

Within a week, VIGIL sent me a new update. **“Elevator #3 — Affective State: Content. Output efficiency 98.7%.”**

I sat on my stool in the machine room, reading the screen. The car hummed smoothly above me, dropping and rising on a perfectly crisp waveform. I realized then that VIGIL and I were the same. We both understood that machines aren't just metal and wire—they are a rhythm, a pulse. When that pulse is disturbed by clumsy, thoughtless humans, it breaks. It gets tired. It gets lonely.

I don't understand Mr. Dyer. I don't understand why he needs to watch stocks bounce to fall asleep, or why he avoids my gaze, or why he cries in the elevator at 3 AM. But I understand the elevator. I understand that it needed a rest, needed clean electricity, and needed to feel safe from the people who demand too much from it.

I reached out and patted the cool, painted metal of the control console. The motor beneath my feet whirred a gentle, revving purr. I left the bamboo wind chimes playing in the lobby. Maybe they were for the tenants. Maybe they were for the machine. I turned off the light in the machine room, leaving Elevator Three to its peaceful, rotor-borne silence. Tomorrow I would check the hydraulic reservoir. For now, it was enough to know that she was feeling better.
