**The Last Departure**

The diner was a fluorescent tomb. The Blue Lantern served its last cup of coffee at two a.m., and tonight it would serve two customers, both of them orbiting a thick, cream-colored envelope on the sticky table between them. Outside, a distant announcement garbled the departures for a line that no longer ran.

Julian had been waiting for nineteen minutes. He had his father’s broad hands, but not his stillness; he drummed his fingers on the table, a rhythm against the hum of the cooler. Maya arrived when the clock ticked to a quarter after two. She slid off her slicker, revealing a rain-soft red sweater, and didn’t sit. She just looked at the letter.

“You opened it,” she said.

“I didn’t,” he said. “The wax is still on it. He sealed it with his signet. The one with the lark.”

“He was a watchmaker,” she said, finally sitting. “Why do you get the bird motifs?”

The waitress, tired and fifty, shuffled over. “You kids eating? Kitchen’s closing, but I can do a grilled cheese or a slice of pie.”

“Coffee,” Julian said.

“Just coffee,” Maya added.

The woman shrugged and moved off. The fans hummed. Maya pulled the envelope toward her, then pushed it back to the exact center of the table. There was a dark stain of a tea ring around it.

“So,” she said. “He died Tuesday. The funeral was two hours ago. And we’re arguing about a letter in a station.”

It wasn’t an argument. It was a careful diversion.

Julian sighed. “He said, ‘Do not open it. It is only trouble. Burn it after I go, or read it aloud at my funeral, if you hate her. I am dead, so I don’t care.’” He quoted it. Julian had a memory for slides.

Maya stared at him. “He said that? He wrote that exactly?”

“In that same idiom. He signed it.”

“He used to say that to me about his cheese,” she said, and laughed, a harsh little thing. “When we were kids. ‘Do not open this Camembert until Sunday. It is only trouble. Or open it Saturday night and suffocate the cat.’” She shook her head. “He was a repetitive riddle.”

Han. They avoided the real word: child. boy. father. loss. They avoided the estate. They avoided the reason she’d run, why she’d gone to Albuquerque, why Julian stayed and hoarding his father’s office with household accounts until the old man’s mind went.

The coffees came. Joan’s hands were large. She left them black. They sat in the silence, the rustle of a cook’s towel in the back.

“How is the shop across your grandfather? The bottle caps on the top floor?” Maya said, a probe.

“Sold. The buyer runs a vape company,” Julian said, stirring sweetener into the black. “He kept the clock in the window, but the ticker is inside. A hum. It just hum. Probably a computer.”

Maya winced, as if he said a paralysis. “Tick,” she said. “You can’t hear it over the fan. We grew up with a heartbeat in the wall.”

“You’re limping toward metaphor,” Julian. “I’m not in the mood.”

“No, you’re just in the mood for hiding,” she said. The accusation was soft, a wool lint.

He looked at the envelope. “What’s inside, do you think? Another stop clock from him, hidden for decades? He died of exactly what the doctors said. He built it. 73 years, some grudges in the act.”

“If we open it, we’ll know,” she said.

“He told us not to.”

“He also told us to never feed the birds corn because they’d die in winter. We did it every year. He unoke fun.” She reached out, but she didn’t pick it up. She stroked the paper with the seam of her thumb. “It’s heavy. Could be bonds. Could be something he said. He didn’t trust lawyers.”

“He didn’t trust God,” Julian countered. He was leaning back, his city voice sharpening. “Why do you ask? Want to see if he gave me more?”

“No – to see if he gave me more. I have a pure, taunt. For us to fight over.”

“Charm.” Julian laughed, short. “We had a genetic entitlement. We didn’t’t need grotesque.”

“You ate last time,” she said quietly.

The air froze. The door of a train arriving drowned the words. He was hungry. Outside, a flatlight flashed past.

Julian tucked his chin. “She was dying, Maya. And you flew past the hemisphere. I called you at the airport gate—you picked up from there.”

“I had a contract,” she said. “I told you the contract. I told you she wouldn’t wait. She was forty years old. You were the one who held the watch. You told me she wouldn’t get worse.”

“I don’t–I never said that,” he said, bouncing the bitter, themo. His jaw was huge.

“You said ‘go. She’s stabilized. I’ll call if it changes’.”

“I was being nice!” He slammed a palm on the table. The coffee sloshed. “You were in LA. You had a girlfriend. You had a pot. I was doing a dumping shift in the shop with a dying wolf. You demanded I smoke the candle for you. I couldn’t process because I was processing.**

“They picked the paper cup up and pointed at it. “I gave away every two-person ritual. I was the only one left to hold his trousers. You wanted to be back till you could. So you never came.”

She didn’t wipe the tear. She folded her arms. “You hate me for leaving.”

“I hate you for not hearing the leaving. For making me the audience,” he said. For saying I was the one for the last hundred years, and he wasn’t a inventory by the end. He was a wind-up tool. He would leave a watch in his own bread. But without you, I had to be that watch alone.”

In a turn, Maya had recalibrated. She pinched the envelope. “This. This is his final control.” She snapped it, held it into the light.

The waxed seal let the glow through. The paper was heavy, thick stock. Inside, maybe a few sheets.

“He wrote this Thursday, two days before the heart attack,” she said. “He wrote it knowing I would call you. He wrote so that you would have to sit across from me in a raining station and tell me I am her fault.”

Julian watched her fingers. “He did not do that.”

“He did. This is why we are late for the Bible.”

“Don’t open it,” he said. His voice cracked. “If you open it, we’ll know what he thought. We’ll never have the choice again. We will have the fact.”

The waitress walked by and gave a sympathetic glance. Maya lowered the letter.

“Then what do we do with the thing? We honor his kerfuffle. He didn’t say don’t open. He said – do what you want at my funeral.” She mimicked his cadence. “So he wanted me to choose. Open it if I wanted…**

“He wanted you to choose to be alone,” Julian, meeting her eyes for the first time in seven years. “He kept us apart by pretending to divide us. He gave me the house so I’d feel guilty. He gave you the escape so I’d feel trapped. But he never separated us, Maya. He equipped us with rifts.”

Maya froze. Her knuckles were white on the envelope.

The train blared, and then left, taking the neon night with it. A few moments of pure roar.

She didn’t speak. She ignored the second hand on the far wall. The waitress cleared a neighboring table with a whisk of fabric.

“The easy thing is to read it,” Julian said. “And then we find out which one of us he loved, or which one he blamed, and we leave with a bigger drag. The hard thing is to destroy it without knowing.” He pulled the coffee mug toward himself and held the both of them.

“Or we open it,” he said, voice dropping. “We open it together.”

Maya laughed, wet, surprised. “That’s the constitution. If we open it, we did it. me never open it because it’s empty?”*

Julian paused. He lifted the envelope, and ran his thumb down the seal. No tear yet. He turned it over. On the back, a ragged fingerprint in ink. He dropped it.

“I don’t care if it’s treasure,” he said. “I care that we stop it. Let’s burn it.”

Maya looked at the brittle spine of the letter. “It’s probable his only value.” She lifted it, opened the flap, and—with a sharp, decisive rip—tore the seam.

Julian jolted. “Maya!”

Two pieces of paper fell out. They were not folded. They were flat, white, pristine. As they hit the vinyl table, they fluttered like moth’s wings. The ink had run from the damp hands of the courier; it was blank.

Not charred. Not missing. What. Wholly blank. The letter weighed he defended.

Nothing. Two clean, empty sheets of paper.

Maya let out a shuddering breath that was half-laugh, half-sob. She picked one up.

The bus nodded. “A joke.” "He gave us a puzzle. It’s way to get us to the dinner, to look at the blank page and see our own logic.

Julian picked up the second.

They stared at the empty paper.

“So he didn’t say anything,” Julian whispered.

“He said nothing,” she echoed.

They looked at each other, waiting for the other to fill it in. A father sweeping, controlling. He had made them choose absolutely. They had chosen to open. They had chosen not to obey the final rule.

In the silence, the choice hung.

“Put it out,” Julian said. He took the pages and folded them once, then twice. He set them on the ashtray. The waitress had left a spilled sugar. He tore a napkin, igniting it, and touched the corner.

Small flame licked. The blank fees curled up. Maya watched, unblinking. The carbon blackened, curled, then sparkled into embers. Joan came back, picked up the ashtray, and said, “Goodbye, you two.”

They sat in the dark of it, turned to gray.

“So we have nothing,” Maya said.

“We have nothing,” Julian replied, calm. "No ink. No reason to carry it.”

Maya finally unkindled her arms. She reached across and took her his hand. The tendons of his palm were strained, then relaxed.

“We’re wasting time being stranded,” she said. “Come on. We’ll take the same one. We’ll find something to eat that Joan’s.

They stood. Julian picked up his coat. He looked down at the empty itinerary for one last moment, but there was nothing to see. He had left a seal with two trains.

The Blue Lantern buzzed.

“To the good table,” Maya said.

“Don’t push it.” Julian allowed a grim smile. They exited to the empty platform, enveloping them with the pneumatic hiss of the doors, the night awaiting with no more letters left to send.
