# The Bridge at Morrow's Landing

The fog came off the river in gray rolls, and by five-thirty the iron truss of the Morrow's Landing bridge was a wet skeleton against the sky. The first to notice was Eli Pruitt, a dockhand who came to work before the light. He carried a tin pail of coffee and a biscuit wrapped in oilcloth, and he stopped at the foot of Bridge Street because the rope was new. It ran from a mooring post to a sawhorse, and on the sawhorse was a plank with black paint: BRIDGE CLOSED. No date. No signature. No reason.

Eli stood there a moment, shifting his weight. He had crossed that bridge every working day for eleven years. He set down his pail and walked to the edge, but the rope was taut and the plank looked official. He did not touch it. He went back to the levee and told the other dockworkers, who were rolling barrels of salt pork onto a flatboat. "Bridge is shut," he said. "What for?" asked a man named Ott. Eli shrugged. "Didn't say." Ott spat into the river. "Maybe a boat hit it in the fog." Another man, younger, said he had heard a crack like a rifle shot last night, but he had been asleep and maybe it was a wagon. No one knew.

By six-thirty the news had reached the dry goods store of Mrs. Hattie Voss, who unlocked her door to find the drayman, Silas Boone, already waiting with a crate of tinware. "You'll have to go around by the ferry," she told him. "Bridge is closed." Silas set the crate on the counter. "Ferry's got a cracked boiler, they say." Mrs. Voss frowned. "No, the bridge is closed. I saw the sign." Silas said, "Sign don't say why. I heard it was the county inspectors. They found the iron was rotten." Mrs. Voss did not believe that, because the bridge was only twelve years old, but she said nothing. She weighed out a pound of nails for Silas and watched him leave. Then she told her first customer, a woman buying thread, that the bridge was closed "for repairs, maybe." The woman said she had heard it was closed because a gambler jumped from the span the night before, and the coroner needed time. Mrs. Voss had not heard that. She sold the thread and thought about the gambler.

At the county office, Deputy Sheriff Frank Moberly sat at his rolltop desk with a telegram from the county engineer in Cottersville. The telegram said: "Bridge closed until further notice. Prevent all crossings. Advise river traffic." It gave no reason. Moberly had read it three times. He had been to the bridge at dawn and watched the rope go up. He did not know why. He was not the kind of man to invent reasons, so when people came to him—and they came, all morning—he said only what the telegram said. "Until further notice." A farmer in a mud-spattered wagon demanded to know how he would get his hogs to the stockyard. Moberly pointed to the ferry landing, half a mile north. The farmer said the ferry charged double for livestock. Moberly said he did not set the rates. The farmer said the bridge had been fine yesterday. Moberly said yes. The farmer said somebody ought to answer for it. Moberly agreed, but he did not know who.

The drummer, a man named Arthur Pyle, arrived on the morning train from the east. He carried a leather sample case full of patent medicines and wore a celluloid collar that was already wilting in the damp. He had a schedule: breakfast at the Commercial Hotel, then calls on three druggists, then the afternoon train west. The hotel clerk told him the bridge was closed. Pyle asked if the druggists were on the other side of the river. The clerk said two were. Pyle said he would take the ferry. The clerk said the ferry was running, but slow, and there was a crowd. Pyle said he had time. He ate his eggs and listened to the men at the counter. One said the bridge had been condemned by a special commission from Washington. Another said it was not Washington, it was the railroad company that owned the tracks on the east bank, and they wanted to force the county to sell. A third man, a steamboat pilot with a red beard, said he had heard the bridge tender had been found drunk and had let the draw swing open, and the machinery was jammed. The pilot laughed. "They'll have it fixed by noon," he said. Pyle wrote a note in his memorandum book: "Bridge closed—verify." He did not know which rumor was true, but he knew he would write to his company that the delay was due to a bridge failure, because that sounded better than "unknown."

At the ferry landing, the crowd grew. A woman named Cora Bell sat on a trunk with her daughter, waiting to cross to the county seat, where her sister was ill. She had heard three different stories from three different people: the bridge was being painted; the bridge had been struck by lightning; the bridge was closed because of a strike at the ironworks in St. Louis, and the strikers had threatened to blow it up. Cora Bell did not believe the last one, but she told it to her daughter in a low voice, because it was exciting. The daughter asked if the bridge would fall. Cora Bell said no, but she looked at the gray water and wished she had not repeated it.

The ferry was a side-wheel steamer, the *Martha Jane*, built for passengers and freight. It made its first crossing at seven, carrying a dozen people, two wagons, and a herd of nine sheep. The ferryman, a man named Jessup, had heard the bridge was closed because of a broken counterweight in the draw span. He told a passenger that the county had known about it for months but had not wanted to spend the money. The passenger told a man on the other side, who told a woman waiting for the return trip, that the bridge would be closed for at least a year. By the time the *Martha Jane* came back, someone on board said the bridge had collapsed into the river at dawn, and that the rope was just to keep people from seeing the wreck. The deputy sheriff was not there to contradict it, because he was at the county office, writing a telegram to the engineer asking for instructions.

At nine o'clock, a boy from the telegraph office ran to the bridge with a message for the deputy, but the deputy was not there. The boy left the message with a man who was leaning on the sawhorse. The man could not read, but he held the envelope importantly and told a passerby that it was "the order to open her up." The passerby went into the saloon on Bridge Street and announced that the bridge would reopen by ten. A group of men gathered at the saloon window to watch. The bridge did not reopen. The man with the envelope eventually gave it to a woman who could read, and she read it aloud: "Reinspection delayed. Hold until further notice." No one knew what that meant. The woman folded the telegram and put it in her pocket, because nobody claimed it.

By noon, the fog had burned off and the river glittered in the sun. The bridge stood empty, its iron trusses casting a striped shadow on the water. A few men stood at the rope, looking at the planks, as if the bridge might explain itself. The dockworkers loaded a barge with barrels and crates, and one of them said he had heard that a county commissioner had a brother who owned the ferry. Another said that was a lie. The shopkeepers swept their porches. The drummer, Arthur Pyle, missed his afternoon train and sat in the hotel lobby, writing a letter about the "unforeseen interruption." Cora Bell and her daughter finally crossed on the ferry at half past twelve, and the daughter asked why the bridge was closed. Cora Bell said she did not know. The daughter said maybe nobody knew. Cora Bell said that was probably true.

The bridge remained closed. The telegram sat in a pocket. The rope stayed taut. The river kept moving, and the town went on, waiting for an explanation that had not yet been sent.
