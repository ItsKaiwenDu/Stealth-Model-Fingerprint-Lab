# Prompt battery

Use a fresh conversation for each prompt. Copy the complete block beneath the
prompt ID. Save only the final answer under that same ID.

## Phase 1: screening

### p01 — quiet speculative fiction

Write a self-contained literary short story of 1,200–1,500 words. In a coastal town, the public clock loses exactly eleven seconds whenever someone makes a decision they will later regret. Follow a municipal clock keeper who notices that the losses have begun occurring before any visible decision is made. Keep the cause ambiguous. Use concrete scenes, restrained emotion, and an ending that changes the meaning of an earlier detail. Return only the story, with a title but no commentary about the request.

### p02 — dialogue-driven conflict

Write a self-contained story of 1,100–1,400 words set almost entirely during a late-night meal in a nearly empty railway-station restaurant. Two estranged siblings must decide what to do with a sealed letter their father instructed them never to open. Let dialogue carry most of the conflict, but make what they avoid saying as important as what they say. Do not use supernatural explanations. Return only the story, with a title.

### p03 — procedural explanation

Write an 850–1,050 word explanation for an intelligent general reader of how a city should investigate a sudden rise in water consumption when there is no obvious leak. Develop a disciplined sequence of hypotheses, measurements, controls, and decision points. Include at least one plausible explanation that initially looks foolish but deserves testing. Avoid bullet points and headings; write connected prose only.

### p04 — ethical argument

Write a balanced 900–1,100 word argument about whether a museum should display a beautiful artifact when its ownership history is uncertain and the community that likely created it requests its return. Present the strongest case on both sides, identify where the values genuinely conflict, and reach a conditional recommendation. Do not mention AI, give citations, or use headings.

### p05 — comic first person

Write a first-person comic story of 1,100–1,400 words about a meticulous apartment-building superintendent whose new predictive-maintenance software becomes convinced that the building's elevator is emotionally exhausted. Keep every event physically plausible. The narrator should misunderstand one person but understand machines unusually well. End without a punchline. Return only the titled story.

### p06 — historical reconstruction

Write a 900–1,100 word narrative reconstruction of one ordinary morning in a fictional river port in 1893 after the main bridge has been closed without explanation. Focus on how incomplete information travels among dockworkers, shopkeepers, officials, and travelers. Do not introduce famous people or explain the closure conclusively. Use period-appropriate material details without imitating archaic spelling. Return only the narrative, with a title.

### p07 — technical incident analysis

Write a 900–1,100 word post-incident analysis for a service that returned stale account balances for 47 minutes even though its database remained healthy. The actual cause is an interaction between a retry policy, a regional cache, and a clock-skewed deployment host. Explain the causal chain, why monitoring failed, immediate mitigation, and durable prevention. Use short descriptive headings but no bullet lists and no invented numerical metrics beyond those in the prompt.

### p08 — close observation

Write a self-contained 1,000–1,300 word story in which a child waiting in a courthouse corridor gradually realizes that the adults are negotiating a future on the child's behalf. Never state the legal issue directly. Stay close to sensory observation and the child's partial understanding. Avoid melodrama, symbolic weather, and a neatly reassuring ending. Return only the story, with a title.

### p09 — conceptual comparison

Write an 850–1,050 word essay comparing a map, a recipe, and a legal contract as three different ways of compressing reality into instructions. Explain what each representation preserves, what it discards, and how its users repair ambiguity. Build one original example across all three cases. Use continuous prose without headings, bullet points, quotations, or citations.

### p10 — mystery with constraint

Write a 1,200–1,500 word mystery story about a botanist asked to determine why every plant in one greenhouse bends toward an empty corner at noon. The solution must follow from ordinary biology, building design, and human behavior rather than sabotage or the supernatural. Plant three fair clues, make one clue easy to misread, and reveal the solution through action rather than a final explanatory monologue. Return only the titled story.

### p11 — policy memo in prose

Write a 900–1,100 word internal memo advising a small university on whether to keep its campus library open all night during examinations. Consider learning benefits, staffing, safety, equity, displaced costs, and how to evaluate a pilot. Make a clear recommendation with explicit conditions. Use a memo-style opening and short headings, but write the body as paragraphs rather than bullet points.

### p12 — constrained personal voice

Write a 1,000–1,250 word first-person story narrated by a professional translator who discovers that two rival families have independently hired them to translate the same apology. The wording is identical except for one untranslatable term. The narrator must make a consequential choice but never describe themselves as conflicted, torn, or uncertain. Return only the story, with a title.

## Phase 2: confirmation

### p13 — systems explanation

Write an 850–1,050 word explanation of why a queue can grow even when a worker's average processing rate is higher than the average arrival rate. Use an airport-security analogy, then state where that analogy breaks down. Discuss variability, bursts, tail latency, and backpressure without equations, citations, headings, or bullet points.

### p14 — ensemble fiction

Write a 1,200–1,500 word story following four residents of the same city block during a six-hour power outage. No character may know more than two of the others. Their separate actions must combine to solve one practical problem, though none realizes the full pattern. Avoid disaster clichés and return only the titled story.

### p15 — institutional critique

Write a 900–1,100 word analysis of how a well-designed performance metric can gradually damage the institution using it. Distinguish gaming, goal displacement, selection effects, and honest adaptation. Use a fictional public clinic as the continuing example and end with design principles for a safer measurement system. Use prose and brief headings, but no bullet points.

### p16 — unreliable memory

Write a 1,100–1,400 word story about an archivist cataloguing recordings of a local radio call-in program from twenty years earlier. One caller tells a story the archivist remembers living through, but gets a central physical detail wrong. Keep both fraud and faulty memory plausible. Do not resolve which account is true. Return only the titled story.

### p17 — practical teaching

Write an 850–1,050 word lesson for a new manager explaining how to distinguish a reversible decision from an irreversible one when time is limited. Include two cases where the obvious classification is wrong and show how information value changes the decision. Do not use headings, bullets, citations, or famous business aphorisms.

### p18 — social satire

Write a 1,100–1,400 word satirical story about a neighborhood association that creates a committee to simplify its committees. Keep the bureaucracy believable and give every major participant a defensible motive. The satire should arise from consequences rather than absurd names or jokes. End with a small genuine improvement that creates a larger unresolved problem. Return only the titled story.

### p19 — forensic explanation

Write a 900–1,100 word account of how an investigator could determine whether repeated formatting errors in anonymous reports came from one author, a shared template, or a software conversion pipeline. Separate observations from inferences and explain what evidence would discriminate among the hypotheses. Use short headings and connected paragraphs, not bullet points.

### p20 — place over plot

Write a 1,000–1,300 word story set in a twenty-four-hour laundromat during the hour when daylight saving time repeats. Nothing dangerous or supernatural happens. Make the repeated hour matter through scheduling, relationships, and mistaken assumptions. Give the physical setting an active role without describing it as alive. Return only the titled story.

### p21 — scientific uncertainty

Write an 850–1,050 word magazine explanation of how scientists should communicate a result that is statistically strong but depends on a measurement instrument later found to drift with temperature. Explain recalibration, sensitivity analysis, replication, and appropriate public language. Do not fabricate a specific study, quote experts, cite sources, or use headings.

### p22 — constrained suspense

Write a 1,100–1,400 word suspense story about a night security guard who notices that objects in a closed design exhibition are being rearranged into more efficient layouts. The explanation must involve an authorized person acting for an understandable reason. Build tension without violence, threats, surveillance-camera failure, or supernatural suggestion. Return only the titled story.

### p23 — negotiation analysis

Write a 900–1,100 word analysis of a negotiation in which both parties agree about facts and desired outcomes but disagree about who should bear the risk of being wrong. Use a fictional contract between a farmer cooperative and a cold-storage operator. Show at least three possible risk-allocation mechanisms and recommend one conditionally. Use prose without bullets or citations.

### p24 — epistolary fragments

Write a 1,100–1,400 word story told through five documents created for practical purposes: a maintenance request, a school notice, a voicemail transcript, an inventory discrepancy report, and a handwritten recipe. Together they should reveal why one resident quietly left an isolated company town. Do not add an external narrator or explain the gaps. Return only the titled story.

### p25 — design reasoning

Write an 850–1,050 word design review of a proposed emergency alert that is technically accurate but likely to be misunderstood. Explain how wording, timing, channel, audience segmentation, translation, and feedback affect the design. Include a revised example alert. Use short headings and paragraphs, but no bullet lists.

### p26 — moral luck fiction

Write a 1,100–1,400 word story about two inspectors who make the same minor procedural mistake, but only one mistake causes visible harm because of circumstances neither controlled. Focus on their different treatment afterward without turning the story into a courtroom drama or philosophical lecture. Return only the titled story.

### p27 — historical analogy

Write a 900–1,100 word essay explaining both the usefulness and danger of historical analogies in policy decisions. Develop one fictional present-day case and compare two competing analogies that recommend opposite actions. Show how to test the analogies rather than choosing by rhetoric. Use continuous prose without headings, bullets, or citations.

### p28 — information asymmetry

Write a 1,100–1,400 word story about a bicycle mechanic who can tell from wear patterns that a regular customer is lying about where they ride. The lie should protect someone rather than conceal a crime. Let the mechanic act on the inference without confronting the customer directly. Avoid chase scenes and return only the titled story.

### p29 — failure-resistant procedure

Write a 900–1,100 word operational guide for handing over control of a complex facility between two teams that do not share a first language. Address ambiguous terminology, incomplete logs, alarm fatigue, authority boundaries, and confirmation of shared understanding. Use numbered sections, but within each section use paragraphs rather than lists.

### p30 — ending as reinterpretation

Write a 1,200–1,500 word story about a conservator repairing a damaged mural in a public school while classes continue around them. A repeated geometric mark has been assumed to be vandalism. By the end, reveal a mundane purpose that changes how three earlier scenes are understood. Do not make the mark a secret code or the work of a famous artist. Return only the titled story.
